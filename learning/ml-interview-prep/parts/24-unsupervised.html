Part B · Classical ML · Module 24

Unsupervised learning Finding structure with no answer key: clustering, dimensionality reduction, and anomaly detection. The last of those is how every fraud system starts life, before anybody has labels — which makes this module a direct prerequisite for the walkthrough in Part G.

The three jobs

Unsupervised is not one technique but three quite different goals that happen to share the property of having no labels:

GoalQuestion it answersMain tools
ClusteringWhich of these things belong together? k-means, GMM, DBSCAN, hierarchical
Dimensionality reductionCan I describe this with fewer numbers without losing much?PCA, SVD, autoencoders, UMAP
Anomaly detectionWhich of these does not belong at all? Isolation Forest, LOF, one-class SVM, reconstruction error
The thing that makes all of it hard

There is no held-out accuracy to check. Every one of these methods will return an answer on pure noise, confidently and without complaint. k-means asked for 5 clusters will produce 5 clusters in uniformly random data. The main skill in unsupervised learning is scepticism, and interviewers probe for it — they want to hear that you would validate the structure against something external, not just admire it.

The terms

k-means basic

Pick k centres, assign every point to its nearest, move each centre to the mean of its assigned points, repeat until nothing moves.

Placing k ice-cream vans in a park: each person walks to the nearest van, then each van moves to the middle of its own crowd, and you repeat until the vans stop moving.

k-means++ mid

A smarter initialisation: choose the first centre at random, then choose each subsequent one with probability proportional to its squared distance from the nearest existing centre.

The default in every library, because random initialisation gets stuck in bad local optima often enough to matter. k-means is non-convex, so the starting point genuinely decides the outcome.

Inertia / WCSS basic

The total squared distance from each point to its own centre. What k-means minimises.

It falls monotonically as k grows — which is exactly why you cannot pick k by minimising it, and why the elbow method exists.

Silhouette score mid

For each point: how much closer it is to its own cluster than to the next-nearest one, scaled to [−1, 1]. Averaged over all points.

A more defensible way to choose k than the elbow, because it has an interpretable scale — but it still assumes convex, roughly spherical clusters.

GMM & EM mid

Model the data as a mixture of Gaussians. E-step: given the current Gaussians, compute each point's probability of belonging to each. M-step: given those responsibilities, refit each Gaussian. Repeat.

k-means makes every point pick a team. A GMM lets each point be 70 % on one team and 30 % on another.

k-means is literally the limiting case of a GMM with spherical, equal-variance components and hard assignment.

DBSCAN mid

Cluster by density: a point is a core point if at least minPts neighbours sit within eps. Connected core points form a cluster; everything else is noise.

Finding cities from a map of house locations. You do not decide there are seven cities in advance — you look for places where houses are packed together.

Two properties nothing else gives you at once: it finds the number of clusters itself, and it has an explicit category for "this point is not in any cluster".

Hierarchical clustering mid

Repeatedly merge the two closest clusters (agglomerative), producing a dendrogram you can cut at any level.

You get every k at once. Costs O(n²) memory, so it is for thousands of points, not millions.

PCA basic

Find the directions of greatest variance and re-express the data in those coordinates, keeping the top few.

Photographing a chair. Some angles show you a chair; the angle straight down the seat shows you almost nothing. PCA finds the informative angles.

Module 10's eigenvectors and SVD, applied. The principal components are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, equivalently the right singular vectors of the centred data.

Explained variance ratio basic

What fraction of total variance each component accounts for. The usual way to decide how many to keep — a cumulative 90–95 % is the common convention.

t-SNE / UMAP mid

Non-linear methods that place points in 2D so that neighbourhoods are preserved.

Visualisation tools. Distances between clusters, cluster sizes, and empty space in the plot are all meaningless — see the trap in the questions.

UMAP is faster, preserves more global structure, and unlike t-SNE can transform new points without refitting — which matters if you ever wanted it in a pipeline.

Autoencoder mid

A network trained to reconstruct its own input through a narrow bottleneck. The bottleneck is a learned, non-linear compression.

A linear autoencoder with squared error recovers exactly the PCA subspace. The non-linearity is the whole difference.

Isolation Forest senior

Build trees on random splits. Anomalies get isolated in very few splits, because there is nothing around them to keep splitting.

Twenty questions to identify someone in a crowd. A person standing alone in the corner takes two questions; someone in the middle of the crowd takes fifteen.

The default unsupervised anomaly detector: linear time, handles high dimensions reasonably, few parameters. First thing I would try in a fraud cold start.

LOF — Local Outlier Factor senior

Compares a point's local density to that of its neighbours. Catches points that are anomalous relative to their own neighbourhood, even if not globally unusual.

The one that catches a €300 transaction from a customer who has never spent above €40 — globally unremarkable, locally extreme. That is most fraud.

k-means: the assumptions that break it

k-means is asked about constantly because its failure modes are all consequences of assumptions that are easy to state and easy to violate:

It assumesSo it fails whenUse instead
Clusters are sphericalClusters are elongated or curved — two concentric rings get sliced in halfGMM (elliptical) or DBSCAN (any shape)
Clusters are similar in sizeOne cluster has 10,000 points and another 50 — the large one gets split and the small one absorbedGMM, DBSCAN
Clusters have similar densityDensity varies a lotGMM; note DBSCAN also struggles here, since eps is global
Every point belongs somewhereThere is genuine noise, which gets forced into a cluster and drags its centreDBSCAN, which has a noise label
Euclidean distance is meaningfulFeatures are on different scales, or the data is very high-dimensionalScale first; reduce dimension first; use cosine on embeddings
You know kYou do notSilhouette, gap statistic, or DBSCAN/hierarchical

The compensations: k-means is fast — roughly linear in n — trivially parallel, and scales to enormous data via mini-batch variants. That is why it remains the default despite the list above.

PCA: what it does and what it does not

Centre the data, compute the covariance matrix, take its eigenvectors. The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the direction of greatest variance; the next is the largest-variance direction orthogonal to it; and so on. Keep the top k and you have projected onto the best possible k-dimensional subspace in the least-squares sense — which is the Eckart–Young theorem from module 10.

Uses: compression, decorrelation, noise reduction (small components are frequently noise), visualisation, and speeding up downstream models. In practice you compute it by SVD on the centred matrix rather than by forming the covariance matrix, because forming XᵀX squares the condition number.

The three things PCA does not do
  • It does not preserve discriminative information. Variance is not relevance. The direction separating your two classes may have small variance and get discarded, and the largest-variance direction may be an irrelevant global effect. If you have labels and want a discriminative projection, that is LDA, not PCA.
  • It does not give interpretable features. Each component is a dense mixture of every original feature. "Component 3 increased by 2" means nothing to a stakeholder, and it destroys the explainability that a credit model needs.
  • It does not handle non-linear structure. It is a linear projection. Data on a curved manifold — the classic Swiss roll — is not helped. Kernel PCA, autoencoders or UMAP are the answers there.

And the one that bites in production: you must scale before PCA. Variance is measured in the feature's own units, so a feature in euros dominates one in years automatically. Fit the scaler and the PCA on the training fold only — otherwise it is preprocessing leakage from module 20.

Anomaly detection — the fraud cold start

Every fraud system has a period at the beginning with no labels: nobody has confirmed a chargeback yet, or the product is new. Unsupervised anomaly detection is what runs then, and it is also what keeps running afterwards to catch attack patterns the supervised model has never seen.

MethodCatchesCost / caveat
Statistical rules (z-score, IQR, hand-written thresholds)Gross outliers on one featureTrivial to build and explain; misses everything multivariate. Still the first thing to ship
Isolation ForestGlobally unusual combinations of features Linear time, few parameters. Needs a contamination estimate that you are guessing at
LOFLocally unusual — normal-looking overall, abnormal for this entityThe right shape for fraud, but O(n²) naively and needs a neighbourhood definition
One-class SVMA boundary around "normal"Sensitive to parameters, scales poorly. Rarely the practical choice
Autoencoder reconstruction errorAnything unlike the training distributionHandles high dimensions and mixed data types; needs enough clean data and a training pipeline
Per-entity baselinesDeviation from this customer's own history Often the strongest signal in fraud, and it is feature engineering rather than a model — which is why it belongs in the feature store, module 51
The sentence that shows you have done this

"Anomalous is not the same as fraudulent." A customer's first large legitimate purchase is anomalous. A wealthy customer's routine behaviour is anomalous relative to the population. An unsupervised detector produces a review queue, not decisions — and its real job is to generate the labels that let you train a supervised model later. Framing it as a bootstrapping step rather than as the solution is the answer senior interviewers are listening for.

Questions

BASICHow does k-means work, and how do you choose k?
Assign each point to the nearest of k centres, move each centre to the mean of its points, repeat until stable. Choose k with the silhouette score or the gap statistic — the elbow method is a heuristic that is often ambiguous.

Two properties of the algorithm worth stating: it always converges, because each of the two steps can only reduce the objective; and it converges to a local optimum, so the initialisation matters, which is why k-means++ is the default.

Choosing k:

  • Elbow — plot inertia against k and look for the bend. Note that inertia always decreases with k, so there is no minimum to find; you are looking for a change in slope, and on real data there is often no clear one.
  • Silhouette — bounded in [−1, 1] with an interpretable meaning, so you can pick the k that maximises it. My default.
  • Gap statistic — compares inertia against what you would get on uniform random data of the same shape. Principled, and slower.
  • The business — very often the honest answer. If the clusters become customer segments that a marketing team will act on, then k is about how many campaigns they can run, and a statistically optimal 23 clusters is useless.

And the check I would insist on: run it several times with different seeds and see whether the clustering is stable. If the assignments change substantially between runs, the structure is not really there and no choice of k will fix that.

MIDWhen does k-means fail, and what would you use instead?
It assumes spherical, similarly-sized, similarly-dense clusters with no noise, on scaled features, with k known. Break any of those and it fails in a predictable way.

The two canonical failures I would describe: concentric rings, where the true structure is two rings sharing a centre and k-means cuts them into pie slices, because it can only produce convex regions; and very unequal cluster sizes, where a large cluster gets split in two and a small nearby cluster gets absorbed, because splitting the large one reduces inertia more.

What I would reach for: GMM when the clusters are elongated or overlapping, since it fits full covariances and gives soft assignments; DBSCAN when shapes are arbitrary, when there is genuine noise, or when I do not know k; hierarchical when the dataset is small and I want to inspect structure at several granularities; and on high-dimensional data, reduce the dimension first or move to cosine similarity over embeddings, because Euclidean distance in raw high-dimensional space has stopped discriminating.

The precondition underneath all of it: scale the features first. Unscaled k-means is clustering by whichever feature happens to have the largest units, and that is the most common real-world failure of the lot.

Trap

DBSCAN is not a universal upgrade. It has a single global eps, so it struggles exactly where cluster densities differ — and it degrades in high dimensions for the same distance-concentration reason as everything else. HDBSCAN relaxes the global-density assumption and is worth naming.

MIDExplain PCA — and when is it a bad idea?
PCA re-expresses the data in the directions of greatest variance and keeps the top few. It is a bad idea when you need interpretability, when the important signal has small variance, or when the structure is non-linear.

Mechanically: centre the data, take the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix, order them by eigenvalue, project onto the top k. Each eigenvalue is the variance captured along its direction. Equivalently, and how it is actually computed, take the SVD of the centred matrix — numerically better, because forming XᵀX squares the condition number.

When I would not use it:

  • Interpretability is required. Components are dense mixtures of all features. In credit or fraud, where I have to state why a decision was made, PCA destroys exactly the property I need.
  • Variance is not the signal. PCA is blind to labels. A low-variance direction can be the one that separates the classes, and PCA will discard it first. If I have labels and want a projection, LDA optimises the right thing.
  • The manifold is curved. A linear projection cannot unroll a Swiss roll. Kernel PCA, an autoencoder, or UMAP.
  • The features are already few and meaningful. Then it is pure downside.

And the operational note: PCA must be fitted on the training fold and applied to validation and test, inside the pipeline — fitting it on everything is textbook preprocessing leakage.

Trap

"PCA reduces overfitting." Only incidentally, by reducing dimensionality. It is not a regulariser, it is blind to your target, and it can discard exactly the direction that mattered. Regularisation is the tool for overfitting.

SENIORYour t-SNE plot shows two well-separated blobs. What can you conclude?
Very little. t-SNE preserves local neighbourhoods and actively distorts global structure — distances between clusters, relative cluster sizes and the empty space between them are all artefacts.

What is going on: t-SNE minimises a KL divergence between neighbour distributions in the original and the embedded space. It is optimised to keep near things near. It has no objective at all for keeping far things at a proportionate distance, so:

  • Distance between clusters means nothing. Two blobs on opposite sides of the plot may be adjacent in the original space.
  • Cluster size means nothing. t-SNE expands sparse regions and compresses dense ones, so a large blob is not a large cluster.
  • Perplexity changes everything. The same data at perplexity 5 and 50 can produce entirely different pictures, and neither is more correct.
  • It finds clusters in random data. This is the demonstration worth citing — run t-SNE on pure noise and you get convincing blobs.

What I would actually do: treat the plot as a hypothesis generator, then verify the separation with something quantitative — cluster in the original space and check whether the partition agrees, or check whether the groups differ on features I can name and defend. Run it at several perplexities and several seeds; structure that survives all of them is more likely real. And I would use UMAP instead when global structure matters, since it preserves more of it and can project new points without refitting.

Trap

Never cluster on t-SNE coordinates, and never feed them into a downstream model. It is a non-parametric visualisation with no meaningful transform for unseen points — t-SNE cannot even place a new point without rerunning the optimisation.

SENIORYou need to detect fraud and you have no labels. What do you build?
A layered system: rules for the obvious, per-entity baselines for the real signal, an unsupervised model for the unknown — all feeding a human review queue whose decisions become the labels for a supervised model later.

The framing first, because it is the actual answer: with no labels the goal is not a fraud detector, it is a label-generating machine. Everything I build is judged by how efficiently it fills an analyst review queue with cases worth looking at.

  1. Rules, immediately. Velocity checks, impossible-travel, mismatched BIN/country, card-testing patterns of many tiny authorisations. Cheap, explainable, catch the crude attacks, and they ship on day one.
  2. Per-entity baselines. The strongest signal in fraud is deviation from a customer's own history — amount versus their trailing median, a merchant category they have never used, a device they have never had. This is feature engineering, not modelling, and it belongs in the feature store with point-in-time correctness so it does not leak later.
  3. Unsupervised models on top. Isolation Forest as the general detector, LOF for the locally-anomalous cases that are globally unremarkable. Ensemble them — agreement across methods is a much better ranking than any single score.
  4. A review queue with capacity in mind. If analysts can handle 200 cases a day, my threshold produces 200 cases a day. This turns an unbounded scoring problem into a ranking problem, which is far better posed.
  5. Capture every decision as a label. This is the part people forget. Each reviewed case is a labelled example, and within weeks there is a supervised training set.
  6. Then switch, keeping the unsupervised layer running permanently for novel attack patterns the supervised model has never seen — a supervised model cannot detect a fraud type absent from its training data, and attackers change tactics deliberately.

The bias I would flag out loud: the labels I generate are only for cases the system flagged, so the supervised model trains on a biased sample and inherits my blind spots. Mitigating that means reviewing a small random sample of unflagged traffic too — expensive, and the only way to measure the false-negative rate at all. That is the feedback-loop problem from module 12 arriving in concrete form, and module 54 deals with it in production.

MIDk-means or GMM — what is the actual difference?
k-means makes hard assignments and assumes spherical, equal-variance clusters. A GMM makes soft, probabilistic assignments and fits a full covariance per cluster, so it handles elliptical and overlapping ones. k-means is the limiting case of a GMM.

The precise relationship: take a GMM, force every component to be spherical with the same variance, then let that variance go to zero. The soft responsibilities collapse to hard 0/1 assignments and EM becomes exactly Lloyd's algorithm. So k-means is not a different family of method, it is a constrained special case.

What the extra generality buys: elliptical clusters, because each component has its own covariance; overlapping clusters, because a point can be 60/40 between two; a genuine likelihood, so you can use BIC or AIC to choose the number of components rather than relying on a heuristic; and a density estimate, which makes it usable for anomaly detection — low density under the fitted mixture means unusual.

What it costs: more parameters to estimate, so it needs more data, particularly with full covariances in high dimensions; slower; sensitive to initialisation, usually seeded by running k-means first; and it can degenerate, with a component collapsing onto a single point and sending the likelihood to infinity, which is why implementations add a small ridge to the covariance.

My practical rule: k-means when I want fast segmentation and the clusters look convex; GMM when I need soft membership, elliptical shapes, or a density I can threshold.
