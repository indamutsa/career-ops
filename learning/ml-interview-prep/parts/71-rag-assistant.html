Part G · Walkthrough · Module 71

RAG assistant over internal documents The most-asked LLM design question of the moment, and the one candidates most often answer as "embed the docs, stuff the top 5 into the prompt, done". The interesting parts are chunking, permissions, attribution and evaluation — and every one of them is where real deployments fail.

How to use this module

Same drill as module 70: read once, then draw it from memory in twelve minutes. The parts people drop here are permissions inside the retrieval filter, evaluating retrieval separately from generation, and what happens when the answer is not in the corpus.

The prompt, and the first four minutes

The prompt

"Build an assistant that answers employee questions from our internal documentation."

What to askWhy it changes the design
What kind of questions — lookup, synthesis, or troubleshooting?Lookup needs precise retrieval of one passage. Synthesis needs many passages and a larger context. They are different systems.
How big is the corpus, and how fast does it change?A million documents changing hourly is an indexing problem; ten thousand static PDFs is not.
What formats?Clean Markdown is easy. Scanned PDFs, tables inside slide decks and Confluence exports are most of the actual work.
Are documents access-controlled?The critical question. If yes, retrieval must be permission-filtered per user, and that shapes the index.
What is the cost of a wrong answer?An HR policy question answered wrongly is a legal problem; a "which build command do I run" is an annoyance.
Do answers need citations?Almost always yes internally — and attribution changes the prompt, the response format, and the evaluation.
Latency and volume?Determines whether you can afford reranking, multi-step retrieval and a large generator.
Who maintains the corpus?Stale documents are the number-one source of confidently wrong answers, and no model fixes that.
The assumptions I would design against
  • ~200k documents, mixed Confluence, Google Docs, PDFs and code READMEs; a few thousand change per day.
  • Access-controlled — engineering, HR and finance spaces are not universally readable.
  • Citations required, every claim traceable to a source passage.
  • p95 under 4 seconds to first token, streaming after that.
  • A few thousand queries a day — small enough that quality beats cost, but not so small that cost is free.

Two systems, as always: an indexing pipeline that runs continuously and is a data engineering problem, and a query path that runs in seconds and is an information retrieval problem with a language model bolted to the end. Most failures are in the first one, and most candidates spend all their time on the second.

Parsing and chunking — where quality is actually decided

Retrieval can only return what indexing preserved. Say this early, because it reframes the whole answer away from "which embedding model".

Layout-aware parsing underrated

A PDF is a bag of positioned glyphs, not a document. Naive extraction interleaves columns, destroys tables and drops headers.

Use a layout parser that recovers reading order, headings and table structure; OCR scanned pages. A table flattened into prose is unanswerable no matter how good the retriever is — and internal docs are full of tables.

Structure-aware chunking the main lever

Split on document structure — heading sections first, then paragraphs — with a target size and a small overlap, rather than every N characters.

Fixed-size splitting cuts sentences and separates a claim from the condition that qualifies it, which is exactly how a RAG system produces an answer that is true in general and wrong here. Target roughly 300–600 tokens with ~15 % overlap as a starting point, then tune it against the retrieval metric — this is a hyperparameter, not a constant.

Contextual enrichment senior

Prepend each chunk with its document title and heading path, and optionally a one-line model-written summary of where it sits in the document.

A chunk reading "this is not permitted for contractors" is meaningless alone. Enriched with "Travel Policy › Expenses › Business class", it is retrievable and answerable. Cheap at index time, large effect on both retrieval and faithfulness.

Incremental indexing mid

Consume the source's change feed, re-embed only what changed, and delete chunks whose source document was deleted.

The deletion path is the one people forget, and it is the one that causes an assistant to quote a policy that was retracted six months ago. Tombstone deletions and treat a full reindex as a recovery mechanism, not a routine.

Retrieval — hybrid, filtered, then reranked

  1. Query rewriting. Resolve pronouns and follow-ups against the conversation ("what about contractors?" is unretrievable alone), expand internal acronyms, and split a multi-part question into separate retrievals. Cheap, and it fixes a large share of "the model did not find it" complaints.
  2. Hybrid search. Dense embeddings for paraphrase and concept matching; BM25 for exact tokens — error codes, ticket IDs, function names, product names — which is precisely where embeddings are weakest. Fuse with Reciprocal Rank Fusion, which needs no score calibration between the two systems.
  3. Permission filtering inside the query. Every chunk carries the ACL of its source document. The user's groups become a filter in the vector and keyword query, not a post-filter over the results.
  4. Reranking. A cross-encoder scores the ~100 fused candidates jointly with the query and keeps the best 5–10. This is the single highest-leverage quality upgrade in a RAG system, because bi-encoder retrieval is a coarse filter by construction (module 42).
  5. Assembly. Order the kept passages, tag each with an id, and instruct the model to cite the ids it used. Put the most relevant passages at the beginning and end of the context — "lost in the middle" is real (module 45).
Post-filtering permissions is a data leak, not a bug

If you retrieve the top 10 and then remove what the user may not see, two things happen. The obvious one: they get fewer or zero results. The serious one: the number and shape of what was removed is itself a signal, and if any part of a filtered document reaches the prompt, the model may paraphrase it into the answer. Filter in the query.

The follow-up: what about a document whose existence is confidential? Then the ACL must cover the metadata too, and "no results found" must be indistinguishable from "you cannot see the results". Saying this unprompted marks you as someone who has shipped this.

Generation, citations and refusal

The generation step is the easy part, with three requirements that are not:

  • Ground the answer. Instruct the model to answer only from the passages and to say when they are insufficient. This reduces fabrication substantially but does not eliminate it — the model's parametric knowledge is still there, and a plausible-sounding gap-filler is exactly what it is good at (module 46).
  • Cite at the claim level. Sentence-level citations, not a list of links at the bottom. Then verify them: check post-hoc that each cited passage actually supports the sentence, either with an entailment model or a second cheap LLM call. Uncheckable citations are worse than none, because they manufacture trust.
  • Refuse well. When the top reranker score is below a threshold, or the passages disagree, say so and show what was found rather than synthesising. Most business damage from these systems is not a wrong answer to a hard question — it is a confident answer to a question whose answer was never in the corpus.

Evaluation — three layers, and none is optional

LayerWhat it measuresHow
RetrievalIs the answer even in the retrieved set?Recall@k and MRR against a labelled set of question → correct-passage pairs. Purely mechanical, no LLM needed.
FaithfulnessIs every claim supported by the retrieved passages?Claim extraction plus entailment against the sources — catches the failure the user cannot see.
Answer qualityWould a person accept this?LLM judge against a rubric, calibrated against human labels — report the agreement (Cohen's κ) or the judge is decoration.

Why separating them matters. They have completely different fixes. Low retrieval recall → chunking, hybrid weights, reranking. Good retrieval but low faithfulness → prompt, model, citation verification. Both good but users unhappy → the corpus is stale or contradictory, and no amount of ML fixes that. A single end-to-end score tells you the system is bad and nothing about which of the three to work on.

Building the eval set is the unglamorous prerequisite: 100–300 real questions with verified answers and source passages, drawn from support tickets and search logs rather than invented. Synthetic questions generated from the documents test whether you can retrieve a passage you already have — the easy case, and not the one that fails.

When not to build this

Worth saying out loud, because it is a senior signal. If the corpus is small and stable, a good search box plus a curated FAQ beats RAG at a fraction of the cost. If the questions are mostly "what is the current value of X", the answer belongs in a database query, not a document retriever. And if the documentation is wrong, this project makes the wrongness faster to reach — fixing the corpus is the higher-value work, and you should say so before building.

If you only get five minutes

"Two pipelines. Offline: connectors with a change feed, layout-aware parsing, structure-aware chunking enriched with the heading path, embedded into a hybrid index that carries each document's ACL. Online: rewrite the query against the conversation, retrieve dense and BM25 in parallel with the user's permissions applied inside the query, fuse with RRF, rerank with a cross-encoder to about eight passages, generate with claim-level citations, and refuse when the top score is weak. Evaluate retrieval, faithfulness and answer quality separately, because they have different fixes, against a few hundred real questions rather than synthetic ones. The failure modes that matter are a stale corpus, deletions that never propagate, and confident answers to questions the corpus never covered."
