Calculus & minima Module 10 handed you a price rule that already fits four houses exactly, as if it fell from the sky. Same four houses, one new question — which way do I turn this knob to make the error smaller?

The problem this was invented to solve

Module 10 gave you a price rule out of nowhere: w = [3, 8, −0.4, −12] — 3 k€ per m², 8 k€ per bedroom, −0.4 k€ per year of age, −12 k€ per km from the centre. Plugged into house A's features x = [120, 3, 41, 4.2], it predicts exactly the 317.2 k€ house A sold for. Convenient, and unrealistic: nobody hands you w. You have to search for it, starting from a guess that is almost certainly wrong.

Call a candidate price list w — it might be wrong in every slot. For any house, x·w (the dot product from 10.3) is what that candidate predicts. Write ŷ = x·w for the prediction, and let p be the price the house actually sold for. The gap between them is the error: e = p − ŷ

Squaring the error keeps an overestimate and an underestimate equally bad, and — the property this whole page leans on — turns the result into a smooth curve with no kinks, so it always has a well-defined slope. Call that squared error the loss:

L = e² = (p − x·w)²
WhatL is a machine: put a candidate w in, get one number out — how wrong that guess is.
WhyYou cannot improve a guess you cannot measure. The loss is the single number every training step tries to shrink.
Where in MLEvery model has one: mean-squared error, cross-entropy, hinge loss. All are "how wrong am I", written as a function of the weights.

Try it on house A with a guess that gets two of the four prices right and two wrong: keep the age-price and km-price at module 10's values (−0.4, −12), but guess 2 k€/m² for area (correct answer: 3) and 5 k€/bedroom for bedrooms (correct answer: 8). Call this guess (w₁, w₂) = (2, 5), and leave w₃ = −0.4 and w₄ = −12 fixed for the rest of this page — w₁ and w₂ are the only two unknowns calculus has to find here, and (3, 8) is the answer module 10 already found, sitting there to check against.

ŷ = 120(2) + 3(5) + 41(−0.4) + 4.2(−12)
= 240 + 15 − 16.4 − 50.4 = 188.2 k€
e = p − ŷ = 317.2 − 188.2 = 129 k€
L = e² = 129² = 16 641
A guess that is 129 k€ too low on house A produces a loss of 16 641. That number means nothing on its own — it only matters compared with the loss of a different guess, which is exactly what the next section computes.
what each symbol is xhouse A's features — unchanged since module 10 wthe current guess: (2, 5, −0.4, −12) ŷpredicted price for this guess — "y hat" pthe price house A actually sold for: 317.2 k€ eerror — how far the guess missed, in k€ Lloss — the squared error, the number we will shrink
Interview takeawayA loss function is not special — it is a function like any other, and the weights are its input. Training means searching that function's input space for a smaller output.

From two guesses to a slope

WhatSlope is rise over run: how much the loss changes per unit change in one weight.
WhyIt tells you which direction to move a weight, and roughly how much difference that move makes.
Where in MLThe building block of every derivative and gradient on this page.
?Freezing w₂ so w₁ has a single, plottable loss curve

Hold w₂ at its current guess, 5, and let only w₁ move. The loss becomes a function of one number instead of two: e(w₁) = 384 − 120w₁ − 15 = 369 − 120w₁, so L(w₁) = (369 − 120w₁)². (The constant 384 is p + 16.4 + 50.4 = 317.2 + 66.8 — everything in the loss that does not depend on w₁ or w₂, collected into one number.) Checking w₁ = 2 against this formula: 369 − 240 = 129, and 129² = 16 641 — the same loss found in 11.1, as it must be.

Now guess a second value of w₁, further from 2, and see how much the loss moved:

L(2) = (369 − 240)² = 129² = 16 641
L(2.5) = (369 − 300)² = 69² = 4 761
average slope = (4 761 − 16 641) / (2.5 − 2) = −11 880 / 0.5 = −23 760
Moving w₁ from 2 to 2.5 dropped the loss by 11 880 over a step of 0.5 — a negative slope, because increasing w₁ toward its correct value of 3 makes the loss smaller.
what each symbol is L(2), L(2.5)the loss at two different guesses for w₁ average slopetotal change in L divided by the total change in w₁ — the secant line between the two points
Interview takeawayAn average slope is a real, measurable quantity — no calculus required — but it depends on which two points you pick. Section 11.3 removes that dependence.

The derivative — shrinking the gap

WhatThe slope at a single point, found by shrinking the gap between two guesses until it vanishes.
WhyAn average slope depends on which second point you chose; the derivative does not — it is one number attached to one point.
Where in MLEvery number written into a gradient tensor after a framework's backward pass is one of these.
?Watch the secant settle as the gap shrinks

Pick the second guess closer and closer to w₁ = 2 and recompute the same average-slope calculation each time:

The average slope is not wandering randomly — it is converging on one number. Shrink the gap all the way to zero and it settles at −30 960. That settled value is the derivative, written dL/dw₁ at w₁ = 2.

The same number falls straight out of algebra, with no shrinking required — confirmation that the table above was heading somewhere real. Expand the loss curve from 11.2:

L(w₁) = (369 − 120w₁)² = 369² − 2(369)(120)w₁ + 120²w₁²
= 136 161 − 88 560w₁ + 14 400w₁²
dL/dw₁ = −88 560 + 28 800w₁
at w₁ = 2: dL/dw₁ = −88 560 + 57 600 = −30 960
Same answer as the shrinking-gap table, found by ordinary polynomial differentiation instead of a limit of secants. Both routes describe the same tangent line.
what each symbol is dL/dw₁the derivative of L with respect to w₁ — the exact slope at one point, not an average −30 960at the guess w₁ = 2, the loss is falling at 30 960 units per unit of w₁
?Why this is also how you would debug a gradient by hand

Nudge w₁ by a tiny amount ε, see how much L changes, divide by ε — that is exactly the shrinking-gap table above, stopped at a very small gap instead of zero. If that number does not roughly match what a framework's automatic differentiation reports for the same point, the backward pass has a bug. That check is called gradient checking, and it is still the fastest way to catch a wrong derivative in a hand-written layer.

Interview takeawayA derivative is the limit of an average slope as the gap between two points shrinks to zero — a speedometer reading, not the average speed of the whole trip.

Partial derivatives — freezing every other knob

WhatThe slope in one weight's direction while every other weight is held frozen at its current guess.
WhyThe loss depends on several weights at once; you need to isolate one to ask "if I nudge only this one, what happens?"
Where in MLOne weight's entry in the gradient tensor — a model with a billion weights has a billion of these per step.

Unfreeze w₂ and write the loss as a function of both unknowns: L(w₁, w₂) = (384 − 120w₁ − 3w₂)². The partial derivative with respect to w₁ asks: holding w₂ at its current value, how does L change per unit of w₁? Written ∂L/∂w₁ — the curly ∂ is the signal that other variables are being held still.

e(w₁, w₂) = 384 − 120w₁ − 3w₂
∂L/∂w₁ = 2e·(−120) = −240e
at (2, 5): e = 384 − 240 − 15 = 129, so ∂L/∂w₁ = −240(129) = −30 960
∂L/∂w₂ = 2e·(−3) = −6e = −6(129) = −774
Freezing w₂ at 5 and asking about w₁ alone reproduces the exact −30 960 found in 11.3 by ordinary differentiation — a partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with a promise attached: every other input stays put. The w₂ partial is a much smaller number, −774, because a 1-unit change in bedroom price moves the prediction by only 3 k€ per house, against 120 k€ per house for a 1-unit change in area price.
what each symbol is ∂L/∂w₁slope of the loss in the w₁ direction only ∂L/∂w₂slope of the loss in the w₂ direction only −120, −3house A's own area and bedroom count, carried in from the dot product
Interview takeawayA partial derivative is a full derivative with everything else nailed down; collecting all of them for every weight is the very next section.

The gradient — all the partials at once

WhatAll the partial derivatives collected into one vector, written ∇L.
WhyOne number per weight isn't a plan; one direction in weight-space, covering every weight at once, is.
Where in MLThe full contents of the gradient tensor after a backward pass — the input every optimizer (SGD, Adam, …) starts from.

∇L points in the direction of steepest ascent — the single direction in (w₁, w₂) space that increases the loss fastest — and its length says how steep. Training wants the loss to go down, so the update subtracts it:

w ← w − η·∇L

η (eta) is the learning rate — how large a step to take along the negative-gradient direction, chosen by you rather than handed to you by the data.

∇L = −30 960−774 at the guess 25
pick η = 0.00003 (3×10⁻⁵) — small enough that the steep w₁ direction does not overshoot past its target of 3 in one step
w₁ ← 2 − 0.00003(−30 960) = 2 + 0.9288 = 2.9288
w₂ ← 5 − 0.00003(−774) = 5 + 0.02322 = 5.02322
new loss: e = 384 − 120(2.9288) − 3(5.02322) ≈ 17.47, L ≈ 17.47² ≈ 305
One step dropped the loss from 16 641 to about 305 — most of the way there. But notice the asymmetry: w₁ moved by 0.93 toward its target of 3, while w₂ crept by only 0.02 toward its target of 8, even though both started equally wrong in relative terms. That is not a fluke of this particular η — 11.11 explains exactly why.
what each symbol is ∇Lthe gradient — both partial derivatives, stacked as a vector ηlearning rate — the fraction of the gradient actually subtracted each step
?Why real training never computes the gradient from one house alone

This whole page uses house A on its own so every number stays checkable by hand. A real training step would sum (or average) the loss over every house in the batch before differentiating, because a single house's gradient is a noisy, biased estimate of the direction that helps every house — it happily drags an already-correct weight away from its fitted value just to explain one example better. Averaging over many houses (or a mini-batch of them) is what keeps that noise from dominating the step. This is the calculus behind why stochastic gradient descent uses random mini-batches instead of the whole dataset every time: cheaper per step, at the cost of a noisier gradient — a trade the sheer size of most datasets makes worthwhile.

Interview takeawayThe gradient is provably the direction of steepest ascent; the update subtracts it because training wants descent. The minus sign is the entire idea — everything else is bookkeeping.

Stationary points — where the gradient goes quiet

WhatAny point where the gradient is exactly zero — flat ground in every direction at once.
WhyGradient descent stops changing the weights once the gradient vanishes, so this is where training would converge to.
Where in ML"Training converged" means the gradient got small — it does not by itself mean the weights landed anywhere good.

Both partial derivatives found in 11.4 are just e multiplied by a constant (−240 and −6). They can only both be zero at once when e itself is zero:

∇L = [0, 0]  ⇔  e = 384 − 120w₁ − 3w₂ = 0
⇔ 120w₁ + 3w₂ = 384
check module 10's answer: 120(3) + 3(8) = 360 + 24 = 384 ✓
Module 10's (3, 8) sits on this line, as it must — but so does every other point on it. One house's loss gives one equation in two unknowns, so the gradient vanishes along an entire line, not at one point. A stationary point is a necessary condition for a minimum; on its own it does not say the answer is unique, or even that it is a minimum at all.
Interview takeaway"The gradient is zero" and "this is the answer" are not the same claim. Zero gradient is flat ground; whether it is a valley floor, a peak, or a whole flat trough still needs checking.

Minimum — and, here, a whole line of them

WhatA stationary point where the surface curves upward in every direction — step any way and the loss goes up. Local: lowest in its neighbourhood. Global: lowest anywhere.
WhyThis is the actual target of training: not just flat ground, but flat ground with nowhere lower nearby.
Where in MLWhat "the model has converged to a good solution" is supposed to mean.

Every point on the line found in 11.6 has e = 0, hence L = e² = 0 — and zero is the smallest a squared number can ever be. So every point on that line is a global minimum, tied with every other point on it: house A's loss cannot tell (3, 8) apart from, say, (3.2, 0) — check it: 120(3.2) + 3(0) = 384, also zero loss. One house's evidence pins down only one combination of the two weights, not both of them separately. Whether this trough collapses back to a single point once more houses are added is exactly the question the Hessian answers in 11.11.

Interview takeawayA minimum being global does not make it unique. With too few constraints relative to unknowns, "the minimum" can be an entire flat set rather than one point — the calculus mirror of module 10.8's rank-deficiency story.

Saddle points — flat ground that isn't a minimum

WhatFlat ground (zero gradient) that curves down in some directions and up in others.
WhyA stationary point is not automatically a minimum — descent can stall here even though there is a lower point nearby.
Where in MLIn high-dimensional networks, saddles vastly outnumber true local minima and are the more common reason training slows down.

Could house A's own loss ever produce one? No. Section 11.15 shows this loss is convex — a single trough, curving up or staying flat in every direction, never down — so a genuine saddle (one direction curving up, another curving strictly down) is mathematically impossible here. Saddles need a function that stops being a simple squared linear combination — exactly what section 11.14's hypothetical deeper version of this model would introduce.

Interview takeawayZero gradient classifies into three outcomes — minimum, maximum, or saddle — and you need curvature, not just the gradient, to tell which. Squared-error linear regression only ever produces the first.

The second derivative — curvature in one direction

WhatThe slope of the slope — how sharply a curve bends. Positive: bowl-shaped. Negative: dome-shaped.
WhyZero gradient alone doesn't classify a stationary point; curvature is the second piece of evidence needed.
Where in MLThe one-dimensional version of the Hessian test that follows in 11.11.

Reuse 11.3's single-weight curve: dL/dw₁ = −88 560 + 28 800w₁. Differentiate once more.

d²L/dw₁² = d/dw₁ [ −88 560 + 28 800w₁ ] = 28 800
A positive constant, unchanging wherever w₁ sits — this curve is a fixed upward-bending parabola in the w₁ direction: no matter where you stand on it (with w₂ held at 5), stepping either way increases the loss. That is the one-dimensional confirmation of a minimum. What it cannot tell you is what happens in the w₂ direction at the same time — for that you need every second partial derivative at once, which is exactly what the Hessian collects.
Interview takeawayA positive second derivative confirms a bowl in one direction only. In more than one dimension, "positive in every direction" has to be checked all at once — that check is the Hessian.

What actually certifies a minimum

WhatTwo conditions together: first order — the gradient is zero. Second order — the curvature is positive in every direction.
WhyFirst order alone cannot distinguish a minimum from a maximum or a saddle; both conditions together can.
Where in MLThe precise version of "did training converge to something good," in any number of dimensions.

In one dimension: f′ = 0 and f″ > 0. In d dimensions: ∇f = 0 and every eigenvalue of the Hessian (11.11 defines it) is positive. That second version hands you saddle points for free: a saddle is exactly a stationary point whose Hessian has mixed-sign eigenvalues.

Why local minima turned out not to be the main problem

At a stationary point in d dimensions there are d curvature directions, each independently positive or negative. A local minimum needs all d of them positive. Treating the signs as a coin flip, that probability is 2⁻ᵈ — and d is in the millions for a real network. Saddle points, which need only a mixture of signs, are overwhelmingly more likely.

So large-scale training does not get trapped in a bad hole; it gets slowed down crawling across nearly flat saddle regions. That is a different problem with a different fix: momentum, which carries velocity through flat ground, and the noise from mini-batch sampling, which knocks the weights off an exact saddle. This is the standard answer to "why does deep learning work despite non-convexity."

Interview takeawayState the test as "gradient zero and Hessian positive definite," not just "gradient zero" — the second half is what tells a minimum apart from a saddle.

The Hessian — curvature in every direction at once

WhatThe matrix of all second partial derivatives — curvature in every direction, collected in one object.
WhyIts eigenvalues classify a stationary point exactly: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle, a zero eigenvalue means a flat direction the test can't decide.
Where in MLIts condition number — largest eigenvalue over smallest — is what makes a loss surface a "ravine," which is precisely what momentum and Adam exist to compensate for.
module 10.9 → an eigenvector is a direction a matrix stretches without turning, and its eigenvalue is the stretch factor → apply that same lens to curvature instead of correlation → the Hessian's eigenvalues classify a stationary point

Differentiate both of 11.4's partial derivatives one more time, with respect to both weights:

∂²L/∂w₁² = 28 800    ∂²L/∂w₁∂w₂ = 720    ∂²L/∂w₂² = 18

det(H−λI) = λ² − 28 818λ + 0 = λ(λ − 28 818) = 0  ⇒  λ₁ = 28 818, λ₂ = 0

One eigenvalue is a large positive number (28 818); the other is exactly zero. The strongly curved direction, v₁ = (120, 3), is nothing exotic — it is house A's own feature pair (area, bedrooms). That makes sense: this one-house loss only ever "sees" w₁ and w₂ through the single combination 120w₁ + 3w₂, so it can only curve along that direction. The zero-curvature direction, v₂ = (3, −120), is exactly the stationary line from 11.6 — flat because nothing about a single house's loss resists sliding along it.
what each symbol is Hthe Hessian — every second partial derivative, arranged as a matrix λ₁, λ₂the eigenvalues — how much H stretches each invariant direction v₁, v₂the eigenvectors — the two directions H does not turn
This is the same zero as module 10.8's

det(H) = 0 here means exactly what det(D) = 0 meant for the duplicate square-metre/square-foot column in 10.8: one direction carries no independent information. There, one repeated feature collapsed a direction; here, one house's worth of evidence collapses a direction in weight-space. Same signal, same fix — more independent evidence. Summing the loss over all four houses A–D, instead of just A, adds three more constraints; module 10.7 already showed that four independent house rows are enough to solve for a unique w = X⁺p — using the (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀp route. In calculus terms, the combined four-house Hessian has no zero eigenvalue left, the trough from 11.7 collapses to the single point (3, 8, −0.4, −12), and gradient descent on that combined loss converges to the exact weight module 10 solved for algebraically. Two different routes, one destination.

The asymmetry from 11.5's update step — w₁ jumping 0.93, w₂ crawling 0.02 — is this same Hessian showing up as an ill-conditioned surface: 28 800 versus 18 on the diagonal is a thousand-times difference in curvature, caused by area (a large-scale feature, values near 120) sitting next to bedroom count (a small-scale feature, values near 3). That is exactly the raw-feature-scale trap module 10.4 flagged for distance calculations, reappearing here as the reason plain gradient descent zig-zags across a "ravine" instead of heading straight for the minimum.

Interview takeawayThe Hessian's condition number (largest eigenvalue over smallest) is why plain gradient descent struggles on ill-conditioned losses — the steepest direction points across the ravine, not along it, which is exactly what momentum and adaptive optimizers correct for.

The Jacobian — when the output is a vector too

WhatThe matrix of first partial derivatives when a function takes many inputs and returns many outputs.
WhyA gradient is only defined for a function with one scalar output; predicting several houses at once returns a vector, and each output needs its own gradient.
Where in MLBackprop never builds a full Jacobian — it computes vector–Jacobian products, which is why it costs about one extra forward pass rather than one pass per parameter.

The chain rule — multiplying sensitivities

WhatTo find the sensitivity through a chain of steps, multiply the sensitivity of each step.
WhyThe loss depends on a weight only through intermediate quantities (a prediction, an error); the chain rule is how a derivative passes through those intermediates.
Where in MLBackpropagation is the chain rule, evaluated right to left so shared prefixes are computed once instead of once per weight.

11.3's single-weight loss is already a two-step chain, whether or not it was named as one. Step one turns w₁ into a shortfall; step two turns that shortfall into a loss:

step 1 (inner): u(w₁) = 369 − 120w₁    step 2 (outer): L(u) = u²
dL/du = 2u    du/dw₁ = −120
dL/dw₁ = (dL/du)·(du/dw₁) = 2u·(−120) = −240u
at w₁ = 2: u = 369 − 240 = 129, so dL/dw₁ = −240(129) = −30 960
Exactly the −30 960 found by direct expansion in 11.3 and by the partial derivative in 11.4 — three routes to one number. Backpropagation is this same multiply, repeated once per link in a longer chain: the sensitivity of the loss to a late quantity, times that quantity's sensitivity to an earlier one, times the next link back, computed right to left so the shared prefix — dL/du here — is reused instead of recomputed for every weight.
what each symbol is uthe intermediate quantity — the shortfall between 369 and the scaled weight dL/du, du/dw₁the two link-by-link sensitivities being multiplied
Interview takeawayBackpropagation is not "based on" or "similar to" the chain rule — it is the chain rule, applied right to left so the shared work is done once per layer instead of once per weight.

Vanishing and exploding gradients

A hypothetical extension of this page's example

The house-price model is one linear layer, so its chain in 11.13 has exactly two links. A real 50-layer network doing the same job would be absurd overkill for four houses — but it is the smallest way to see what a 50-link chain does that a 2-link chain cannot. Suppose each layer's local derivative squashes its input by a roughly constant factor before passing it on, the way 11.13's −120 scaled the shortfall. Chain fifty of those factors together, one multiply per link, exactly as in 11.13.

factor 0.9 per layer (typical of a squashing activation): 0.9² = 0.81, 0.9⁵ = 0.59049
0.9¹⁰ = (0.9⁵)² ≈ 0.3487    0.9²⁵ = 0.9²⁰·0.9⁵ ≈ 0.0718    0.9⁵⁰ = (0.9²⁵)² ≈ 0.00515
factor 1.5 per layer: 1.5² = 2.25, 1.5⁵ = 7.59375, 1.5¹⁰ ≈ 57.67
1.5²⁵ = 1.5²⁰·1.5⁵ ≈ 25 253    1.5⁵⁰ = (1.5²⁵)² ≈ 6.4×10⁸
With a typical factor under 1, fifty multiplications shrink the signal to about half a percent of its size — the earliest layers receive almost no learning signal, which is vanishing gradients. With a typical factor over 1, the same fifty multiplications inflate the signal past six hundred million times its size — one catastrophic step and likely NaNs on the next iteration, which is exploding gradients. (A commonly repeated version of this example claims a small-enough factor, like sigmoid's derivative cap of 0.25, "underflows to exactly zero in any float format" after fifty layers. That is not quite right: 0.25⁵⁰ ≈ 7.9×10⁻³¹, which a 64-bit float still represents — it is only "effectively zero" in the sense of carrying no useful learning signal, and it does genuinely underflow in the lower-precision float16 formats common in large-scale training.)

Two of the standard fixes are one-line consequences of this same product. Writing a layer as x + f(x) instead of f(x) — a residual connection — makes its local derivative 1 + f′(x) instead of f′(x). That extra constant 1 is never multiplied down, so it gives the gradient a path straight back through the network regardless of how many layers deep it started. Clipping the gradient's overall length before the update (gradient clipping) treats the exploding side directly: it rescales the vector without changing its direction whenever it exceeds a chosen limit.

Interview takeawayVanishing and exploding gradients are the same fact — a long product of per-layer factors — read in two directions. Say the product explicitly; it is the difference between a memorised term and a derived one.

Convexity — one bowl or many

WhatA function shaped like a single bowl: the straight line between any two points on the graph lies on or above the graph.
WhyA convex function has no bad local minima to get stuck in — every local minimum is automatically global.
Where in MLLinear regression, logistic regression and SVMs are convex — one guaranteed answer. Neural networks are not.

Equivalently, for a smooth function: convex means the Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere — no eigenvalue is ever negative. House A's loss is a squared linear function, L = e² with e linear in (w₁, w₂), and its Hessian from 11.11 is the outer product H = 2[x₁, x₂]ᵀ[x₁, x₂] — a form whose eigenvalues are always 0 and 2(x₁² + x₂²) ≥ 0, for any weight guess, not only the one checked in 11.11. Both eigenvalues are non-negative everywhere, so this loss is convex — and that is precisely why 11.8 could rule out a saddle in advance: a saddle needs a strictly negative eigenvalue somewhere, and a positive semi-definite Hessian can never produce one. It also explains 11.7's tied minimum line: semi-definite, not strictly positive definite, because one eigenvalue sits at exactly zero.

Interview takeawayConvex means "no eigenvalue of the Hessian is ever negative" — that single fact rules out saddles, rules out spurious local minima, and is why classical linear models get one guaranteed answer that deep networks don't.

Differentiability — where the slope stops existing

WhatSmooth enough to have a single well-defined slope everywhere. A sharp kink has no one slope at that point.
WhyGradient descent needs a slope to follow at every point it visits; a kink is a point where that slope is undefined.
Where in MLReLU's kink at exactly zero, and why you cannot backpropagate through argmax at all.

House A's squared-error loss, L = e², is a smooth polynomial in (w₁, w₂) — differentiable everywhere, no kinks, which is exactly why every derivative on this page could be written down cleanly. That smoothness is a choice, not a law: had the loss instead been the absolute error, |e|, it would have a sharp kink exactly where e = 0 — precisely 11.6 and 11.7's minimizing line. At a kink there is no single slope, only a left-hand slope and a right-hand slope that disagree; frameworks pick one value in between (a subgradient, usually 0) and move on.

?When there is no gradient signal at all, not just an ambiguous one

A subgradient is still a usable, if slightly arbitrary, number. argmax — picking the index of the largest entry in a vector, used to turn model scores into a hard decision — is worse: its derivative is zero almost everywhere (nudging the scores by a hair almost never changes which index wins) and undefined exactly at a tie. Multiply zero into the chain rule from 11.13 and every weight upstream of that argmax receives no learning signal whatsoever.

Two standard escapes, both used with real models:

  • Gumbel-softmax — a smooth, temperature-controlled relaxation of argmax: sample a hard choice on the forward pass, but use a soft, differentiable version of the same choice on the backward pass, sharpening it toward a true argmax as training proceeds.
  • Straight-through estimator — take the true hard argmax forward, and simply pretend the backward pass was the identity function. It is a genuinely biased gradient, not an approximation that improves with more compute, and it is how vector-quantised models like VQ-VAE train their discrete codebooks anyway.
Interview takeawayA kink costs you a unique slope, which a subgradient patches over. A step function like argmax costs you the slope entirely — that is a strictly worse problem, and it needs a genuinely different fix.

Questions

BASICWhat is a derivative? Explain it without notation.
It is the steepness of a curve at one exact point — a speedometer reading, not the average speed over the whole trip.

Take two points on the loss curve, measure the average slope between them, then slide the second point toward the first. Section 11.3 did this on house A's loss holding w₂ fixed at 5: the average slope over [2, 2.5] was −23 760, over [2, 2.1] it was −29 520, over [2, 2.01] it was −30 816, converging on −30 960 as the gap shrinks to nothing. That settled number is the derivative.

In machine learning, the curve is "how wrong is my model," plotted against one weight. The derivative says which way to turn that weight and how urgently. Every entry a framework writes into a gradient tensor after a backward pass is one of these.

Trap

"So a zero derivative means we're at the best point?" No — a zero derivative means flat ground, which section 11.6 showed can be a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle. You need the second derivative (11.9) or, in more than one dimension, the Hessian (11.11) to tell which.

BASICWhat is a gradient and why does the update subtract it?
The gradient is the vector of every partial derivative, and it points straight uphill — so subtracting it is what moves downhill.

For house A's loss at the guess (2, 5), the gradient was ∇L = [−30 960, −774] — each entry answers "if I nudge only this one weight, how does the loss respond?" That vector is provably the direction of steepest ascent; its negative is the steepest descent, hence w ← w − η·∇L.

Length matters too: a large gradient means a steep surface, so the same learning rate produces a bigger step. Section 11.5's update moved w₁ by 0.93 but w₂ by only 0.02 in the same step, purely because their gradient components differed by a factor of forty.

Trap

Steepest descent is only the best direction locally, and only when the weights are comparably scaled. Section 11.11 showed exactly why an elongated, ill-conditioned loss makes the steepest direction point across a ravine rather than along it — the failure momentum and Adam exist to fix.

MIDWhat is a saddle point and why does it matter more than local minima?
A saddle is a point with zero gradient that goes down in some directions and up in others — and in high dimensions it is overwhelmingly the more common kind of flat point.

The counting argument from 11.10: at a stationary point in d dimensions there are d curvature directions, and a local minimum needs every single one positive. Treating signs as roughly independent coin flips, that probability is about 2⁻ᵈ — for a model with millions of parameters, effectively zero. Saddles, needing only a mixture of signs, dominate combinatorially.

This reframes what "stuck" means: training is not trapped in a bad hole, it is crawling across an almost-flat region where the gradient is tiny in most directions. The fixes follow directly: momentum carries velocity through flat ground, mini-batch noise knocks the weights off the exact saddle, and adaptive methods rescale the tiny directions back up.

Trap

"So local minima never matter?" They matter a great deal in small, low-dimensional non-convex problems like k-means or GMM fitting, where restarts genuinely change the answer. The saddle argument is a large-d argument; do not generalise it to an 8-cluster k-means run.

MIDHow does the chain rule become backpropagation?
Backpropagation is the chain rule, evaluated right to left so the shared factors are computed once instead of once per parameter.

Section 11.13 showed the smallest possible version: house A's loss is a two-step chain — a linear shortfall, then a square — and its derivative is the product of the two link sensitivities, dL/du = 2u and du/dw₁ = −120, multiplying to reproduce the same −30 960 found three other ways on this page. A real network is the same idea over many more links.

Reverse-mode (right to left) wins by an enormous margin for our shape of problem: one scalar loss, millions of weights. Going backward from the loss, every prefix of the product is shared across all the weights in a layer, so it is computed once. The cost is roughly two forward passes regardless of parameter count; forward-mode would cost one pass per parameter.

Trap

"Is backprop an optimisation algorithm?" No — backprop computes gradients. SGD, Adam and similar optimisers are what consume them to actually update the weights. Conflating the two is a common and very visible slip.

MIDExplain vanishing and exploding gradients in terms of calculus.
The gradient reaching an early layer is a long product of per-layer derivatives; a typical factor below 1 makes the product decay toward zero, above 1 makes it blow up.

Section 11.14 derived both directions by repeated squaring: fifty layers with a typical factor of 0.9 gives 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.00515 — the earliest layers get almost no learning signal. With a typical factor of 1.5 the same fifty layers give 1.5⁵⁰ ≈ 6.4×10⁸ — one catastrophic step and likely NaNs next iteration.

The standard fixes each attack one factor of that product: activation functions with a derivative of 1 on their active side instead of a fraction; residual connections, which make a block's local derivative 1 + f′(x) so the gradient always has an unattenuated path straight through; careful initialisation, which sets the typical factor near 1 from the start; and gradient clipping, a hard cap on the gradient's length that treats the exploding symptom directly.

Trap

People say residuals "let gradients flow." Be ready to say why: the identity path contributes a derivative of exactly 1, a term that is never multiplied down no matter how deep the stack. Without that sentence it sounds memorised rather than derived.

SENIORWhat does the Hessian tell you, and why don't we use second-order optimisers?
The Hessian is curvature in every direction at once; it is impractical at scale because it has N² entries and N is in the billions.

What it buys you: section 11.11 built a real one — H = [[28 800, 720], [720, 18]] for house A's two-weight loss — and read off its eigenvalues, 28 818 and 0, to see that one direction curves sharply and another is perfectly flat. In general, all-positive eigenvalues mean a minimum, mixed signs mean a saddle, and the condition number (largest eigenvalue over smallest) tells you how elongated the loss surface is. Newton's method uses H⁻¹∇L, rescaling every direction by its own curvature — for a clean quadratic loss it would reach the minimum in a single step.

Why it is impractical at scale: a 7-billion-parameter model's Hessian has roughly 5×10¹⁹ entries. Storing it is impossible, inverting it far more so. Even a rank-limited quasi-Newton approximation like L-BFGS — which estimates the Hessian's action from a short history of recent gradients instead of forming it exactly — wants consistent gradients from one step to the next, which mini-batch noise disrupts.

So training uses cheap surrogates instead, and this is the useful framing: Adam is a crude diagonal approximation to the inverse Hessian. Dividing by the square root of the running second moment of the gradient rescales each weight by an estimate of its own curvature — exactly the kind of correction 11.11's ill-conditioned Hessian needed. That is why Adam handles badly conditioned surfaces far better than plain SGD.

Trap

Do not claim Adam is second order — it uses squared first derivatives, not second derivatives. It approximates the diagonal of the Fisher information matrix — the expected curvature of the negative log-likelihood, a close statistical cousin of the Hessian — which coincides with true curvature only under specific assumptions. Say "approximates," and say which assumption.

SENIORDeep networks are non-convex. Why is that not fatal?
Because in very high dimensions the bad local minima people feared are vanishingly rare, most flat points are saddles you can escape, and the many minima that do exist turn out to have similar loss.

House A's own loss (11.15) is the convex baseline to contrast against: a positive semi-definite Hessian everywhere, no saddle possible, a whole tied line of global minima rather than a maze of unequal local ones. A deep network's loss surface fails every one of those properties. Three strands explain why that is survivable rather than fatal:

  • Geometry. A local minimum needs all d Hessian eigenvalues positive (11.10); saddles need only a mixture, so they dominate combinatorially. Getting stuck in a hole is not the failure mode; crawling across a plateau is.
  • Empirical flatness. The minima large networks actually reach cluster tightly in loss value — which one you land in barely changes test performance, unlike a small non-convex problem where restarts change the answer.
  • Over-parameterisation. With more parameters than constraints, the solution set is closer to a connected manifold than isolated points, and a descent direction is nearly always available — the high-dimensional cousin of 11.7's flat trough.

Where the honesty belongs: non-convexity still costs you. There is no uniqueness guarantee, so results depend on seed, initialisation and data order; you cannot certify optimality; and reproducibility becomes an engineering discipline rather than a mathematical fact.

Trap

"Then convexity is irrelevant?" No — it is exactly why logistic regression is still the right first model for a fraud baseline: one global optimum, reached deterministically, in seconds, with coefficients a regulator can read. Know when you would choose the convex model.

SENIORWhy can't you backpropagate through argmax, and what do people do instead?
Because argmax is a step function — its derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jumps — so it transmits no gradient at all.

Nudge the input scores by a hair and the winning index almost always stays the same, so the output does not change: derivative zero. At the precise tie where the winner flips, the output jumps discontinuously: derivative undefined. Multiply zero into the chain rule from 11.13 and everything upstream of that argmax receives nothing — a strictly worse problem than 11.16's kink, where at least a subgradient exists.

The standard escapes, from mildest to most drastic:

  • Use softmax instead — a smooth relaxation of the same idea, differentiable everywhere. This is why training uses the full probability distribution and only inference takes a hard argmax.
  • Gumbel-softmax — sample discretely on the forward pass but use a temperature-controlled soft version on the backward pass, annealing the temperature down during training.
  • Straight-through estimator — take the hard argmax forward and pretend the backward pass was the identity. Biased, and it works anyway; it is how vector-quantised models like VQ-VAE train their codebooks.
  • Reinforcement learning — when the discrete step genuinely cannot be relaxed (sampling text, calling a tool, choosing an action), stop trying to differentiate through it and use policy-gradient methods instead. This is exactly why RLHF and GRPO exist: they optimise through a non-differentiable sampling step by treating it as an action to be reinforced, not a function to be differentiated.
Trap

Straight-through is not an approximation that gets better with more compute — it is a genuinely biased gradient. It works because the bias points roughly the right way, not because it is nearly correct.
