Calculus & minima Training a model is one question asked ten million times: which way do I turn this knob to make the error smaller? Calculus is the machinery that answers it, and a “minimum” is the place where the answer becomes “nowhere”.

The problem this was invented to solve

Imagine you are on a hillside in thick fog. You want to get to the bottom of the valley. You cannot see the valley. All you can do is feel the ground under your feet and notice which way it tilts. So you take a step downhill, feel again, step again.

That is gradient descent, and it is the entire training algorithm of every model in this course. The fog is real: a model with 7 billion parameters lives on a 7-billion-dimensional hillside that nobody has ever seen or ever will. All we ever have is the local tilt.

The derivative is the tilt. That is the whole of calculus for our purposes. Everything else is bookkeeping for when there are many knobs instead of one.

The terms

Function basic f(x)

A machine: put a number in, get a number out. In ML the machine is “given these parameter values, how wrong am I?”

Your loss function. Input = every weight in the model. Output = one number.

Slope basic

Rise over run: how much the output changes per unit of input change. Positive = going up as you move right. Negative = going down.

On a road sign, “12 % gradient” is a slope of 0.12.

Derivative basic f′(x), df/dx

The slope at a single point, obtained by measuring the average slope over a gap and then shrinking the gap to nothing.

Your speedometer. Average speed over an hour is rise/run; your speed right now is the derivative of distance with respect to time.

Every number in .grad after loss.backward().

Partial derivative basic ∂f/∂x

The slope in one direction while every other input is held frozen. “If I nudge only this knob, what happens?”

One weight's gradient. Seven billion of them per step.

Gradient basic ∇f

All the partial derivatives collected into one vector. It points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length says how steep.

Standing on the hill, the gradient is the compass bearing straight up the slope, with the needle length telling you how brutal the climb is.

This is why the update is w ← w − lr·∇L. The minus sign is the entire reason: the gradient points uphill and we want down.

Stationary point basic

Anywhere the gradient is exactly zero — flat ground. Could be a minimum, a maximum, a saddle, or a plateau. Zero gradient alone does not tell you which.

Training “converging” means the gradient got small. It does not by itself mean you arrived anywhere good.

Minimum basic

A stationary point where the ground curves upward in every direction — step any way you like and you go up. Local: the lowest point in its own neighbourhood. Global: the lowest point anywhere.

A puddle on a hillside is a local minimum. The lake at the bottom is the global one. Water in the puddle has no local reason to move.

Saddle point mid

Flat ground that goes down in some directions and up in others. Zero gradient, but not a minimum.

A horse's saddle, or a mountain pass: downhill to the two valleys, uphill to the two peaks.

In high dimensions saddles vastly outnumber local minima, and they are the real reason training stalls — this is the key insight below.

Second derivative mid f″(x)

The slope of the slope — how sharply the curve bends. Positive = bowl-shaped (minimum-like). Negative = dome-shaped (maximum-like). Zero = flat or an inflection.

Your speedometer is the first derivative; your accelerator pedal is the second.

Hessian senior H

The matrix of all second partial derivatives — curvature in every direction at once. Its eigenvalues classify a stationary point: all positive ⇒ minimum, all negative ⇒ maximum, mixed signs ⇒ saddle.

Its condition number (largest eigenvalue over smallest) is what makes a loss surface a “ravine”, and is precisely what momentum and Adam exist to compensate for.

Jacobian mid J

The matrix of first partial derivatives when the function takes many inputs and returns many outputs. Row i is the gradient of output i.

Backprop never builds a Jacobian. It computes vector–Jacobian products, which is why it costs about the same as a forward pass rather than N times more.

Chain rule basic (f∘g)′ = f′(g)·g′

To get the sensitivity through a chain of steps, multiply the sensitivities of each step.

Turn a dial that moves a lever that lifts a bucket. How much does the bucket rise per turn of the dial? Multiply the three ratios.

Backpropagation is the chain rule. Not “uses”, not “is like” — it is the chain rule, applied right to left so the shared work is done once.

Convex mid

A function shaped like a single bowl: the straight line between any two points on the graph lies on or above the graph. A convex function has exactly one minimum, and every local minimum is global.

A salad bowl is convex. An egg carton is not.

Linear regression, logistic regression, and SVMs are convex — one answer, always reachable. Neural networks are wildly non-convex. That difference explains why the classical models have closed-form or guaranteed solutions and deep ones need seeds, schedules and luck.

Differentiable mid

Smooth enough to have a slope everywhere. A sharp kink (like |x| at 0) has no single slope there.

ReLU is not differentiable at exactly 0, and nobody cares: frameworks pick a subgradient (usually 0) and the probability of landing precisely on the kink is nil. But it is why you cannot backprop through argmax — and why Gumbel-softmax and straight-through estimators exist.

What a derivative actually is

Take two points on a curve and draw the straight line between them: that is the average slope over the gap, and it is a thing you can genuinely measure. Now slide the second point toward the first. The line pivots. As the gap goes to zero, the line settles on one particular tilt — the tangent. That settled value is the derivative.

This matters practically because it is exactly how you would check a gradient implementation by hand. Perturb one weight by a tiny ε, see how much the loss changes, divide. If that number does not match what autograd reported, your backward pass is wrong. That is gradient checking, and it is still the fastest way to find a bug in a custom layer.

So what is a minimum?

Two conditions, and interviewers want both:

  1. First order: the gradient is zero. Flat ground. This alone is not enough — a hilltop and a mountain pass are also flat.
  2. Second order: the curvature is positive in every direction. Whichever way you step, you go up.

In one dimension that is “f′ = 0 and f″ > 0”. In many dimensions it is “∇f = 0 and every eigenvalue of the Hessian is positive”. That second version is the answer to the interview question, because it hands you saddle points for free: a saddle is where the gradient is zero but the Hessian has both positive and negative eigenvalues.

Why local minima turned out not to be the problem

For twenty years the fear was that gradient descent would get trapped in bad local minima. It mostly does not, and the reason is a counting argument. At a stationary point in d dimensions you have d curvature directions, each independently positive or negative. For a local minimum you need all d of them positive. If it were a coin flip, that is 2−d — and d is in the millions. Saddle points, where the signs are mixed, are overwhelmingly more common.

So training does not get stuck in a hole; it gets slowed down crawling across nearly flat saddle regions. And that is a very different problem, with a very different fix: momentum, which keeps you moving through flat ground, and stochastic noise, which knocks you off the exact saddle. This is the single best thing to say if asked “why does deep learning work despite non-convexity”.

The chain rule is backpropagation

A neural network is a chain: input → layer 1 → layer 2 → … → loss. You want ∂loss/∂w for a weight buried in layer 1. The chain rule says: multiply the sensitivity of the loss to layer 2's output, by layer 2's output's sensitivity to layer 1's output, by layer 1's output's sensitivity to w.

∂L∂w₁ = ∂L∂a₃ · ∂a₃∂a₂ · ∂a₂∂a₁ · ∂a₁∂w₁ Backprop computes this right to left, so the shared prefix is computed once for every weight in the layer instead of once per weight.

Two consequences fall straight out of that product, and both are interview answers:

  • Vanishing gradients. Each factor in the product is typically less than 1. Multiply fifty of them and you get something like 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005. The early layers receive almost no signal and effectively stop learning. Sigmoid makes this much worse, because its derivative maxes out at 0.25 — fifty layers of that is 0.25⁵⁰, which is zero in any float format.
  • Exploding gradients. Same product with factors above 1. Now you get 1.5⁵⁰, which is about 6×10⁸, a single catastrophic step, and NaNs on the next iteration. Hence gradient clipping.

And this is why residual connections work. Writing a block as x + f(x) instead of f(x) makes the local derivative 1 + f′(x). That constant 1 gives the gradient a path straight through the network that is never multiplied down. It is the single most important architectural idea of the last decade and it is a one-line consequence of the chain rule.

Questions

BASICWhat is a derivative? Explain it without notation.
It is the steepness of a curve at one exact point — the reading on a speedometer rather than the average speed over the whole trip.

The construction is: take two points, measure the average slope between them (rise over run), then slide them together. As the gap shrinks the answer settles on one number, and that is the derivative.

In machine learning the curve is “how wrong is my model”, plotted against a parameter. The derivative tells me which way to turn that parameter and how urgently. Every entry in .grad after a backward pass is one of these.

Trap

“So a zero derivative means we are at the best point?” No — a zero derivative means flat ground, which is a minimum, a maximum, or a saddle. You need the second derivative to tell which, and in high dimensions the flat point is a saddle far more often than a minimum.

BASICWhat is a gradient and why does the update subtract it?
The gradient is the vector of all the partial derivatives, and it points straight uphill — so to go downhill you subtract it.

Each component answers “if I nudge only this one weight, how does the loss respond?”. Assemble them and you get a direction in parameter space. That direction is provably the steepest ascent; its negative is the steepest descent. Hence w ← w − lr·∇L.

The length matters too: a large gradient means a steep surface, so the same learning rate produces a bigger step. That coupling is why an unscaled loss (say, summing instead of averaging over a batch) silently changes your effective learning rate.

Trap

Steepest descent is only the best direction locally, and only when the axes are comparably scaled. On an elongated ravine the steepest direction points across the valley, not along it, so you zig-zag. That is exactly the failure momentum and Adam fix.

MIDWhat is a saddle point and why does it matter more than local minima?
A saddle is a point with zero gradient that goes down in some directions and up in others — and in high dimensions it is overwhelmingly the more common kind of flat point.

The counting argument: at a stationary point in d dimensions you have d curvature directions. A local minimum needs every single one positive. Treat the signs as roughly independent and the probability is about 2−d — for a model with millions of parameters, effectively zero. Saddles, which need only a mix, dominate.

This reframes what “stuck” means. Training is not trapped in a bad hole it can never escape; it is crawling across an almost-flat region where the gradient is tiny in most directions. The fixes follow directly: momentum carries velocity through flat ground, stochastic noise from mini-batching knocks you off the exact saddle, and adaptive methods rescale the tiny directions back up.

The empirical result that backs this up — Choromanska and LeCun's work, and Dauphin's saddle-point paper — is that in large networks the local minima that exist are nearly all close in value to the global one. Which minimum you land in matters much less than people assumed.

Trap

“So local minima never matter?” They matter a great deal in small models and in classical non-convex problems like k-means or GMM fitting, where the dimension is low and restarts genuinely change the answer. The saddle argument is a large-d argument; do not generalise it to your 8-cluster k-means.

MIDHow does the chain rule become backpropagation?
Backpropagation is the chain rule, evaluated right to left so the shared factors are computed once instead of once per parameter.

The loss depends on a first-layer weight through a chain of intermediate activations, so its derivative is a product of per-step sensitivities. You could evaluate that product left to right (forward-mode) or right to left (reverse-mode).

Reverse-mode wins by an enormous margin for our shape of problem: one scalar output, millions of inputs. Going backward from the single loss, every prefix of the product is shared across all the parameters in a layer, so you compute it once. The total cost is roughly two forward passes regardless of parameter count. Forward-mode would cost one pass per parameter.

The practical consequences: you must keep the forward activations in memory to compute the backward pass, which is why activation memory dominates training and why gradient checkpointing (recompute instead of store) trades compute for memory.

Trap

“Is backprop an optimisation algorithm?” No. Backprop computes gradients. SGD, Adam and friends are the optimisers that consume them. Conflating the two is a very common and very visible slip.

MIDExplain vanishing and exploding gradients in terms of calculus.
The gradient reaching an early layer is a long product of per-layer derivatives; if the typical factor is below 1 the product decays to zero, if above 1 it blows up.

Fifty layers with an average factor of 0.9 gives 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005 — the first layers get essentially no learning signal. With 1.5 you get 1.5⁵⁰ ≈ 6×10⁸ and one step destroys the model.

Sigmoid was the classic culprit: its derivative peaks at 0.25 and is far smaller once the unit saturates, so deep sigmoid stacks were untrainable. The fixes each attack one factor of the product:

  • ReLU/GELU — derivative of 1 on the active side instead of ≤ 0.25.
  • Residual connections — the block derivative becomes 1 + f′, giving the gradient an unattenuated path all the way back. This is the big one.
  • Normalisation layers — keep activations in the regime where derivatives are healthy, which also flattens the loss surface.
  • Careful init (He, Xavier) — set the initial factor near 1 by construction.
  • Gradient clipping — a hard cap on the norm; treats the symptom, but it is what actually saves a transformer run at 3 a.m.
Trap

People say residuals “let gradients flow”. Be ready to say why: the identity path contributes a derivative of exactly 1, so the product has a term that is never multiplied down no matter how deep the stack. Without that sentence it sounds memorised.

SENIORWhat does the Hessian tell you, and why don't we use second-order optimisers?
The Hessian is the curvature in every direction; we skip it because it has N² entries and N is in the billions.

What it buys you: its eigenvalues classify stationary points (all positive = minimum, mixed = saddle), and its condition number — largest eigenvalue over smallest — tells you how elongated the loss ravine is. A high condition number is precisely why plain gradient descent zig-zags: the steepest direction points across the ravine rather than along it. Newton's method uses H⁻¹∇L, which rescales every direction by its curvature and would reach the minimum of a quadratic in a single step.

Why it is impractical: for a 7B-parameter model the Hessian has 4.9×10¹⁹ entries. Storing it is impossible, inverting it far more so. Even a rank-limited approximation like L-BFGS wants consistent gradients across steps, which mini-batch noise destroys.

So we use cheap surrogates, and this is the useful framing: Adam is a crude diagonal approximation to the inverse Hessian. Dividing by √v (the running second moment of the gradient) rescales each coordinate by an estimate of its own curvature. That is why Adam handles badly conditioned surfaces so much better than SGD, and why it needs almost no learning-rate tuning per layer.

Trap

Do not claim Adam is second order — it uses squared first derivatives, not second derivatives. It approximates the Fisher information diagonal, which coincides with curvature under some assumptions. Say “approximates”, and say which assumption.

SENIORDeep networks are non-convex. Why is that not fatal?
Because in very high dimensions the bad local minima people feared are vanishingly rare, most flat points are saddles you can escape, and the many minima that do exist turn out to have similar loss.

Three strands, and a good answer touches all three:

  • Geometry. A local minimum requires all d Hessian eigenvalues positive. Saddles need only a mixture, so they dominate combinatorially. Getting stuck in a hole is not the failure mode; crawling across a plateau is.
  • Empirical flatness. The minima that large networks actually reach cluster tightly in loss value. Which one you land in barely changes test performance — a very different situation from a small non-convex problem where restarts change the answer.
  • Over-parameterisation. With more parameters than constraints the solution set is a connected manifold rather than isolated points, and there is nearly always a descent direction available.

Where the honesty belongs: non-convexity does still cost you. There is no uniqueness guarantee, so results depend on seed, initialisation and data order; you cannot certify optimality; and reproducibility becomes an engineering discipline rather than a mathematical fact. That is exactly why MLflow tracking seeds and data versions is not bureaucracy — with a convex model you could re-derive the fit, and here you cannot.

Trap

“Then convexity is irrelevant?” No. It is exactly why logistic regression is still the right first model for a fraud baseline: one global optimum, reached deterministically, in seconds, with coefficients a regulator can read. You should be able to say when you would choose the convex model.

SENIORWhy can't you backpropagate through argmax, and what do people do instead?
Because argmax is a step function — its derivative is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jumps — so it transmits no gradient at all.

Nudge the logits by a hair and the winning index almost always stays the same, so the output does not change: derivative zero. At the precise tie where the winner flips, the output jumps discontinuously: derivative undefined. Multiply zero into the chain rule and everything upstream receives nothing.

The three standard escapes:

  • Use softmax instead — a smooth relaxation of the same idea. This is why training uses the full probability distribution and only inference takes an argmax.
  • Gumbel-softmax / concrete relaxation — sample discretely in the forward pass but use a temperature-controlled soft version in the backward pass. Anneal the temperature down during training.
  • Straight-through estimator — take the hard argmax forward and simply pretend the backward pass was the identity. Biased, and it works anyway; it is how vector-quantised models like VQ-VAE train their codebooks.

And the fourth option, which is the honest answer for LLMs: when the discrete step genuinely cannot be relaxed — sampling text, calling a tool, choosing an action — stop trying to differentiate and use reinforcement learning instead. That is exactly why RLHF and GRPO exist: they optimise through a non-differentiable sampling step using policy gradients rather than backprop through the choice.

Trap

Straight-through is not an approximation that gets better with more compute — it is a genuinely biased gradient. It works because the bias points roughly the right way, not because it is nearly correct.
