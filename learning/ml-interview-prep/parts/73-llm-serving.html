LLM serving platform The design question where arithmetic wins. Everyone can say "KV cache" and "batching"; very few can say how much memory a request costs, why prefill and decode are different machines pretending to be one, and where the money actually goes. Do the numbers out loud.

How to use this module

This is where module 45 cashes out. Learn the three numbers — KV bytes per token, the prefill/decode split, cost per million tokens — well enough to derive them at the whiteboard. The rest of the answer follows from them.

The prompt, and the first four minutes

The prompt

"We're serving an open-weights LLM for several internal products. Design the platform."

What to askWhy it changes the design
Interactive chat, or batch/offline jobs?Completely different objectives — one optimises latency, the other throughput per dollar, and they should not share a fleet.
Streaming or full response?Streaming makes time-to-first-token the metric users feel, which changes what you optimise.
Typical prompt and output lengths?Decides the prefill/decode ratio, and therefore whether you are compute-bound or memory-bandwidth-bound.
Model size, and can we quantise?Decides GPU count per replica and whether tensor parallelism is needed at all.
How many tenants, and is isolation required?Noisy-neighbour and fairness handling, plus whether one shared pool is even allowed.
Traffic shape — steady, spiky, business hours?GPUs cannot autoscale in seconds; a spiky load with cold starts is the hardest constraint in the whole design.
Do products need different fine-tunes?Multi-LoRA on a shared base versus separate fleets — an order-of-magnitude cost difference.
The assumptions I would design against
  • Mixed workload: an interactive chat product and several batch enrichment jobs.
  • Model: a 13 B-class open-weights model, bf16 by default, quantised variants allowed for the batch path.
  • Targets: p95 time-to-first-token < 500 ms — the 95th percentile, so 95 of every 100 requests start streaming within half a second — sustained inter-token latency < 50 ms (≈ 20 tokens/s, faster than reading).
  • Shape: ~2,000-token prompts, ~300-token outputs — prefill-heavy, which matters.
  • Five internal tenants, several with their own LoRA adapters.

Why three pools. A batch job with a 4,000-token prompt sitting in the same batch as an interactive turn destroys that turn's latency. Separating them by SLO — not by model — is the single highest-leverage decision in the design, and it also lets the batch pool run quantised on preemptible capacity — cheap GPUs the provider may reclaim at short notice, which is fine for a job that can simply restart — while the interactive pool stays bf16 on reserved capacity.

The arithmetic, out loud

Weights the easy number

bf16 is 2 bytes per parameter, so a 13 B model is ≈ 26 GB of weights. INT8 halves it to ~13 GB, INT4 quarters it to ~6.5 GB. On an 80 GB GPU that leaves roughly 50 GB for KV cache and activations in bf16 — and that remainder is what decides your concurrency.

KV cache per token the number people fumble

2 (K and V) × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes. For a 40-layer model with 40 heads of dimension 128 in bf16 that is 2 × 40 × 40 × 128 × 2 ≈ 820 KB per token — so a 2,000-token conversation holds ~1.6 GB of KV, and 50 GB of headroom serves only about 30 concurrent conversations.

This is the arithmetic that makes grouped-query attention worth explaining: cutting 40 kv_heads to 8 divides KV memory by five, taking the same conversation to ~330 MB and the concurrency to ~150. That is the entire reason modern models ship with GQA.

Prefill vs decode the mental model

Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel — one big matrix multiply, compute-bound, saturates the GPU, and it sets time-to-first-token. Decode produces one token at a time — every step reads all the weights to compute a single token, so it is memory-bandwidth-bound: the limit is how fast weights can be read out of memory, not how fast the chip can multiply, and the GPU's arithmetic units sit mostly idle.

Almost every serving optimisation is one of two things: making decode less bandwidth-starved (batching more sequences so one weight read serves many tokens; speculative decoding so one read produces several tokens), or stopping long prefills from blocking decode (chunked prefill).

Cost per million tokens say a number

Take an 80 GB GPU at ≈ $2/hour. If the replica sustains 2,000 output tokens/second under load, that is 7.2 M tokens/hour, so ≈ $0.28 per million output tokens. Halve the throughput with poor batching and the cost doubles; a fleet at 20 % utilisation costs five times what the arithmetic suggests.

Utilisation, not the sticker price, is the cost story. Committing to real numbers with stated assumptions is far stronger than "it depends" — and you invite them to correct the assumption rather than the reasoning.

Continuous batchingNew requests join the running batch at the next decode step and finished ones leave immediately, instead of the whole batch waiting for its slowest memberScheduler complexity; latency variance under load

The trap: "just autoscale it"

GPU replicas do not scale like stateless web servers. Pulling a multi-gigabyte image, loading 26 GB of weights and warming the engine is minutes, and the capacity may not even exist when you ask for it. So the real answer is a layered one: admission control and queueing absorb the first burst, a warm pool holds pre-loaded spare replicas, predictive scaling uses known traffic shape rather than reactive CPU metrics, and graceful degradation — shed batch traffic first, then shorten max output length, then route to a smaller model — keeps the interactive product alive. Say what you shed, in what order: that is the part that shows you have operated one of these.

Multi-tenancy and safety

  • Quotas in tokens, not requests. One request can be a thousand times another. Meter and bill in prompt and output tokens per tenant.
  • Fair queueing. Weighted round-robin across tenants so a batch job cannot starve interactive traffic, plus per-tenant concurrency caps.
  • Hard limits. Max prompt length, max output length, stop sequences and a per-request timeout — an unbounded generation is a resource leak with a friendly interface.
  • Isolation. Prefix caching across tenants is a real leak vector if cache keys are not tenant-scoped; scope them and say so.
  • Guardrails. Input and output classifiers run on the small-model pool so they do not consume the expensive fleet, and their latency is part of the budget.

Rolling out a model or engine change. The output is text, so a classic accuracy canary does not apply — you need three gates. First, a fixed prompt suite replayed against old and new, comparing outputs for exact-match on deterministic settings and a judge score otherwise. Second, a shadow pass on live traffic measuring latency and throughput only. Third, a real canary at 1/5/25/100 % watching TTFT, error rate and any product-level feedback signal. A quantisation change in particular must be gated on the prompt suite, because the degradation is quality-shaped, not latency-shaped, and will not show up in any system metric.
