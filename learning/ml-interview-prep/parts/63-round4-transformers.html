Part F · Interview rounds · Module 63

Round 4 · Transformers & LLMs The round the title is about. Expect architecture questions you must answer precisely, numbers you must be able to produce on demand, and at least one question designed to see whether you will overclaim.

How to run this round

60 minutes. Do the arithmetic questions on paper — being able to size a model or a KV cache in your head is a disproportionately strong signal, and it is pure practice. The last section is the one where the correct answer is sometimes "I do not know, and here is how I would find out".

Rapid fire — 20 seconds each

BASICWhat are Q, K and V?
A library lookup. The query is what this token is looking for, keys advertise what each token offers, values are what it actually contributes. Match query against keys, turn the matches into weights, take the weighted sum of values.
MIDWhy divide by √dk?
The dot product of two d-dimensional vectors with unit-variance entries has variance d, so the logits grow with dimension and the softmax saturates — one weight goes to 1 and the gradients vanish. Dividing by √d holds the variance at 1.
MIDWhat does the causal mask do, mechanically?
Sets the logits for future positions to −∞ before the softmax, so their weights are exactly zero. It lets every position be trained in parallel while each one still only sees its past.
MIDWhy multiple heads?
One softmax can only concentrate on one thing at a time. Multiple heads let the model attend to several relationships in parallel in different subspaces. The caveat: pruning studies show many heads are redundant, so "each head learns a clean role" is an overstatement.
MIDWhy does a transformer need positional information at all?
Attention is a weighted sum, which is permutation-equivariant — with no position signal, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce the same representations. Interesting subtlety: the causal mask alone leaks some position information, which is why NoPE models work.
SENIORWhat is RoPE's key property?
It rotates queries and keys by an angle proportional to position, so the resulting dot product depends only on the relative position m − n. Relative encoding with absolute-style implementation, and it extends via interpolation.
MIDPre-norm or post-norm, and why?
Pre-norm: normalise before the sublayer, so the residual path stays clean and gradients flow. It trains stably without warmup, which is why everything modern uses it. Post-norm can reach slightly better quality but needs careful warmup.
MIDWhat does the FFN do?
Position-wise processing: expand to ~4× width, apply a non-linearity, project back. It holds roughly two thirds of the parameters, and evidence suggests it functions as key-value memory storing factual associations.
MIDRMSNorm versus LayerNorm?
RMSNorm drops the mean-centring and the bias, scaling only by the root-mean-square. Cheaper, and empirically just as good — so it is what most recent models use.
SENIORRoughly how many parameters per transformer layer?
About 12d² — 4d² in attention (Q, K, V, O) and 8d² in a 4× FFN. So the model is roughly 12·L·d² plus embeddings. GQA reduces the attention share and SwiGLU's three matrices at ⅔ width keep the FFN share about the same.
MIDTraining and inference FLOPs, per token?
Training ≈ 6N per token (2 forward, 4 backward); inference ≈ 2N. So training on D tokens is about 6ND.
MIDWhat is the KV cache and how big is it?
Stored keys and values for previous tokens so decoding is O(n) rather than O(n²). Size = 2 × layers × tokens × kv-dim × bytes, per sequence — which for a 13B model at 4K context is around 3 GB, usually more binding than the weights.
MIDPrefill or decode — which is compute-bound?
Prefill is compute-bound because it processes all prompt tokens in parallel. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound: one token at a time, but the whole weight matrix must be read. That is why batching helps decode enormously.
SENIORIs FlashAttention an approximation?
No. It is an exact, IO-aware kernel that tiles the computation in SRAM so the n×n matrix is never written to HBM. Same mathematics, far less memory traffic.
MIDWhat does LoRA train?
A low-rank update ΔW = BA with rank r ≪ d, while W stays frozen — well under 1 % of the parameters. It can be merged into W afterwards, so inference latency is unchanged.
MIDChinchilla's rule?
For a fixed training budget, scale parameters and tokens equally — roughly 20 tokens per parameter. But if you will serve the model heavily, deliberately overtrain a smaller one, because inference cost dominates over its lifetime.
SENIORWhat does GRPO remove, and what replaces it?
The value network. It samples a group of responses per prompt and uses the group's mean reward as the baseline, which roughly halves the memory of a PPO setup and pairs naturally with verifiable rewards.

The arithmetic — do these on paper

Three sizing questions. Two minutes each.

1. A model has 32 layers and d = 4096. Roughly how many parameters?
2. You have 10²³ FLOPs. What is the Chinchilla-optimal model size and token count?
3. That 32-layer, d = 4096 model, MHA, fp16, 8K context. How much KV cache for one sequence? How many fit alongside the weights on an 80 GB card?

Deep questions — 5 minutes each

SENIORWalk me through what happens to one token from input to output.
Tokenised to an id, looked up in the embedding table, positional information applied, then through L identical blocks each adding attention output and FFN output into a residual stream, a final norm, and a projection to vocabulary logits which softmax into a distribution.

Concretely, following the word: BPE splits the text into subwords and each maps to an integer id. The id indexes the embedding matrix, giving a d-dimensional vector. Position is applied — as an added encoding, or in modern models as a rotation of Q and K inside each attention operation.

Then, per block: normalise a copy of the residual stream, project it to Q, K and V, score this token's query against all previous keys, scale by √d, mask the future, softmax, take the weighted sum of values, project back, and add to the stream. Then normalise again, expand through the FFN, contract, and add again. The stream is never overwritten — each block reads it and writes a correction back, which is the residual-stream view and the reason interpretability can decompose a model's output into per-block contributions.

At the end: a final norm, then a projection to vocabulary size — usually tied to the embedding matrix — giving one logit per token. Softmax turns those into a distribution, and the sampler picks from it.

The two things I would emphasise: attention is the only place tokens exchange information — everything else is position-wise — so the reach of the model is entirely determined by attention. And at inference all of this happens per generated token, with previous K and V read from the cache rather than recomputed, which is what makes generation linear rather than quadratic.

SENIORWhy did transformers replace RNNs? Give the real reason.
Parallel training. An RNN's sequential dependency means a sequence of length n takes n steps that cannot be parallelised; a transformer processes all positions at once. Combined with the constant-length gradient path, that made scale possible — and scale was the thing that mattered.

The mechanism: RNN training is O(n) sequential steps regardless of hardware, so a GPU sits mostly idle. A transformer's attention is a matrix multiplication over all positions — more total FLOPs at O(n²d) versus O(nd²), but perfectly parallel, and modern hardware is built for exactly that shape. Trading serial steps for parallel FLOPs was the winning trade.

The secondary reason: path length. Information between two distant tokens crosses O(n) transformations in an RNN, each attenuating the gradient; in a transformer it is O(1) — one attention hop. Long-range dependencies became learnable rather than merely representable.

The honest qualifier I would add: RNNs were not worse at everything. Inference memory is constant for an RNN and grows linearly for a transformer's KV cache, and per-token inference cost is lower. That is exactly why the state-space family — S4, Mamba, RWKV — is a live research direction: it attempts to recover parallel training via a scan formulation while keeping a constant-size state at inference. The trade transformers made was the right one for training at scale, and it is not free at serving time.

SENIORSomething you believe about LLMs that you cannot fully justify?
This is a calibration question, not a knowledge question. The point is whether you can distinguish what you know from what you assume — answer it honestly and name the evidence that would change your mind.

A defensible answer: "I believe most of what post-training does is elicitation rather than teaching — that the capability is already in the pretrained weights and SFT and preference tuning mainly make it reliably accessible. The evidence for it is suggestive: LIMA's results from a thousand examples, the fact that base models can do many tasks with the right prompt, and how little post-training compute changes so much behaviour. But it is not settled — RLVR on reasoning appears to produce genuinely new capability rather than only surfacing it, and I do not know how to cleanly separate those two claims experimentally."

Why this shape works: it states a position, gives the actual evidence, names the counter-evidence, and identifies what would resolve it. That is what a research engineer is being hired to do.

What to avoid: a non-answer ("I try to only believe what is proven"), a grandiose claim about consciousness or AGI, and — worst — an assertion delivered with more confidence than the evidence supports. The question is explicitly probing for overclaiming, and the whole value of the answer is in the caveat.
