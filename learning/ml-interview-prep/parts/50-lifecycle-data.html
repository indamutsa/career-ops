The sentence worth saying out loud in a system-design round

The model is maybe 5 % of the system. The other 95 % is data plumbing, and almost every production ML incident is a data incident — a schema change, a broken upstream job, a unit that switched from cents to euros — not a modelling one. Interviewers are listening for whether you know that.

Streaming, and why backpressure exists

Kafka mid

A distributed, durable, append-only log. Producers append to topics split into partitions; consumers read at their own pace and track an offset.

Not a queue that drains — a tape that keeps everything for its retention period. That is why you can replay last Tuesday to rebuild a feature, and why the same stream can feed the model, the warehouse and the audit trail independently.

Partition & key mid

The unit of parallelism. Ordering is guaranteed within a partition only, and records with the same key land in the same partition.

Key by card_id and every transaction for a card is ordered and handled by one consumer — which is exactly what stateful per-entity features need. Also the source of hot partitions when one key dominates.

Consumer lag mid

Latest offset minus committed offset: how far behind the consumer is, in records.

The single most important number to alert on in a streaming ML system. Rising lag means your "real-time" features are stale, and a fraud score computed on stale features is worse than no score.

Backpressure senior

The mechanism by which a slow consumer tells the system to stop sending faster than it can cope — rather than silently dropping data or running out of memory.

A queue at a coffee counter. The barista cannot serve faster, so either the queue grows (buffer), people are turned away (load shedding), or the door is held (blocking). What you must never do is accept orders you have no intention of making — that is an unbounded buffer, and it ends in an OOM kill with the data gone.

Delivery semantics senior

At-most-once — may lose. At-least-once — may duplicate. Exactly-once — neither, within a bounded system.

At-least-once plus idempotent processing is the standard production answer. "Exactly-once" is real inside Kafka's transactional boundary, but the moment you write to an external system it becomes at-least-once plus deduplication again.

Idempotency mid

Applying the same message twice has the same effect as applying it once.

Achieved with a deterministic key: upsert on event_id rather than append, or keep a seen-set with a TTL. This is what makes at-least-once safe, and it is the property to name when someone asks how you handle duplicates.

Event time vs processing time senior

When the thing happened, versus when your system saw it. They diverge, and events arrive out of order.

Windowed aggregations must use event time with a watermark and an allowed lateness, or a network hiccup silently changes your feature values. Computing "transactions in the last hour" on processing time is a bug that only appears under load.

CDC mid

Change Data Capture: stream a database's changes by reading its replication log rather than polling it.

How an operational database becomes an event stream without the application having to publish events, and without the polling query hammering production.

Data contract senior

An enforced, versioned agreement about a stream's schema, semantics, and quality guarantees — validated at the boundary, with a compatibility policy for changes.

The organisational fix for the most common ML outage: an upstream team renames a field or changes a unit, and your model degrades silently because nothing rejected the change.

Questions

SENIORWhat is backpressure, and how do you handle it?
It is the feedback signal from a slow consumer back to the producer. You handle it by choosing deliberately between buffering, slowing the producer, shedding load, or scaling out — and the one thing you never do is buffer without a bound.

Why it exists. Producers and consumers have independent rates. Without a feedback path, a faster producer fills memory until the process is killed and everything in flight is lost. Backpressure converts an unbounded, invisible failure into a bounded, visible one.

The four responses, and when each is right:

  • Buffer (bounded). Absorbs bursts. Correct when the overload is transient — but the bound is the entire point, and the buffer's depth is a latency cost you are choosing to pay.
  • Block / slow the producer. Correct when the producer can wait, e.g. a batch job. Wrong on a user-facing path, because the pressure propagates all the way to the user.
  • Shed load. Drop or sample the lowest-value work. Correct when partial service beats no service — and it demands a policy about what to drop. For fraud scoring I would shed the low-value transactions and always score the high-value ones.
  • Scale out. Add consumers. Real fix if the load is sustained, but bounded by partition count — you cannot have more useful consumers in a group than partitions, which is a capacity decision made months earlier.

With Kafka specifically, the log is the buffer: the consumer pulls, so a slow consumer just lags rather than overwhelming anyone, and lag is observable. That converts backpressure from a crash into a metric — which is a large part of why the architecture is log-based. The danger becomes silent staleness instead of loss, so lag alerting is mandatory, and retention must exceed your worst realistic outage or you lose data to the retention window while catching up.

The design point I would close on: decide the degradation policy up front, at design time, and write it down. Under overload the system will pick one of those four behaviours; the only question is whether you chose it or the OOM killer did.

MIDSynchronous or asynchronous scoring — how do you decide?
Synchronous when the decision blocks an action that cannot proceed without it — approving a payment. Asynchronous when the decision informs something that can happen later — a review queue, an alert, a batch. The question is whether the caller must wait for correctness.

Synchronous means the transaction waits for the score. It gets you a decision before the money moves, which for card authorisation is the whole product. The costs are severe and should be stated: you inherit a hard latency budget (typically tens of milliseconds end to end); the model is now on the critical path of revenue, so its availability is the payment system's availability; and you need a defined fallback for when the model is slow or down — a timeout with a rules-based default, and the business must agree in advance whether that default is approve or decline.

Asynchronous means the event is written to a stream and scored downstream. You get to spend more compute, use features that arrive late, batch for efficiency, and fail without taking payments down. The cost is that the action already happened — you are detecting, not preventing, and recovery becomes a chargeback rather than a block.

The architecture I would actually propose is both, which is what real fraud systems do: a fast synchronous path with cheap precomputed features and a small model inside the latency budget, plus an asynchronous path that rescores with expensive graph and sequence features, feeds a review queue, and updates the features the fast path will read next time. The synchronous path buys prevention; the asynchronous path buys accuracy; and the async path's outputs are what keep the sync path's features fresh.

The trap to avoid: putting a slow feature lookup — an external API, a graph traversal — inside the synchronous path. Every synchronous dependency's latency and failure rate become yours. Precompute into a fast store (module 51) and let the sync path do lookups only.

SENIORHow do you guarantee correctness with at-least-once delivery?
Make processing idempotent so duplicates are harmless, rather than trying to eliminate them. Deterministic keys, upserts instead of appends, and a dedup window on the consumer.

Why at-least-once is the realistic target. A consumer that commits its offset before processing can lose messages on a crash; one that commits after can reprocess them. Since losing a transaction is worse than counting one twice, you choose commit-after and accept duplicates. Everything then rests on making duplicates a non-event.

How, concretely:

  • Deterministic ids. Derive the output key from the input — a natural transaction_id, or a hash of the payload — never a generated UUID or a timestamp, which make a reprocessed message look new.
  • Upsert, don't append. "Set the score for transaction X" is idempotent; "insert a score row" is not. Counters are the classic non-idempotent operation — keep the contributing set and count it, or use a mergeable sketch, rather than incrementing.
  • Dedup window. A seen-set of ids with a TTL longer than your worst replay window, sized against realistic retry behaviour.
  • Idempotent side effects. The hard part is external: sending an email twice or blocking a card twice is a real incident. Guard those with a dedup key at the boundary, and treat the side-effect layer as the only place that needs strong guarantees.

On "exactly-once": Kafka's transactions do give it for read-process-write within Kafka, by writing offsets and outputs in one atomic transaction. It is genuine, and it is bounded — the moment a side effect leaves that boundary you are back to at-least-once plus idempotency. So I treat exactly-once as a useful local property, not as an excuse to skip idempotent design.

And the ML-specific version of this: reprocessing must be safe, because you will replay the stream — to backfill a feature, to fix a bug, to rebuild after an outage. A pipeline that cannot be replayed without corrupting its own outputs is a pipeline you can never fix. Replayability is a feature, and idempotency is what buys it.

MIDAn upstream team changes a field and your model degrades. How do you prevent it?
Validate at the boundary and fail loudly: an enforced schema with a compatibility policy, statistical checks on the values as well as the types, and monitoring on the inputs rather than only on the outputs.

Why it is so damaging. A model does not crash on bad input — it produces plausible numbers from wrong data. A currency field that switches from cents to euros makes every amount 100× smaller, the model still scores confidently, and nothing alerts until someone notices the approval rate weeks later. Silent is the operative word.

The layered defence:

  • Schema enforcement at ingestion — a registry with an explicit compatibility policy, so a breaking change is rejected at publish time rather than discovered at inference time.
  • Semantic validation. Types are not enough; the cents/euros bug is type-valid. Assert ranges, units, null rates, and cardinalities.
  • Input distribution monitoring — PSI per feature (module 54). This is what catches the change that passes every static check, and it is the reason drift monitoring earns its keep even before concept drift matters.
  • A data contract with the producing team: an owner, a version, a deprecation window, and their tests running in their CI. The technical controls detect; the contract prevents.
  • Fail closed on the features that matter. If a critical feature is missing or out of range, fall back to a rules-based decision and alert — do not impute a median and score anyway, which converts a visible outage into an invisible one.

The organisational point I would make: the upstream team is usually not doing anything wrong — they own a service, not your model, and they have no way of knowing you depend on that field. The fix is to make the dependency explicit and testable on their side, which is exactly what a data contract is for. Blaming them scales to zero; a contract with a test in their pipeline scales.
