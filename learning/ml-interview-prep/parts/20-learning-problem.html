Part B · Classical ML · Module 20

The learning problem Before any algorithm: what does it actually mean for a machine to learn, why does a model that scores 99 % in your notebook fail in production, and what is the discipline that prevents it. This module is the one that separates people who can train a model from people who can be trusted to ship one.

What learning is — and the one thing it is not

The founding idea

A child learns "dog" from maybe thirty dogs, then correctly calls a breed they have never seen a dog. They did not memorise thirty dogs. They extracted what makes a dog a dog.

Machine learning is that, and its central failure mode is that too: a system that memorised the thirty and calls the thirty-first a cat. We have a name for the difference — generalisation — and essentially every technique in this part exists to buy more of it.

Formally: you assume there is some unknown true function f mapping inputs to outputs. You get a finite, noisy sample of its behaviour. You search a space of candidate functions for one that agrees with your sample, and then you hope it agrees with f everywhere else. That hope has a name too — the inductive bias — and it is an assumption you make, not something the data gives you.

Why this framing pays off in interviews

Almost every "why did this fail in production" question reduces to one of three things: the sample was not representative of what you deployed on, the hypothesis space was too big or too small for the amount of data, or something told you the answer during training that will not be there at serving time. Learn to sort symptoms into those three buckets and you will diagnose faster than people who know more algorithms than you.

The terms

Supervised learning basic

Learning from labelled examples — inputs with the right answer attached.

Flashcards with the answer on the back.

Fraud detection, credit scoring, click prediction. Almost everything with business value, because the label is the business outcome.

Unsupervised learning basic

Finding structure with no labels at all — clusters, low-dimensional structure, outliers.

Sorting a drawer of unlabelled screws into piles by how they look.

Self-supervised learning mid

Manufacture labels from the data's own structure: hide part of the input, predict it from the rest. Unsupervised in the sense that nobody labelled anything; supervised in its mechanics.

This is how every LLM is trained. "Predict the next token" is a supervised problem whose labels the corpus supplies for free — which is precisely why it scales to trillions of tokens when human labelling does not.

Reinforcement learning mid

Learning from consequences rather than answers. No one tells you the right move; you find out later whether things went well.

Learning to cook without a recipe, judging only by whether people ate it.

Training / validation / test basic

Fit on train. Make decisions with validation. Touch test exactly once, at the end, to estimate what you will see in production.

Homework, mock exam, real exam. Sitting the real exam twenty times and reporting your best score is not an estimate of anything.

Generalisation gap basic

Training performance minus held-out performance. A big gap means memorisation.

Capacity / hypothesis space mid

How many different functions your model could represent. A straight line: tiny. A depth-20 tree: enormous.

A vocabulary. Too few words and you cannot express the idea; too many and you can describe noise as fluently as signal.

Inductive bias mid

The assumptions baked into the model that let it prefer one fitting function over another.

CNNs assume nearby pixels relate and that a cat is a cat anywhere in the frame. Transformers assume much less, which is exactly why they need much more data — and why they overtake CNNs only past a certain scale.

Underfitting / overfitting basic

Too simple to capture the pattern / complex enough to have captured the noise as well.

Underfit: "everything is a dog." Overfit: "a dog is an animal with exactly these 1,247 pixels."

Bias & variance mid

Bias is being consistently wrong in the same way. Variance is giving wildly different answers depending on which data you happened to train on.

A rifle sighted 10 cm left shoots a tight group in the wrong place — bias. A shaky shooter with correct sights scatters around the target — variance.

Regularisation mid

Anything that deliberately makes fitting the training data harder, in exchange for better held-out performance. L1/L2, dropout, early stopping, data augmentation, smaller models.

Module 14 showed what it really is: a Lagrange multiplier on a complexity budget.

Cross-validation basic

Split k ways, train on k−1 and validate on the held-out fold, rotate, average. Uses all your data for both jobs.

Essential under ~50k rows. Usually too expensive, and unnecessary, above that.

Data leakage senior

Information reaching the model during training that will not be available — or would not legitimately be known — at prediction time.

Studying with an answer key accidentally photocopied onto the back of the exam paper. You look brilliant right up until the real exam.

The single most common reason an excellent offline model is worthless in production. Section 20.6 is entirely about it.

Curse of dimensionality mid

As you add features, the space grows exponentially, your data becomes desperately sparse within it, and distances between points converge until "nearest" stops meaning anything.

Why kNN degrades in high dimensions and why embeddings and PCA exist.

No free lunch mid

Averaged over all possible problems, every algorithm performs identically. There is no universally best model.

The practical reading: an algorithm wins on your problem because its assumptions happen to match your data. That is why GBDT dominates tabular data and transformers dominate sequences — and why "which model is best?" is not a well-formed question until you say what the data looks like.

Splits — the discipline, not the syntax

Everyone can call train_test_split. The interview is about the judgment around it.

SetWhat it is forHow often you may look
TrainFitting parametersConstantly
ValidationEvery decision: hyperparameters, architecture, features, threshold, early stoppingOften — but each look spends a little of its trustworthiness
TestOne unbiased estimate of production performanceOnce. Ideally at the very end, ideally by someone else
The test set is a consumable

Each time you look at the test set and change something because of what you saw, you have turned it into a validation set and you no longer have an unbiased estimate. Try fifty configurations against it and the best of the fifty is optimistically biased by roughly the spread of the noise — you have simply run a search for the configuration that best fits your test set's particular quirks. This is the mechanism behind an entire literature of benchmark overfitting, and it is why serious teams hold out a final set that is not touched until sign-off.

Then the part that trips people: a random split is only valid if your data really is i.i.d. Three situations where it is not, and each has a specific fix:

Structure in the dataWhat a random split does wrongCorrect split
Time (fraud, demand, anything with a trend)Trains on the future, tests on the past. Reports an accuracy you can never achieve liveTemporal / walk-forward: train on everything before date D, test after
Groups (multiple rows per user, per patient, per merchant)The same user appears in both sets, so the model recognises the user rather than the pattern Grouped split: every row for a given user goes entirely to one side
Severe imbalance (0.1 % fraud)A fold may contain almost no positives, making the metric pure noiseStratified: preserve the class ratio in every fold

Bias, variance, and the trade-off that is not always a trade-off

Expected error decomposes into three pieces, and only two are yours:

error = bias² + variance + irreducible noise the third term is a property of the world; no model reduces it

The classical picture: as capacity grows, bias falls and variance rises, so total error is a U-curve with a sweet spot in the middle. Everything from k in kNN to tree depth to regularisation strength is a knob that moves you along that curve.

The diagnosis is mechanical and worth memorising as a rule:

Train errorValidation errorDiagnosisDo this
HighHigh, close to trainUnderfitting — high biasBigger model, more features, less regularisation, train longer
LowMuch higherOverfitting — high varianceMore data, more regularisation, simpler model, augmentation, early stopping
LowLowWorkingCheck for leakage before celebrating
HighLower than trainSomething is offUsually dropout/augmentation active in training but not in eval — see the questions
The modern caveat you must know

The U-curve is not the whole story any more. Push capacity far past the interpolation point — where the model can fit the training data exactly — and test error frequently falls again. This is double descent, and it is why a model with more parameters than training examples can generalise well, which the classical picture says is impossible. It is the single biggest reason bias–variance is now taught as a useful mental model rather than a law. If you recite the U-curve without mentioning this, a senior interviewer will notice.

Choosing a validation scheme

  1. Ask what the model will be doing at prediction time. Predicting the future from the past? Then validation must do the same. Scoring users it has never seen? Then no user may appear in both sets.
  2. Make the validation split imitate that exactly. This one principle generates every rule in the table above; you never need to memorise the rules themselves.
  3. Then pick the mechanics. Under ~50k rows, k-fold (5 or 10) for a stable estimate. Above that, a single large held-out split is fine and far cheaper. Time series: walk-forward with an expanding or rolling window, never shuffled k-fold.
  4. Add a gap if labels arrive late. If a fraud chargeback takes 60 days to materialise, then at serving time your most recent 60 days are unlabelled — so leave a 60-day gap between the end of training and the start of validation. Otherwise you are validating a model that has fresher information than the deployed one ever will.

Leakage — the taxonomy

If you take one thing from this module, take this section. Leakage is what makes a 0.99 AUC turn into a 0.62 in production, and the interview question is almost always "give me an example and tell me how you caught it".

KindWhat it looks likeConcrete example
Target leakageA feature that is a consequence of the label, not a causeaccount_frozen in a fraud model. It is set because fraud was detected. AUC 0.99 offline, useless live — at scoring time it is always false
Train–test contaminationThe same information in both splits Deduplication run after splitting; a user with 40 transactions spread across both sides
Temporal leakageFuture information in a past row A "customer lifetime value" feature computed over the whole dataset, including after the transaction being scored
Preprocessing leakageFitting a transform on all the data before splittingA scaler, imputer, target encoder or feature selector fitted on train+test. The test mean has quietly entered the model
Feature-store leakageServing the current value of a feature rather than its value as of the eventThe classic point-in-time-correctness failure — module 51 is entirely about preventing this
The one test that catches most of it

For every feature ask: "at the exact moment of prediction, in production, would this value be knowable — and would it hold this value?" If it needs a database write that happens after the event, it leaks. If it needs an aggregate over rows that do not exist yet, it leaks. That question catches target, temporal and feature-store leakage in one pass.

Questions

BASICExplain overfitting to a non-technical stakeholder.
The model memorised the practice questions instead of learning the subject, so it aces the practice test and fails the real one.

Concretely: it has enough flexibility to fit not just the real pattern in your historical data but also the accidents in it — the coincidences that will not repeat. Those coincidences are noise, and fitting them actively hurts on anything new.

How I detect it: a large gap between training and held-out performance. How I fix it, roughly in order of what I would try: more data if it is available, since it is the only fix with no downside; then regularisation, a simpler model, early stopping, or augmentation.

And I would add the business version, because it is what the stakeholder actually needs to hear: this is why I will not quote you the training accuracy. The only number that means anything is performance on data the model has never seen.

MIDExplain the bias–variance trade-off, and is it still true?
Expected error is bias² + variance + irreducible noise. Bias is being consistently wrong; variance is being unstable across datasets. Classically, capacity trades one for the other — but double descent shows that picture breaks down in the heavily over-parameterised regime.

The classical story: a too-simple model cannot represent the truth, so it is wrong in the same way everywhere — high bias. A too-flexible model chases the noise, so a different sample would have given a very different model — high variance. Total error is U-shaped in capacity.

How I use it day to day is as a diagnostic, not a theory: train error high and validation error close to it means bias, so I add capacity; a large gap means variance, so I add data or regularisation. That single fork determines what I try next, which is the whole practical value of the decomposition.

Where it stops being true: past the interpolation threshold — where the model can fit the training set exactly — test error often decreases again as you keep growing the model. Modern deep networks live entirely in that regime. So I treat bias–variance as a very good mental model for classical models and small data, and as a rough guide rather than a law for large networks.

Trap

"So more parameters is always better?" No — double descent needs the interpolation regime plus implicit regularisation from the optimiser and the architecture. A 10-million-parameter model on 500 rows of tabular data will still overfit spectacularly.

SENIORGive me a real example of data leakage and how you caught it.
The tell is a metric that is too good. My rule is that an unexpectedly excellent result is a bug report until proven otherwise, and the first thing I check is feature importance for something that could only be known after the fact.

The canonical shape, using fraud since it is what this course builds toward: a model reaches 0.99 AUC. Top feature by a mile is something like account_status. But accounts get frozen because fraud was confirmed — that field is downstream of the label. At scoring time, milliseconds after the transaction arrives, it is always the default value. The offline model was reading the answer.

How I catch it, as a routine rather than as luck:

  • Suspicion threshold on the metric. If AUC jumps above what the problem plausibly supports, I investigate before I celebrate.
  • Look at feature importance first, not last. One feature carrying most of the signal is the classic signature.
  • For every top feature, ask the timing question: would this value exist, and hold this value, at the instant of prediction?
  • Ablate it. If removing one feature collapses performance to something ordinary, that feature was doing something suspicious.
  • Validate temporally, not randomly. Temporal leakage frequently survives a random split and dies immediately under a walk-forward one.
  • Fit every transform inside the pipeline, so it can only ever see the training fold. This eliminates the whole preprocessing category structurally rather than by vigilance.

The structural fix that matters most in a real system is a feature store with point-in-time joins, so training features are reconstructed as of the event timestamp rather than as of today. That is the difference between preventing leakage and repeatedly noticing it.

Trap

The follow-up is usually "how would you prevent it rather than detect it?" The answer is process, not cleverness: transforms inside the pipeline, point-in-time feature retrieval, a temporal holdout as the default, and an offline-vs-online metric comparison after launch, since a large discrepancy is leakage announcing itself.

MIDYour validation accuracy is higher than your training accuracy. What is going on?
Almost always an artefact of how the two numbers were measured, not a real phenomenon — regularisation is active during training and off during evaluation.

Causes in the order I would check them:

  • Dropout and augmentation. Training runs with dropout on and augmented inputs; evaluation runs clean. The model is genuinely solving a harder problem during training. This explains most cases and is not a bug.
  • When the metric is computed. Training loss is often averaged across the epoch while the model is still improving; validation is computed at the end. The training number is an average over a worse model.
  • An easier validation set. With a small or unlucky split, the validation fold can simply be less difficult. Check by rotating folds — if it moves around a lot, it is noise.
  • Leakage into validation. Rarer, but this is the direction where a duplicated or contaminated validation set shows up.

So: mildly higher and stable is expected and I would move on. Dramatically higher, or the gap growing over training, and I would look at the split itself.

MIDHow do you choose a cross-validation scheme?
Make the split imitate the prediction task. Whatever the model will not know at serving time, validation must not know either.
  • Independent rows, plenty of data → a single stratified held-out split. Cheap and sufficient.
  • Small data → 5- or 10-fold, stratified if classes are imbalanced. Repeated CV if the variance across folds is large.
  • Time matters → walk-forward with expanding or rolling windows, plus a gap equal to the label delay. Never shuffle.
  • Repeated entities → group k-fold on the entity id, so no user or merchant appears on both sides.
  • Both time and groups → combine: group by entity, split by time, gap for the label delay. This is the fraud case, and it is the one worth describing in detail because it is where people simplify and get it wrong.
  • Nested CV when you are both tuning hyperparameters and reporting a performance estimate — otherwise the reported number is contaminated by the tuning.

The sanity check I apply at the end: if my validation setup would have been impossible to run in real time on the day of prediction, it is wrong regardless of what number it produces.

Trap

Leave-one-out CV sounds maximally rigorous. It is nearly unbiased but has high variance and costs n fits, and for many models it is dominated by 10-fold. Do not reach for it reflexively.

SENIORWhat is the curse of dimensionality, and why do embeddings not suffer from it?
In high dimensions, volume grows exponentially, data becomes sparse, and all pairwise distances converge — so "nearest neighbour" stops carrying information. Embeddings escape it because their dimensions are learned and dense, so the data occupies a low-dimensional manifold inside the space rather than being scattered through all of it.

The arithmetic that makes it vivid: to cover a unit interval at 0.1 resolution you need 10 points. In 10 dimensions, 10¹⁰. In 100, more points than there are atoms available. Any real dataset in a high-dimensional space is overwhelmingly empty space.

The consequence that breaks algorithms: the ratio of the nearest to the farthest distance tends to 1 as dimension grows. So kNN, k-means, and anything else built on a distance are measuring something that no longer discriminates.

Why embeddings are different: a 768-dimensional embedding is not 768 independent coordinates that data was sprinkled across. It was trained so that semantically related things sit near each other, and real data concentrates on a much lower-dimensional manifold within that space — the manifold hypothesis. The effective dimension is far below the nominal one, which is exactly why cosine similarity in 768 dimensions works while Euclidean distance over 768 raw hand-built features does not.

Practical implications I would mention: prefer learned dense representations over wide sparse ones; use dimensionality reduction before distance-based methods; and note that this is also why approximate nearest-neighbour indexes exist — exact search in high dimensions degenerates toward a linear scan.

MIDHow much data do you need?
There is no universal number — it depends on the signal-to-noise ratio, the number of features, the model's capacity, and how much error you can tolerate. What I do instead of guessing is plot a learning curve.

The learning curve settles the question empirically: train on 10 %, 25 %, 50 %, 100 % of what you have and plot held-out performance against training-set size. If it is still climbing steeply at 100 %, more data is your highest-return investment. If it has flattened, more data of the same kind will not help and you need better features, a different model, or a better-posed problem. That single plot redirects a lot of wasted effort, and saying it is usually a better answer than any rule of thumb.

Rough starting anchors, offered as anchors and not as rules: tabular problems with tens of features often work from a few thousand rows; classical text classification wants thousands per class; training a vision model from scratch wants tens of thousands per class, and fine-tuning a pretrained one wants hundreds; fine-tuning an LLM for style or format can work with a few hundred well-chosen examples, because you are steering an existing capability rather than creating one.

The point underneath all of them: what varies is how much of the work is already done. Every "we need less data now" story of the last decade is a transfer-learning story.
