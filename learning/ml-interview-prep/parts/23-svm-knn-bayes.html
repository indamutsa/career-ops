Part B · Classical ML · Module 23

SVM, kNN & Naive Bayes Three classics that are rarely the model you ship, and are asked about constantly — because each one is a clean test of something deeper. The SVM is where the Lagrange machinery from module 14 pays off. kNN is the ancestor of every vector database. Naive Bayes is the cleanest example of a wrong assumption that works anyway.

SVM: draw the line with the most room on both sides

The founding idea

Two groups of people are standing in a field and you must paint a line between them. Infinitely many lines separate them. Which is best?

The one with the widest empty corridor on either side. If someone new arrives and stands slightly off from their group, a line squeezed up against one side misclassifies them; a line down the middle of the widest corridor still gets it right. That corridor is the margin, and maximising it is the entire idea of a support vector machine.

Notice what that gives you: a principled reason to prefer one separating boundary over another, derived from robustness to new points rather than from fit to the ones you have. That is a generalisation argument built into the objective, which is why SVMs were the dominant method for a decade and why the idea is still worth knowing.

The terms

Hyperplane basic w·x + b = 0

The flat dividing surface. A point in 1D, a line in 2D, a plane in 3D, and the same equation in any dimension.

Margin basic

The width of the empty corridor between the boundary and the nearest point of either class. SVM maximises it.

Maximising the margin turns out to be minimising ‖w‖², which is why the SVM objective looks like a regulariser — it is one.

Support vector mid

A training point sitting exactly on the margin boundary (or inside it). These are the only points that define the model.

The corridor is held open by the people standing right at its edges. Everyone standing further back could go home and the corridor would not move.

Hard vs soft margin mid

Hard: no point may cross the margin — only possible if the data is perfectly separable. Soft: allow violations, at a cost.

Every real SVM is soft-margin. Real data has overlap and mislabels.

Hinge loss mid max(0, 1 − y·f(x))

Zero once a point is correctly classified with margin to spare; grows linearly otherwise.

A teacher who gives full marks for anything comfortably right and stops caring how right it was. Log loss keeps rewarding extra confidence; hinge does not.

That flat region is the source of the sparsity — points in it contribute no gradient at all.

C mid

The penalty for margin violations. Large C: fit the training data hard, narrow margin, overfit. Small C: tolerate mistakes, wide margin, underfit.

C is inversely a regularisation strength — small C is more regularised. People get the direction backwards constantly.

Kernel trick senior

Compute similarities as if you had projected the data into a huge (sometimes infinite) feature space, without ever constructing the projection.

You want to know whether two recipes taste similar. You could list every chemical compound in each and compare — or you could just taste them. The kernel is the tasting.

RBF kernel mid exp(−γ‖x−x′‖²)

Similarity that decays with distance. γ sets how fast: large γ means only very close points count.

The default kernel. Corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature space, which is the standard "how can that possibly work" moment — and the answer is that you never build it.

kNN basic

To classify a point, look at its k nearest neighbours in the training set and take a vote. There is no training step at all.

Judging a neighbourhood by the houses immediately around it.

Lazy learning mid

All the work happens at prediction time, not training time.

The trade you are making: instant "training", expensive inference, and the whole training set must be kept in memory. That is a bad trade for a classifier and an excellent one for retrieval, which is why kNN survives as the core of vector search.

Naive Bayes basic

Apply Bayes' theorem while pretending every feature is independent of the others given the class.

Judging a restaurant by scoring the food, the service and the price completely separately, as though good food told you nothing about likely service.

Laplace / additive smoothing mid

Add a small count to every category so nothing has probability exactly zero.

Without it, one unseen word makes the entire product zero and the class becomes impossible regardless of all other evidence. A one-line fix for a catastrophic failure.

Generative vs discriminative senior

Generative models learn P(x, y) — how the data itself is produced. Discriminative models learn P(y | x) — only the boundary.

Naive Bayes is generative, logistic regression is discriminative, and they are a matched pair: same functional form, different fitting criterion. An LLM is generative in exactly this sense — it models the distribution of the data.

The dual — where module 14 pays off

The SVM problem is: minimise ‖w‖² subject to every point being on the correct side by a margin of at least 1. That is a constrained optimisation with one inequality constraint per training point — exactly the setup Lagrange was built for.

Attach a multiplier αᵢ ≥ 0 to each constraint, form the Lagrangian, and eliminate w. Two things fall out, and both are consequential:

  1. Complementary slackness gives you sparsity for free. The KKT condition αᵢ·(constraintᵢ) = 0 says each point either sits exactly on the margin, with αᵢ > 0, or lies comfortably beyond it, with αᵢ = 0. Only the first group contributes. That is what a support vector is — a point with a non-zero Lagrange multiplier. Delete every other training point and refit: identical model.
  2. The data appears only inside dot products. In the dual, training and prediction touch the inputs exclusively through xᵢ·xⱼ. Never individually. Which means...
The kernel trick, in one move

If the data only ever appears as a dot product, replace the dot product with any function that behaves like one — K(xᵢ, xⱼ). You are now implicitly working in whatever feature space that kernel corresponds to, at the cost of computing K instead of a dot product. With an RBF kernel that space is infinite-dimensional, and you never build a single coordinate of it.

None of this is possible in the primal, where w lives in the feature space explicitly and would therefore have to be infinite. The dual is what makes the kernel trick expressible. That is the connection the question is really testing.

The condition on K is Mercer's: it must correspond to a positive semi-definite kernel matrix, which is precisely the guarantee that some feature space exists for it to be a dot product in. In practice you pick from the standard menu — linear, polynomial, RBF — rather than inventing kernels.

kNN — dead as a classifier, everywhere as retrieval

Nobody ships kNN as a fraud classifier: inference costs a full scan of the training set, it needs all the data resident in memory, and the curse of dimensionality from module 20 erodes the meaning of "nearest" as features multiply.

And yet the algorithm is running in essentially every AI product shipped in the last three years, because RAG is kNN. Embed your documents, embed the query, retrieve the k nearest neighbours by cosine similarity, put them in the prompt. What changed is not the algorithm but its two inputs:

  • The space became learned. Distances between raw hand-built features are close to meaningless in high dimensions. Distances between trained embeddings are meaningful, because the training objective was precisely to make them so. The curse of dimensionality was never a curse on dimensions — it was a curse on uninformative dimensions.
  • The search became approximate. Exact nearest-neighbour search degenerates to a linear scan in high dimensions. HNSW, IVF and product quantisation give you 95–99 % recall at a fraction of the cost, and that trade is what makes billion-vector indexes practical. Module 46 covers the index structures.

Practical points that get asked: always scale your features before any distance-based method, since a feature in euros dominates one in years for no good reason; use distance-weighted voting so a neighbour at 0.1 counts more than one at 0.9; pick k by cross-validation, keep it odd for binary classification to avoid ties, and remember that k = 1 has zero training error and enormous variance — it is the purest illustration of overfitting in all of machine learning.

Naive Bayes — wrong, and useful anyway

The assumption is plainly false. In a spam classifier it says that seeing "free" tells you nothing about the chance of seeing "money" — in an email you already know is spam. Obviously untrue. So why does it work?

Because classification only needs the argmax to be right, not the probabilities. The independence assumption makes the estimated probabilities badly wrong — usually pushed to near 0 or 1, because correlated evidence is counted repeatedly as though it were independent. But it tends to push them wrong in a way that preserves the ordering between classes. You get the right answer with an absurd confidence attached to it.

Which sets its boundary exactly: use Naive Bayes when you need a decision and a fast, tiny model trained on very little data. Never use its output as a probability. If a threshold from a cost matrix depends on that number, this is the wrong model — or it needs calibration bolted on, and at that point logistic regression is a better starting point.

Questions

BASICExplain an SVM without any maths.
Of all the lines that separate the two groups, it picks the one with the widest empty gap on either side — because that line is the one most likely to still be right about a point it has not seen.

The important consequence is that only the points nearest the boundary matter. They are the ones holding the gap open, so they are called support vectors. Everything far from the boundary could be deleted and you would get the same model.

Two additions if there is room: real data is not cleanly separable, so we allow some points to sit inside the gap and pay a penalty — that penalty is the C parameter. And when the groups cannot be separated by a straight line at all, the kernel trick measures similarity in a way that behaves as though we had bent the space until they could be.

SENIORWhat is a support vector, formally — and why does the SVM only depend on a few points?
A support vector is a training point whose Lagrange multiplier is non-zero. The sparsity is not a design choice — it falls out of the KKT complementary slackness condition.

Setting it up: minimising ‖w‖² subject to yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) ≥ 1 for every i gives one inequality constraint per training point, so one multiplier αᵢ per point. Complementary slackness requires αᵢ · [yᵢ(w·xᵢ + b) − 1] = 0 for every i. So for each point, either the bracket is zero — the point sits exactly on the margin — or αᵢ = 0.

Since the solution is w = Σ αᵢ yᵢ xᵢ, every point with αᵢ = 0 contributes literally nothing to w. The model is a weighted combination of a handful of boundary points, and everything else is inert.

Why this is worth knowing beyond SVMs: it is the cleanest example of a constraint structure producing sparsity, and the same argument explains why the hinge loss is flat for comfortably-correct points — the flat region is where the gradient, and hence the multiplier, is zero. It is also the practical reason SVM inference cost scales with the number of support vectors rather than the dataset size, which for a well-separated problem can be a very small fraction of it.

Trap

With a soft margin, points inside the margin and misclassified points are also support vectors, with α capped at C. So a heavily overlapping dataset with a large C can end up with most of the training set as support vectors — at which point you have lost the sparsity and inference is slow. That is a real symptom of a badly tuned SVM.

SENIORExplain the kernel trick, and why the dual formulation is what makes it possible.
In the dual, the training data appears only inside dot products. So you can replace the dot product with any valid kernel, and you are implicitly working in that kernel's feature space without ever constructing it.

The problem it solves: data that is not linearly separable in its original space usually is separable if you lift it into a richer one — add squared terms, products of pairs, and so on. But that space explodes. A degree-3 polynomial expansion of 1,000 features is on the order of 10⁹ dimensions, and an RBF's implicit space is infinite. You cannot write down a weight vector there.

The resolution: you do not need to. In the dual, both the training objective and the prediction rule touch the inputs only as xᵢ·xⱼ. Swap in K(xᵢ, xⱼ) — for the polynomial kernel, (xᵢ·xⱼ + c)ᵈ, which costs one dot product and one power regardless of d. You have computed a dot product in a billion-dimensional space in the time it takes to do one in the original.

Why the dual specifically: in the primal, w lives in the feature space and would have to be infinite-dimensional to write down. In the dual, the parameters are the αᵢ — one per training point, always finite, regardless of how large the implicit feature space is. The dual trades a problem whose size is the dimension for one whose size is the number of samples, and that is what makes the whole thing expressible.

The cost, which is the natural follow-up: the dual needs the n×n kernel matrix, so it scales quadratically in memory and roughly quadratically-to-cubically in time. That is exactly why kernel SVMs stopped being used above roughly 100k rows, and why linear SVMs solved in the primal took over for large sparse problems like text.

Trap

"So kernels are free extra capacity?" No. RBF with a large γ can memorise the training set completely — infinite capacity means infinite overfitting potential. γ and C must be tuned together, and this is why kernel SVMs have a reputation for being fiddly.

MIDWhat do C and γ do in an RBF SVM, and how do they interact?
γ controls how far a single training point's influence reaches; C controls how much you are willing to misclassify to keep a wide margin. Both increase capacity, so they must be tuned jointly.
  • Large γ — influence decays fast, so each point only affects its immediate neighbourhood. The boundary becomes wiggly and wraps around individual points. Overfits. In the limit it approaches a 1-nearest-neighbour classifier.
  • Small γ — influence spreads widely, the boundary smooths out, and in the limit it approaches a linear model. Underfits.
  • Large C — violations are expensive, so the model contorts to classify every training point. Narrow margin, overfits.
  • Small C — violations are cheap, wide margin, more points ignored. Underfits. Note the direction: small C is more regularised, which is the opposite convention to λ everywhere else and is worth saying explicitly.

They interact because both add effective capacity, so the good region is a diagonal band in the (C, γ) plane rather than a point — large γ with small C can behave similarly to small γ with large C. The standard practice is a joint grid or random search on a logarithmic scale, roughly 10⁻³ to 10³ for both, and it must be a joint search rather than tuning one and then the other.

The prerequisite people forget: standardise the features first. γ multiplies a squared Euclidean distance, so if one feature is in euros and another in years, the euro feature dominates the kernel entirely and no value of γ fixes it.

MIDWhy is Naive Bayes "naive", and why does it work anyway?
Naive because it assumes all features are conditionally independent given the class, which is essentially never true. It works because classification needs the argmax to be right, and the assumption corrupts the probabilities while usually preserving their order.

The assumption lets you turn P(x₁, x₂, …, xₙ | y) into a simple product of per-feature terms, each estimated from counts. That is what makes it trainable from tiny data in a single pass — and it is exactly the step that is wrong, because in a spam email "free" and "money" are strongly dependent.

Why it survives the error: correlated features are effectively counted several times, which drives the estimated posteriors toward 0 and 1. The magnitudes become nonsense. But the ranking between classes usually survives, and the ranking is all a classifier needs. There is a well-known analysis by Domingos and Pazzani making exactly this point.

The boundary, which is the useful part of the answer: use it when I need a decision quickly from very little data — a fast text baseline, a bootstrap classifier before real labels exist. Never use its output as a probability. Anything requiring calibrated scores, such as a cost-matrix threshold or an expected-value calculation downstream, needs a different model or a calibration layer.

Two implementation details worth mentioning unprompted: Laplace smoothing is mandatory, since one unseen token otherwise zeroes the entire product; and you always compute in log-space, because a product of thousands of small probabilities underflows — the same argument from module 13.

Trap

"Which variant?" Multinomial for word counts, Bernoulli for presence/absence, Gaussian for continuous features. Choosing Gaussian NB for count data is a common and quiet mistake.

SENIORGenerative versus discriminative — what is the real difference and when does it matter?
Generative models learn the joint P(x, y), so they model how the data itself was produced; discriminative models learn only P(y | x), the boundary. Discriminative usually wins on accuracy with enough data; generative wins with little data, and can do things a boundary cannot.

Naive Bayes and logistic regression are the textbook matched pair: with the same features they produce the same functional form of decision boundary, but fitting differs. NB estimates class priors and per-feature likelihoods by counting; LR directly optimises conditional likelihood.

The trade-off, from Ng and Jordan's analysis: the generative model has higher asymptotic error — its assumptions are wrong and no amount of data fixes that — but it approaches its ceiling much faster, so it wins in the small-data regime. The discriminative model has a lower ceiling and needs more data to get there. The learning curves cross. Which side of the crossing point you are on decides the choice.

What generative buys you beyond accuracy: you can sample new data from it, you get a natural handle on anomaly detection because a low P(x) means "unlike anything I was trained on", and you can handle missing features by marginalising them out rather than imputing. Those are real capabilities a discriminative model simply does not have.

And the modern connection worth drawing: an LLM is a generative model in exactly this sense — it models P(next token | context), and generation is sampling from it. A fine-tuned classifier head on top of it is discriminative. The reason pretraining works at all is the generative half: learning to model the whole distribution forces representations that the discriminative task can then reuse cheaply, which is the same small-data argument arriving at a much larger scale.

MIDWould you use kNN today, and how do you make it work at scale?
Not as a classifier — but as retrieval it is everywhere, because RAG and every vector database are kNN over learned embeddings with an approximate index.

Why not as a classifier: inference requires comparing against the whole training set, the whole training set has to be in memory, and in raw high-dimensional feature space the distances stop discriminating. A boosted tree beats it on tabular data on every axis that matters.

Why it dominates retrieval: the two things that were broken got fixed. The space is now learned, so distance actually encodes semantic similarity rather than an arbitrary mixture of units — the curse of dimensionality was really a curse on uninformative dimensions. And the search is now approximate: HNSW's navigable small-world graph, IVF's coarse partitioning into cells, product quantisation to compress the vectors. You accept 95–99 % recall in exchange for sublinear search, and for retrieval that trade is essentially free, because a lost result in position 9 changes nothing downstream.

If I were building it: normalise the embeddings so cosine and Euclidean agree, pick the index by scale — flat exact search is genuinely correct below a few hundred thousand vectors and people over-engineer this — tune HNSW's ef_search as the recall/latency knob, and measure recall against an exact brute-force run on a sample, because an approximate index that has silently degraded is invisible without that check.

The failure mode I would call out: with billions of vectors and a filter (tenant, language, date range), naive post-filtering after ANN retrieval returns far too few results, because the nearest neighbours globally may all be filtered out. Filtered ANN is a genuinely hard problem and a good thing to raise in a system-design round.
