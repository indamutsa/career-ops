Pretraining & scaling Where the capability actually comes from. For a research-engineer role this is the module that separates people who have read about LLMs from people who could plan a run: the data pipeline, the scaling laws, and what the laws do and do not predict.

The data pipeline — the part nobody publishes and everybody fights

Pretraining is one loss function and an enormous amount of data engineering. In rough order:

StageWhat happensWhy it matters
AcquisitionCommon Crawl, code, books, papers, curated corporaSets the ceiling on what the model can ever know
ExtractionHTML → text, boilerplate and navigation strippedCheap stage with outsized quality impact
Language ID + filteringKeep target languages, drop machine-generated junk Determines multilingual capability
DeduplicationExact and near-duplicate (MinHash/LSH) removal at document and paragraph levelThe single highest-value step: cuts memorisation, improves loss at equal compute

MixingWeight the sources; upsample code and high-quality proseCode in the mix measurably improves reasoning on non-code tasks
The claim worth being able to defend

At fixed compute, improving the data has produced larger gains in the last few years than improving the architecture. Deduplication, quality classifiers and better mixing account for much of the distance between a 2020 model and a 2024 model of the same size. If asked "how would you improve this model", data work is usually the correct first answer, and it is the one most candidates skip.

Scaling laws

Scaling law mid

Test loss falls as a power law in model size, dataset size and compute — a straight line on a log–log plot, over many orders of magnitude.

Not a discovery about intelligence; a discovered regularity, like a materials curve. It lets you predict a big run's loss from small runs, which is the only reason anyone dares spend nine figures on one.

Kaplan (2020) mid

Concluded that with extra compute you should mostly grow the model, and only mildly grow the data.

This is why GPT-3 was 175 B parameters trained on only 300 B tokens. The conclusion was an artefact of holding the learning-rate schedule fixed across run lengths.

Chinchilla (2022) senior

Redid the experiment with schedules tuned per run: parameters and tokens should scale equally. The rule of thumb is ≈ 20 tokens per parameter.

Chinchilla, at 70 B trained on 1.4 T tokens, beat Gopher's 280 B on 300 B tokens using the same compute. Four times smaller and better — the most consequential empirical correction in the field.

Compute-optimal vs inference-optimal senior

Chinchilla minimises loss for a fixed training budget. It says nothing about serving.

If a model will serve billions of requests, a smaller model trained far past 20 tokens/param is cheaper overall. Llama-3-8B saw 15 T tokens — roughly 1,875 per parameter, about 90× "optimal" — and that is a deliberate, correct trade.

The 6ND rule mid

Training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens. Inference ≈ 2 × parameters per token.

The 6 is 2 for the forward pass plus 4 for the backward pass. This one formula answers most planning questions asked in an interview.

Emergent abilities senior

Capabilities that appear absent below some scale and present above it.

Heavily contested — Schaeffer et al. argue most reported emergence is an artefact of discontinuous metrics like exact-match accuracy. See the question below; this is a favourite discriminating question.

Loss spike senior

A sudden divergence mid-run, sometimes unrecoverable.

Mitigations that are standard practice: gradient clipping, skipping the offending batch, rewinding to a checkpoint with different data order, z-loss on the output logits, and careful attention to fp16/bf16 numerics.

Questions

MIDWhat is a scaling law, and what is it good for?
An empirical power-law relationship between loss and compute, data or parameters, straight on a log–log plot. Its value is prediction: fit it on cheap small runs and forecast what a 100× larger run will achieve before committing the budget.

The form is L(x) = a·x−α + L∞ — loss falls as a power of the resource and asymptotes to an irreducible floor set by the entropy of language itself (module 13). It holds across roughly seven orders of magnitude, which is remarkable and not theoretically explained.

Why it changed how the field works: before scaling laws, "will a bigger model be better" was a bet. After them it is an extrapolation with error bars. GPT-4's report states its loss was predicted from runs using 10,000× less compute. That predictability is what made nine-figure training runs a defensible decision rather than a gamble.

The limits I would state without being asked: the laws predict pretraining loss, not downstream task performance, and the mapping from one to the other is loose. They say nothing about instruction-following, safety, or reasoning quality. And they assume a fixed data distribution — a law fitted on 2021 web text does not tell you what happens when you triple the code fraction.

SENIORWhat did Chinchilla change?
It showed that models of that era were badly undertrained. For a fixed compute budget, parameters and training tokens should grow in equal proportion — about 20 tokens per parameter — not parameters far faster, as Kaplan's paper had implied.

What went wrong the first time: Kaplan et al. used a single learning-rate schedule across runs of different lengths. A cosine schedule tuned for a long run is badly wrong for a short one, so the shorter-data runs looked worse than they should have, which biased the conclusion toward "spend the compute on parameters". Hoffmann et al. tuned the schedule per run and the exponents changed.

The demonstration: Chinchilla at 70 B parameters on 1.4 T tokens beat Gopher at 280 B on 300 B tokens, at equal training compute — and being 4× smaller it was also 4× cheaper to serve. Both halves of that matter.

The consequence people miss: GPT-3 at 175 B on 300 B tokens is roughly 1.7 tokens per parameter, more than ten times undertrained. A large part of the improvement from GPT-3 to Llama-2 was not architecture at all — it was training the same idea on far more data.

And where the rule now stops applying: Chinchilla optimises training compute only. Once a model is served at scale, inference dominates lifetime cost, so you deliberately overtrain a smaller model — Llama-3-8B on 15 T tokens is ~90× past compute-optimal. The loss improvement per token is tiny out there, and it is still worth it, because it buys a permanently cheaper model. "Chinchilla-optimal" is the answer to a question about training budgets, not about products.

SENIORAre emergent abilities real?
Sharp, unpredictable jumps are largely a measurement artefact — Schaeffer et al. showed the discontinuity usually comes from the metric, not the model. But "smooth underlying improvement" still produces genuinely new usable capabilities, so the practical phenomenon is real even when the cliff is not.

The critique, which is the part to get right: take multi-digit arithmetic scored by exact match. A model whose per-digit accuracy improves smoothly from 0.8 to 0.99 shows almost no exact-match improvement until it crosses the point where all digits are usually right — then exact-match jumps. The underlying capability curve was smooth; the metric is a step function applied to it. Swap to a continuous metric like token edit distance or log-likelihood of the correct answer and the cliff disappears. Their claim is that this accounts for the great majority of published emergence.

What survives the critique: in-context learning appearing at scale, chain-of- thought helping large models and hurting small ones, and induction heads forming abruptly during training with a visible bump in the loss curve. That last one is a genuine phase transition in mechanism, observed in the weights, not in a metric.

Why it matters operationally, which is the answer they are actually probing: if abilities emerge unpredictably, you cannot forecast safety-relevant capabilities and must test empirically at every scale. If they improve smoothly and only look discontinuous, you can forecast them with the right metrics — which argues for tracking continuous proxies rather than accuracy thresholds during a run. I would design evaluation for the second world while keeping checks appropriate to the first.

MIDYou have a fixed compute budget. How do you decide model size and data size?
Start from C ≈ 6ND, apply the Chinchilla ratio of ~20 tokens per parameter as a baseline, then move deliberately toward a smaller, longer-trained model in proportion to how much inference you expect to serve.

The arithmetic. With 10²³ FLOPs: 6ND = 10²³ and D = 20N give N ≈ 9 B parameters and D ≈ 180 B tokens. That is the compute-optimal starting point.

Then the adjustments, in the order I would raise them:

  • Inference volume. If this serves a high-traffic product, shrink N and raise D. Serving cost is ~2N FLOPs per token forever; training is a one-off. A 7 B model trained on 2 T tokens will usually beat a 13 B on 260 B tokens in total lifetime value.
  • Data availability and quality. The ratio assumes you have 180 B good tokens. If you do not, repeating data works for a few epochs — Muennighoff et al. found up to about 4 epochs is nearly as good as fresh data, then returns collapse. Do not train a bigger model just because you ran out of tokens; think about the quality filter first.
  • Memory and topology. N determines whether the model fits your parallelism strategy. A size that forces awkward sharding can cost more in efficiency than it gains in quality.
  • Target context length. Attention's n² is outside the 6ND approximation. Long contexts are usually handled by a short-context bulk phase followed by a long-context extension phase, which is cheaper than training long throughout.

And what I would actually do first: spend a small slice of the budget on a scaling-law sweep — three or four small runs — and fit the exponents on my data mix rather than trusting published constants. The exponents are data-dependent, and that sweep is the cheapest de-risking available.

SENIORWhat actually goes wrong during a large pretraining run?
Loss spikes, hardware failures, silent data corruption, and numerical instability — and the operational discipline around them matters more than the architecture.

Loss spikes. The loss diverges suddenly, sometimes recovering, sometimes not. Causes cluster around a bad batch, an attention-logit blowup, and optimiser state going stale after a long flat stretch. Standard responses: gradient clipping, z-loss to keep output logits bounded, skipping the batch, and rewinding to the last checkpoint with a different data order — the trick the OPT and BLOOM logs both document.

Hardware. At thousands of GPUs for weeks, failures are not exceptional, they are scheduled. You need frequent checkpointing, fast restart, and health checks that catch a silently degraded device — one GPU producing subtly wrong numbers is far worse than one that dies, because the run continues and the damage is invisible.

Numerics. fp16 has too little range for attention logits, which is why bf16 is the standard; loss scaling, fp32 master weights, and fp32 accumulation in reductions are all there to stop the run silently degrading rather than obviously failing.

Data. A tokenisation bug or a mis-shuffled shard can put a whole language or domain in one contiguous stretch, and the model will visibly overfit to it. Deterministic, resumable data ordering is not a nicety — without it you cannot reproduce or debug a spike.

The thing I would emphasise: monitor gradient norm, per-source loss, and activation statistics continuously, not just total loss. Total loss is a lagging indicator; the gradient norm usually moves first, and a per-source breakdown tells you which data caused it, which is what you need to act.
