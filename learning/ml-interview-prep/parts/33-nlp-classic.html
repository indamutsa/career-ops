Classic NLP Everything that happened to text before transformers. Skimmable — but two things in here are load-bearing for an LLM role: tokenisation, which is still exactly how GPT sees the world, and embeddings, which are still the representation everything else is built on.

How to read this module

Read 33.1 and 33.3 properly — tokenisation and embeddings are live topics you will be asked about. The rest (bag of words, TF-IDF, n-grams, POS/NER) is recognition-level: know what each is and when it is still the right tool.

Tokenisation — the layer nobody thinks about until it breaks

A model cannot consume text. It consumes integers. Tokenisation is the mapping, and the choice of mapping has consequences that show up months later in production.

GranularityVocabularyThe problem with it
Characters~100Tiny vocabulary, but sequences become enormous and the model must learn spelling before meaning
Words100k+ and still incompleteEvery unseen word becomes <UNK>. Fails completely on morphologically rich languages and on code
Subwords30k–200kThe compromise everyone uses: common words stay whole, rare ones split into pieces. No <UNK> ever
BPE — byte-pair encoding mid

Start from single characters; repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair and add it to the vocabulary; stop at the target size.

Compression by shorthand. You notice you write "ing" constantly, so you invent one symbol for it. Then you notice "tion". After 50,000 such shortcuts, common text is very short and unusual text still spells out.

GPT-2 onwards uses byte-level BPE — it merges over raw bytes, so every possible input is representable and there is no unknown token, ever.

WordPiece / Unigram mid

WordPiece (BERT) merges by likelihood gain rather than raw frequency. Unigram (SentencePiece, T5, Llama) starts from a large vocabulary and prunes what costs least.

SentencePiece treats the space as a normal character, so it needs no language-specific pre-tokenisation — which is why it dominates multilingual models.

Token ≠ word basic

English averages roughly 1.3 tokens per word. Code, rare names, and non-Latin scripts are far worse.

This is a billing and fairness issue, not a curiosity: the same sentence in some languages costs 2–3× more tokens than in English, so those users pay more and hit context limits sooner for identical content.

Where tokenisation causes visible failures
  • Arithmetic. "1234" may split as "123"+"4" or "12"+"34" depending on the tokeniser, so the digit alignment the model needs for addition is not stable. Several models fixed this by forcing single-digit tokenisation.
  • Character-level tasks. "How many r's in strawberry" is hard because the model never sees letters — it sees two or three opaque chunks. Not a reasoning failure; a representation failure.
  • Glitch tokens. Tokens that appear in the tokeniser's training corpus but almost never in the model's, so their embeddings stay near-random — the SolidGoldMagikarp family. They produce bizarre, sometimes evasive outputs.
  • Trailing whitespace. A prompt ending in a space tokenises differently from one that does not, and completion quality can drop noticeably.

Counting-based representations

Bag of words basic

Represent a document as a vector of word counts. Order is discarded entirely.

Emptying a book into a blender and reporting what came out. You learn the topic; you lose the plot.

Still genuinely competitive for topic classification and spam filtering, where the presence of words carries almost all the signal.

TF-IDF basic

Weight each word by how often it appears in this document (TF) times how rare it is across the corpus (IDF = log(N / documents containing it)).

"The" appears in everything, so it identifies nothing. "Chargeback" appears in a handful of documents, so it identifies them strongly.

Still the retrieval baseline that RAG systems have to beat — BM25, its refined descendant, is a hard baseline that dense embeddings often only match rather than beat.

N-grams basic

Count sequences of n consecutive words instead of single words, recovering a little local order.

The vocabulary explodes combinatorially and the counts get sparse — the curse of dimensionality from module 20.6 in its original setting.

Embeddings — the idea that survived

Counting gives every word its own dimension, so "cat" and "kitten" are as unrelated as "cat" and "bulldozer". The distributional hypothesis says otherwise: a word is characterised by the company it keeps. Train a model to predict context from a word, and the vector it learns encodes meaning.

word2vec mid

Two objectives: skip-gram predicts context words from the centre word; CBOW predicts the centre from the context. Skip-gram with negative sampling is the version that mattered.

Negative sampling is the key efficiency trick: instead of a softmax over 100k words, train a binary classifier on the true pair plus a handful of random pairs. That is the same contrastive idea used today in embedding models like SBERT.

GloVe mid

Factorises the global word co-occurrence matrix directly rather than sliding a window.

A different route to a very similar place — which is itself the interesting result.

king − man + woman ≈ queen basic

The famous analogy: relationships appear as consistent directions in the vector space.

The honest version: it works partly because the evaluation excludes the input words from the answer. Without that exclusion the nearest vector is often "king" itself. The structure is real but weaker than the demo suggests.

Static vs contextual mid

word2vec gives "bank" one vector, averaging the river and the money senses. ELMo, then BERT, then every LLM, give a different vector per occurrence, computed from the sentence.

This is the single biggest jump in the story. The transformer's job, viewed one way, is exactly to turn context-free embeddings into context-dependent ones — which is what self-attention does layer by layer.

Bias in embeddings senior

Trained on human text, embeddings absorb human associations — occupational gender stereotypes are measurable in the geometry.

Debiasing by projecting out a "gender direction" was shown by Gonen and Goldberg to hide the bias rather than remove it: the clustering structure survives the projection. Connects to module 26.7 — a fairness fix whose failure you cannot measure is not a fix.

Questions

BASICWhy do modern models use subword tokenisation?
It is the only option that keeps the vocabulary small while never producing an unknown token. Word-level vocabularies cannot cover the tail; character-level makes sequences too long.

Word-level breaks on every unseen word — new product names, typos, code identifiers, morphologically rich languages where a single lemma has dozens of inflected forms. Every one of those becomes <UNK> and the information is simply lost.

Character-level has no coverage problem but multiplies sequence length by four or five, which with attention's O(n²) cost is brutal, and it forces the model to relearn word structure from scratch.

Subwords give both: frequent words stay single tokens so common text is short, and rare words decompose into pieces the model has seen. Byte-level BPE goes further — merging over bytes means literally any input is representable, so <UNK> ceases to exist as a concept.

The trade to name: the split points are statistical, not linguistic, so "unhappiness" may split as "unh"+"appiness" rather than "un"+"happi"+"ness". The model copes, but it is a real reason character-level tasks are shaky.

MIDWhy do LLMs struggle to count letters in a word?
They never see letters. "strawberry" arrives as two or three opaque token IDs, so counting its r's requires recovering spelling the model was never given directly.

This is a representation failure, not a reasoning failure, and the distinction matters because it predicts what else will fail: reversing strings, rhyming reliably, character-level edits, counting occurrences of a substring, and some arithmetic — all tasks that need sub-token structure.

Models do partially learn spelling, from text that discusses it ("strawberry is spelled s-t-r-a-w..."), which is why they often get it right — inconsistently, since the knowledge is indirect.

Digits are the cleanest case. If "1234" tokenises as "123"+"4" in one number and "12"+"34" in another, place-value alignment is unstable across examples, so the model must learn addition separately for each split pattern. Several recent models force single-digit tokenisation for exactly this reason, and arithmetic improves measurably.

What I would do in production: not ask the model to do it. Character counting, string reversal and exact arithmetic are one line of code — give the model a tool. Fixing it by prompting is fighting the tokeniser.

MIDWhat changed between word2vec and BERT embeddings?
Static became contextual. word2vec gives "bank" one vector forever; BERT computes a fresh vector for each occurrence from its sentence.

A static embedding must average every sense of a polysemous word into one point, which is a lossy compromise for exactly the words that carry the most ambiguity. Any downstream model then has to disambiguate from that blurred starting point.

Contextual embeddings — ELMo with bidirectional LSTMs, then BERT with transformers — make the representation a function of the whole sentence. "River bank" and "savings bank" land in different regions. Self-attention is the mechanism: each layer lets a token pull in information from the tokens that disambiguate it.

The other change is how they are used. word2vec vectors were features fed into a separately-trained model. BERT introduced pretrain-then-finetune: the whole network transfers, and you replace only the head. That is the pattern the entire modern field runs on.

Where static embeddings still win: they are a lookup table. Zero inference cost, trivially cacheable, fine when you need a rough semantic prior at massive scale. And for sentence-level retrieval you want neither raw BERT nor word2vec — raw BERT's mean-pooled vectors are famously poor for similarity, which is why SBERT retrains it contrastively.

SENIORWhen would you still use TF-IDF instead of embeddings?
When exact term matching matters, when you need explainability, when latency or cost budgets are tight, or when the domain vocabulary is nothing like the embedding model's training data. In retrieval, the right answer is usually both.

Exact matching. Searching for an error code, a SKU, a case number or a rare proper noun is where dense embeddings are weakest — they retrieve semantically similar things, and "similar to the error code you wanted" is worthless. BM25 nails exact rare terms by construction.

Explainability. A TF-IDF match tells you which terms matched and with what weight. A cosine similarity of 0.87 tells you nothing you can put in front of a compliance reviewer.

Cost. TF-IDF needs no model, no GPU, no embedding service, and no re-embedding of the corpus when the model version changes — an operational burden people routinely under-count.

Domain shift. On specialised corpora — internal ticket jargon, legal citations, chemical names — a general embedding model may have no useful notion of similarity, while term statistics computed on your own corpus are by definition domain-fitted.

The production answer is hybrid. Run BM25 and dense retrieval, fuse with reciprocal rank fusion, optionally rerank the top ~50 with a cross-encoder. That is what strong RAG systems actually do, and it beats either alone on essentially every benchmark. Picked up again in module 46.
