Part C · Deep learning · Module 33

Classic NLP Everything that happened to text before transformers. Skimmable — but two things in here are load-bearing for an LLM role: tokenisation, which is still exactly how GPT sees the world, and embeddings, which are still the representation everything else is built on. Running example: turning a customer's dispute/chargeback message into features the fraud pipeline from modules 12–26 can score.

How to read this module

Read 33.1 and 33.4 properly — tokenisation and embeddings are live topics you will be asked about. The counting-based representations in 33.2–33.3 are recognition-level, but the TF-IDF arithmetic is worth working through by hand once. 33.5 (POS/NER) is a quick reference table.

The problem this was invented to solve

A fraud analyst's queue is full of free text: a customer's dispute reason ("I don't recognise this charge, I was on a flight all day"), a chargeback note, a merchant description string. The fraud model from module 12 onward only accepts numbers — it cannot consume a sentence. Classic NLP is the set of techniques for turning that sentence into numbers a model can score, before anything as elaborate as a transformer existed. Two questions recur at every step: how do we cut text into units the model can count? (tokenisation) and how do we turn those units into numbers that carry meaning? (counting, then embeddings).

Tokenisation — the layer nobody thinks about until it breaks

WhatTokenisation is the mapping from raw text to the integer IDs a model actually consumes.
WhyA model cannot read characters or words directly — every downstream step depends on how the text was first cut up.
Where in MLEvery text pipeline, from the bag-of-words counts in 33.3 to the token embeddings inside every modern LLM.

Take doc 1: "I don't recognise this charge on my card". There is more than one way to cut that string into units, and the choice has consequences that show up months later in production.

?How BPE and WordPiece actually build that subword list
BPE — byte-pair encoding mid

Start from single characters; repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair and add it to the vocabulary; stop at the target size.

Compression by shorthand. Across enough dispute messages you notice "charge" appears constantly, so you invent one symbol for it. Then "refund". After 50,000 such shortcuts, common text is very short and unusual text still spells out.

GPT-2 onwards uses byte-level BPE — it merges over raw bytes, so every possible input is representable and there is no unknown token, ever.

WordPiece / Unigram mid

WordPiece (BERT) merges by likelihood gain rather than raw frequency. Unigram (SentencePiece, T5, Llama) starts from a large vocabulary and prunes what costs least.

SentencePiece treats the space as a normal character, so it needs no language-specific pre-tokenisation — which is why it dominates multilingual models.

Where tokenisation causes visible failures
  • Token ≠ word. English averages roughly 1.3 tokens per word; code, rare names, and non-Latin scripts are far worse. This is a billing and fairness issue, not a curiosity: the same sentence in some languages costs 2–3× more tokens for identical content.
  • Arithmetic. "1234" may split as "123"+"4" or "12"+"34" depending on the tokeniser, so the digit alignment the model needs for addition is not stable — relevant the moment a case note contains a disputed amount like "$1,234.56". Several models fixed this by forcing single-digit tokenisation.
  • Character-level tasks. "How many r's in strawberry" is hard because the model never sees letters — it sees two or three opaque chunks. Not a reasoning failure; a representation failure.
  • Glitch tokens. Tokens that appear in the tokeniser's training corpus but almost never in the model's, so their embeddings stay near-random — the SolidGoldMagikarp family. They produce bizarre, sometimes evasive outputs.
  • Trailing whitespace. A prompt ending in a space tokenises differently from one that does not, and completion quality can drop noticeably.
Interview takeawaySubword tokenisation is the only option that keeps the vocabulary small while never producing an unknown token; every character-level and digit-alignment failure traces back to this one design choice.

Counting-based representations

WhatRepresent a tokenised document as a vector of word statistics — counts, weighted counts, or short-sequence counts.
WhyOnce text is tokens, the simplest usable number is how often each token appears — no model training required.
Where in MLTopic classification, spam filtering, and the sparse half of every hybrid retrieval system.

The mental model

Emptying each message into a blender and reporting what came out. You learn that doc 2 is about "refund" and "charge"; you lose the fact that it was a polite request rather than an accusation. Still genuinely competitive for topic classification and spam filtering, where the presence of words carries almost all the signal.

?Why raw counts overweight words like "this" and "please"

Look at the table again: "this" has a count of 1 in every document. It tells you nothing about which document you're looking at — it just happens to be common English. Meanwhile "refund" appears in the two documents that are actually asking for money back. A word's raw count inside one document says nothing about whether that word is generically frequent or specifically informative. That gap is exactly what TF-IDF fixes.

Weighting the counts — TF-IDF

WhatWeight each word by how often it appears in this document (TF) times how rare it is across the corpus (IDF).
WhyRaw counts can't distinguish a word that is common everywhere from a word that is specific to this document — TF-IDF makes that distinction a number.
Where in MLSearch ranking, document classification, and the sparse baseline every dense-retrieval system has to beat.
bag of words → count how many documents contain each word → weight rare words higher → TF-IDF

TF-IDF(term, doc) = TF(term, doc) × IDF(term)      IDF(term) = log(N / documents containing term)

?What TF and IDF each mean before combining them

TF (term frequency) is exactly the bag-of-words count from 33.3: how many times this term appears in this one document. IDF (inverse document frequency) looks across the whole corpus instead of one document: of the N documents total, how many contain this term at least once? A term that shows up in every document gets log(N/N) = log(1) = 0 — it is erased. A term that shows up in only one document out of many gets a large positive number.

corpus: the 5 dispute/support messages from 33.1, N = 5
term = "refund"  ·  appears in doc 2 (1×), doc 3 (1×), doc 5 (1×)  ·  absent from doc 1 and doc 4
documents containing "refund" = 3
IDF("refund") = log(N / 3) = log(5/3) = log(1.667) = 0.511
TF("refund", doc 3) = 1  (appears once in "This charge is a duplicate, please refund")
TF-IDF("refund", doc 3) = 1 × 0.511 = 0.511
compare: term = "this"  ·  appears in doc 1, 2, 3, 4 → documents containing = 4
IDF("this") = log(5/4) = log(1.25) = 0.223  ·  TF-IDF("this", doc 3) = 1 × 0.223 = 0.223
"refund" scores more than double "this" in the same document, even though both appear once — because "refund" is rarer across the corpus and therefore more informative about what doc 3 is specifically about.
what each symbol is Ntotal documents in the corpus — 5 dispute messages TF(term, doc)the bag-of-words count of that term in that one document IDF(term)log of (total documents ÷ documents containing the term) — 0 for a universal word, large for a rare one lognatural log here; any consistent base works, it only rescales every weight the same way
Interview takeawayTF-IDF is TF (this document) times IDF (rarity across the corpus) — a word gets weight only if it is both present here and absent elsewhere; still the retrieval baseline BM25 (its refined descendant) that dense embeddings often only match rather than beat.

N-grams — recovering a little order

WhatCount sequences of n consecutive words instead of single words.
WhyBag of words and TF-IDF both discard order entirely — an n-gram of 2+ words recovers some of the local sequence.
Where in MLText classification when local phrasing matters (e.g. "don't recognise" vs. "recognise" alone), before it explodes in size.

Bag of words could never tell "I don't recognise this charge" (dispute) apart from "recognise the excellent service" (not a dispute) — both contain "recognise". Bigrams (2-word sequences) keep the neighbour, so they capture "don't recognise" as one unit instead of two independent words.

doc 1: "I don't recognise this charge on my card"
bigrams(doc 1) = {i-don't, don't-recognise, recognise-this, this-charge, charge-on, on-my, my-card}
doc 4: "I don't recognise this merchant at all"
bigrams(doc 4) = {i-don't, don't-recognise, recognise-this, this-merchant, merchant-at, at-all}
Docs 1 and 4 now visibly share the bigram "don't-recognise" — the exact phrase that flags both as identity-style disputes — which a single-word bag of words also captured (both contain "recognise"), but only the bigram distinguishes this from "recognise the excellent service", which shares the unigram but no bigram.
Trap

Two 9-word documents each produced 7 bigrams from a 16-word unigram vocabulary — the bigram vocabulary is already larger than the unigram one, and it grows combinatorially as the corpus grows. Counts get sparse fast: most possible bigrams never occur, so most columns in the table are zero. This is the curse of dimensionality from module 20.6 in its original setting.

Interview takeawayN-grams trade vocabulary size for a little local word order; beyond n=2 or 3 the sparsity usually outweighs the benefit for classic bag-style models.

Embeddings — the idea that survived

WhatA dense, learned vector per word, trained so that words appearing in similar contexts land near each other.
WhyCounting gives every word its own dimension, so "refund" and "reimbursement" are as unrelated as "refund" and "bulldozer" — no notion of meaning survives.
Where in MLThe representation underneath nearly every modern NLP and LLM system, from static word2vec vectors to today's contextual transformer embeddings.
bag of words / TF-IDF → one dimension per word, no notion of similarity → learn a vector per word from context → embeddings

TF-IDF can tell you doc 3 ("duplicate charge") and doc 5 ("used without permission") are different because they share few words. It cannot tell you that "refund" and "reimbursement" mean nearly the same thing — as far as the TF-IDF vector is concerned, they are two unrelated columns. The distributional hypothesis says otherwise: a word is characterised by the company it keeps. Train a model to predict context from a word, and the vector it learns encodes meaning.

?How word2vec turns "predict the context" into a vector
word2vec mid

Two objectives: skip-gram predicts context words from the centre word; CBOW predicts the centre from the context. Skip-gram with negative sampling is the version that mattered.

Negative sampling is the key efficiency trick: instead of a softmax over 100k words, train a binary classifier on the true pair plus a handful of random pairs. That is the same contrastive idea used today in embedding models like SBERT.

GloVe mid

Factorises the global word co-occurrence matrix directly rather than sliding a window.

A different route to a very similar place — which is itself the interesting result.

Trained on enough dispute and support text, a word2vec-style model would place "refund" near "reimbursement" and near "chargeback" — because they occur beside the same neighbouring words ("please", "my", "the charge") across thousands of messages, not because anyone told it they were synonyms.

The clearest illustration of what these vectors capture isn't a fraud example — it's the famous general-English word analogy, kept here because forcing fraud vocabulary into it would be contrived. The point it makes (relationships appear as directions in the vector space) applies just as much to "refund" and "reimbursement" as it does to the classic pair below:

king − man + woman ≈ queen

Subtracting the "man" vector from "king" and adding "woman" lands near "queen" — the direction from "man" to "king" behaves like the direction from "woman" to "queen". The honest version: this works partly because the evaluation excludes the input words from the answer. Without that exclusion the nearest vector is often "king" itself. The structure is real but weaker than the demo suggests.

Back to the running example: the same directional structure is what lets a fraud model trained on messages containing "unauthorised charge" generalise to a new message saying "charge I didn't authorise" — the embeddings for "unauthorised" and "didn't authorise" land close together even though not one word overlaps.

?Static vs contextual — the jump that mattered most

word2vec gives "charge" one vector, forever — averaging "charge" the noun (a fee on an account) and "charge" the verb (to charge someone with a crime) into a single blurred point. ELMo, then BERT, then every LLM, compute a different vector per occurrence, from the sentence around it. "I don't recognise this charge" and "please charge my account again next month" get different vectors for the same word, because self-attention lets each token pull in information from the tokens that disambiguate it.

Bias in embeddings

Trained on human text, embeddings absorb human associations — occupational gender stereotypes are measurable in the geometry. Debiasing by projecting out a "gender direction" was shown by Gonen and Goldberg to hide the bias rather than remove it: the clustering structure survives the projection. Connects to module 26.7 — a fairness fix whose failure you cannot measure is not a fix.

Interview takeawayEmbeddings replace one-dimension-per-word counting with a learned vector where similar meaning means nearby vectors; static (word2vec/GloVe) give one vector per word type, contextual (ELMo, BERT, every LLM) give one vector per occurrence — that shift from static to contextual is the single biggest jump in this module's story.

Questions

BASICWhy do modern models use subword tokenisation?
It is the only option that keeps the vocabulary small while never producing an unknown token. Word-level vocabularies cannot cover the tail; character-level makes sequences too long.

Word-level breaks on every unseen word — new product names, typos, code identifiers, morphologically rich languages where a single lemma has dozens of inflected forms. Every one of those becomes <UNK> and the information is simply lost.

Character-level has no coverage problem but multiplies sequence length by four or five, which with attention's O(n²) cost is brutal, and it forces the model to relearn word structure from scratch.

Subwords give both: frequent words stay single tokens so common text is short, and rare words decompose into pieces the model has seen. Byte-level BPE goes further — merging over bytes means literally any input is representable, so <UNK> ceases to exist as a concept.

The trade to name: the split points are statistical, not linguistic, so "unhappiness" may split as "unh"+"appiness" rather than "un"+"happi"+"ness". The model copes, but it is a real reason character-level tasks are shaky.

MIDWhy do LLMs struggle to count letters in a word?
They never see letters. "strawberry" arrives as two or three opaque token IDs, so counting its r's requires recovering spelling the model was never given directly.

This is a representation failure, not a reasoning failure, and the distinction matters because it predicts what else will fail: reversing strings, rhyming reliably, character-level edits, counting occurrences of a substring, and some arithmetic — all tasks that need sub-token structure.

Models do partially learn spelling, from text that discusses it ("strawberry is spelled s-t-r-a-w..."), which is why they often get it right — inconsistently, since the knowledge is indirect.

Digits are the cleanest case. If "1234" tokenises as "123"+"4" in one number and "12"+"34" in another, place-value alignment is unstable across examples, so the model must learn addition separately for each split pattern. Several recent models force single-digit tokenisation for exactly this reason, and arithmetic improves measurably.

What I would do in production: not ask the model to do it. Character counting, string reversal and exact arithmetic are one line of code — give the model a tool. Fixing it by prompting is fighting the tokeniser.

MIDWhat changed between word2vec and BERT embeddings?
Static became contextual. word2vec gives "charge" one vector forever; BERT computes a fresh vector for each occurrence from its sentence.

A static embedding must average every sense of a polysemous word into one point, which is a lossy compromise for exactly the words that carry the most ambiguity — "charge" the fee versus "charge" the accusation is one such case. Any downstream model then has to disambiguate from that blurred starting point.

Contextual embeddings — ELMo with bidirectional LSTMs, then BERT with transformers — make the representation a function of the whole sentence. "Unauthorised charge" and "please charge my card" land in different regions. Self-attention is the mechanism: each layer lets a token pull in information from the tokens that disambiguate it.

The other change is how they are used. word2vec vectors were features fed into a separately-trained model. BERT introduced pretrain-then-finetune: the whole network transfers, and you replace only the head. That is the pattern the entire modern field runs on.

Where static embeddings still win: they are a lookup table. Zero inference cost, trivially cacheable, fine when you need a rough semantic prior at massive scale. And for sentence-level retrieval you want neither raw BERT nor word2vec — raw BERT's mean-pooled vectors are famously poor for similarity, which is why SBERT retrains it contrastively.

SENIORWhen would you still use TF-IDF instead of embeddings?
When exact term matching matters, when you need explainability, when latency or cost budgets are tight, or when the domain vocabulary is nothing like the embedding model's training data. In retrieval, the right answer is usually both.

Exact matching. Searching for a case ID, a merchant code, or a rare proper noun in dispute notes is where dense embeddings are weakest — they retrieve semantically similar things, and "similar to the case ID you wanted" is worthless. BM25 nails exact rare terms by construction.

Explainability. A TF-IDF match tells you which terms matched and with what weight — useful when a fraud analyst has to justify why a case was flagged. A cosine similarity of 0.87 tells a compliance reviewer nothing they can act on.

Cost. TF-IDF needs no model, no GPU, no embedding service, and no re-embedding of the corpus when the model version changes — an operational burden people routinely under-count.

Domain shift. On specialised corpora — internal dispute-reason jargon, chargeback codes, merchant-category text — a general embedding model may have no useful notion of similarity, while term statistics computed on your own corpus are by definition domain-fitted.

The production answer is hybrid. Run BM25 and dense retrieval, fuse with reciprocal rank fusion, optionally rerank the top ~50 with a cross-encoder. That is what strong RAG systems actually do, and it beats either alone on essentially every benchmark. Picked up again in module 46.
