Part F · Interview rounds · Module 66

Behavioural & ML judgment The round people prepare least and lose most often. At senior level it is not a personality test — it is a test of whether your technical judgment survives contact with deadlines, disagreement, and being wrong.

How to run this round

45 minutes, and this one needs a voice recorder. Behavioural answers fail by rambling, and you cannot hear yourself ramble while you are doing it. Record each answer, play it back, and cut it until it lands in 90 seconds to two minutes.

The prompts below are deliberately not filled in. Your stories have to come from your own work — a borrowed anecdote collapses on the second follow-up, and the follow-up is where the marks are. Fill them from interview-prep/story-bank.md, and where a story cites a number, make sure you know where that number came from before you say it out loud.

The structure — STAR, plus the part people drop

S · Situation 15 seconds

Enough context to make the problem legible, and not one sentence more. The team, the system, what was at stake.

The most common failure in the whole round is a two-minute Situation. If the interviewer still does not know what you did by the 45-second mark, you have lost them.

T · Task 10 seconds

What you specifically were responsible for. Not the team's goal — yours.

This is where "we" starts creeping in. Use "we" for context and "I" for your actions, deliberately, and notice when you slip.

A · Action 60 seconds — the bulk

The decisions you made and, crucially, why you made them rather than the alternative.

Senior answers live here. A junior answer says what was built; a senior answer says what was considered, what was rejected, and on what evidence. Name at least one option you did not take.

R · Result 20 seconds

What happened, quantified where you honestly can, and what happened to the thing you actually cared about — the business or user outcome, not just the model metric.

If you do not have a clean number, say so and give the qualitative outcome. An invented metric dies on the question "how was that measured?", and that question always comes.

R · Reflection 15 seconds — the differentiator

What you would do differently, or what the experience changed about how you work now.

This is the part almost everyone omits, and it is the cheapest way to sound senior. It converts a story about a project into evidence of learning — which is the actual thing being assessed.

The story inventory — build this before anything else

You do not need twenty stories. You need six to eight strong ones, each of which can be angled at several questions. Write them once, properly, and then practise re-pointing them.

The provenance rule — read this before you rehearse

Stories accumulate. A figure written once to match a job description gets absorbed into the story bank, then cited as fact by the next prep document, and drifts a little further each time. In an interview that is not a stylistic problem — it is the thing that ends the conversation when someone asks how it was measured and you cannot answer.

So before you rehearse, go through your story bank and mark every number: where did this come from? If you can trace it, keep it and know the source. If you cannot, either recover the real figure or drop the number and tell the story qualitatively. "It cut the batch from hours to minutes, I do not have the exact figure to hand" is a completely acceptable senior answer. A precise number you cannot defend is not.

The ML-judgment questions

These are behavioural in form and technical in substance. They are the ones that actually distinguish candidates at this level, and they are much less rehearsed than "tell me about a conflict".

SENIORTell me about a model that did not work in production.
Pick a real one, diagnose it the way you would in round 5, and land on the systemic fix rather than the one-off patch. The interviewer is checking whether you have actually operated a model, because everyone has trained one.

What a strong answer contains:

  • A specific symptom with a number or a shape — "precision fell from X to Y in the first week", not "it performed badly".
  • The order in which you investigated, and why that order. This is the same skill as the incident questions, and it is what makes the story technical rather than anecdotal.
  • The root cause, stated plainly — including if it was your mistake. Leakage, skew, a shifted population, the wrong metric, or the decision changing behaviour.
  • The immediate fix and the systemic one. "We corrected the join" is a patch; "we made training read the logged serving features so the class of bug could not recur" is engineering.
  • What you monitor now that you did not monitor then.

The trap: telling a story where the model was fine and someone else broke it. It reads as deflection. If the cause genuinely was upstream, own the part that was yours — you did not detect it, or you had no alert that would have.

SENIORTell me about a time you had to say a project should not ship.
The point is not that you blocked something — it is that you made the cost of shipping legible to people who were not going to read your evaluation, and that you offered a path rather than only a veto.

The shape that works: what the evidence was, who wanted it shipped and why their reasons were legitimate, how you translated the risk into their terms (customers wrongly declined, regulatory exposure, on-call load — not AUC), what alternative you proposed, and what actually happened, including if you were overruled.

Being overruled is a fine ending if you handled it well: you documented the risk, you agreed on what would trigger a rollback, and you supported the decision once it was made. That version of the story is often stronger than winning, because it shows you can disagree and commit.

The trap: sounding like the person who blocks things. Balance it with a moment where you accepted a known imperfection because the trade was right — senior engineers ship imperfect systems on purpose, with the imperfection written down.

SENIORHow do you decide whether a problem needs machine learning at all?
ML earns its cost when the rule set would be large, would change often, and the inputs are noisy — and when you can measure whether it worked. Otherwise a rule is cheaper, faster, explainable, and instantly modifiable, which in fraud matters enormously.

What I would say I check: is there a labelled outcome, and how delayed is it? Is the pattern stable enough to learn, or adversarial and abrupt? Would a handful of rules get most of the value — because they very often do, and the honest baseline is a rule set, not a coin flip. What is the cost of a wrong decision, and does the domain permit an unexplainable one? And who maintains this in a year?

The senior framing: in fraud the answer is usually both. Rules handle the known, fast-changing, must-be-explainable cases and can be deployed in an hour; the model handles the long tail and the combinations no one wrote down. Presenting it as rules-versus-ML is the junior framing.

Have a real example of choosing not to use ML. It is a strong signal precisely because candidates for ML roles rarely volunteer one.

MIDHow do you explain a model to someone non-technical?
Start from the decision they care about, not the mechanism. Say what the model is optimising, what it will get wrong, and what they can do about it — then offer mechanism only if they ask.

A concrete structure: "It scores how likely each transaction is to be fraud, based on patterns in the last two years of confirmed cases. Above a threshold we hold the payment. The threshold is a business choice: raise it and we catch less fraud but annoy fewer customers; lower it and the reverse. Here is the current trade at today's setting."

What earns trust is being specific about failure. "It will be wrong in these two ways, here is roughly how often, and here is the review path" builds far more confidence than accuracy figures. Then show one worked example with its explanation — a single concrete case does more than any diagram.

Trap: the analogy that overpromises. Comparing a model to "how a human expert thinks" invites people to expect human-like reasoning and to be shocked by machine-like failures. Keep analogies to the mechanism you actually mean.

SENIORTell me about a technical disagreement and how it ended.
The best version is one where you were partly wrong, you found that out because you ran something, and the resolution was evidence rather than seniority.

What to include: the actual technical substance — the interviewer wants to hear the argument, not just that there was one. Both positions stated fairly. What you did to resolve it: an experiment, a prototype, a cost estimate, a small benchmark. And what you did once it was decided.

The strongest ending is "we ran it and the data said something neither of us expected". The second strongest is "I was wrong, and here is what I had missed". A story where you were simply right and eventually everyone agreed is the weakest, because it demonstrates nothing about how you behave under genuine uncertainty.

Trap: characterising the other person badly. Even mild dismissiveness — "they did not really understand the maths" — reads as a warning sign about how you would treat a colleague in their team.

MIDHow do you keep up with the field?
Be specific and honest about your actual routine, and make it concrete with one thing you learned recently and what you did with it. Vague answers ("I read papers, I follow Twitter") are worse than a modest but real routine.

The shape: one or two sources you genuinely use, a filter for what you bother to read deeply, and — the part that matters — an example of something you read and then tried. Reading without implementing is a weak signal in a research-engineering role; implementing something small and reporting what actually happened, including if it did not help, is a strong one.

The reframe worth offering: most of the field is noise on any given week, so the skill is not consuming more but deciding what is load-bearing. Saying that you deliberately go deep on fundamentals and shallow on releases is a defensible, senior position.

Your questions for them

Prepare six; you will get through three. Good questions are the ones whose answers would genuinely change whether you want the job — and they double as a demonstration that you know how this work goes wrong.

  • How do models get from a notebook to production here, and how long does that take end to end?
  • What happens when a model degrades — who notices, and how?
  • How much of the role is research versus productionisation, honestly, over a year?
  • What is the biggest piece of technical debt in the ML stack right now?
  • How do you decide what to work on — who sets the research agenda?
  • What has someone in this role tried that did not work out?
  • What does the feedback loop look like — how do you learn whether a shipped model actually helped?
  • What would you want the person in this role to have accomplished after six months?
Listen to the answers

These are diagnostics, not performance. If nobody can describe how a model gets to production, or the answer to "what happens when it degrades" is a pause, you have learned something important about what the first year would be like.

Checkpoint — you can move on when you can

  • Tell all eight inventory stories in under two minutes each, out loud, recorded.
  • Point any one of them at three different questions without restarting from scratch.
  • Source every number you use — or tell the story cleanly without one.
  • Answer "a model that did not work in production" as a diagnosis with an order, not an anecdote.
  • Give a real example of deciding not to use machine learning.
  • Ask six questions whose answers would change your decision about the job.
