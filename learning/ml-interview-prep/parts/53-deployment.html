Deployment Getting a model in front of traffic without betting the business on it. Shadow, canary, blue-green, A/B — four different questions, four different answers, and knowing which question you are asking is the whole skill.

The four strategies

StrategyTraffic to new modelThe question it answersRollback
Shadow100 % copied, 0 % servedDoes it work at all — latency, errors, sane outputs?Nothing to roll back
Canary1 % → 5 % → 25 % → 100 %Does it hurt anything at increasing exposure?Shift traffic back, seconds
Blue-green0 % then 100 %, instantlyCan we switch and revert atomically?Flip back, instant
A/B test50/50, held for a fixed periodIs it measurably better on the business metric?Stop the test
The analogy

Shadow is a trainee doctor writing their own diagnosis while the consultant treats the patient — nobody is at risk, and you learn whether the trainee is any good.

Canary is the caged bird in the mine: send a small, expendable sample in first and watch it. Blue-green is a second identical kitchen — you switch the whole service to it and switch back if the first plate comes out wrong. A/B is the actual experiment, with statistics attached.

The terms

Shadow / dark launch mid

Send production traffic to the new model, log its predictions, discard them. The old model still serves.

Zero user risk, and it catches almost every engineering failure — latency, memory, missing features, encoding mismatches — before anyone is exposed. It cannot measure whether the model is better, because its decisions never take effect.

Canary mid

Route a small percentage to the new model, watch guard metrics, then increase in stages.

Each stage needs a hold time long enough for the metrics to be meaningful, and automatic rollback triggers defined before you start. A canary you have to watch manually at 3 a.m. is not a canary.

Blue-green mid

Two full environments; switch all traffic at once; keep the old one warm.

Fastest possible rollback and no version-mixing, at the cost of double the infrastructure. Preferred when partial exposure is unacceptable or when the two versions cannot safely coexist — a schema change, for instance.

Guard metrics senior

The small set that can automatically abort a rollout: error rate, p99 latency, prediction distribution shift, and one business metric with a fast enough signal.

Model quality metrics usually cannot be a guard, because labels arrive days later. This is what makes ML deployment different from software deployment, and it is the point to make explicitly.

Multi-armed bandit senior

Adaptively shift traffic toward whichever variant is performing better, instead of holding a fixed split.

Less regret than a fixed A/B when the feedback is fast and the goal is to exploit a winner. Worse when you need a clean, unbiased estimate of the effect — the adaptive allocation complicates the statistics — or when the reward is delayed, which in fraud it is.

Feature flag / kill switch mid

A runtime control that routes traffic to the fallback without a deploy.

The single highest-value piece of deployment infrastructure. Decoupling "ship the code" from "enable the behaviour" is what turns an incident into a config change.

Questions

MIDHow would you deploy a new fraud model?
Shadow first to prove it runs, then a staged canary with automatic rollback on guard metrics, then a proper A/B for the business decision — and a kill switch throughout.

Phase 1 — shadow, about a week. Mirror live traffic, log both models' predictions, serve only the old one. I am checking engineering health (p99 latency inside budget, error rate, no missing features) and agreement analysis: where do the two models disagree, and when I sample those cases by hand, which one is right? Disagreement analysis on shadow traffic is the cheapest quality signal available before anyone is exposed.

Phase 2 — canary. 1 % for a day, 5 % for a day, 25 %, then 100 %, with automatic rollback on error rate, latency, approval-rate change beyond a threshold, and prediction distribution shift. Route by a hashed entity id, not per request, so a card gets a consistent experience — otherwise the same card is scored by two models and your comparison is contaminated.

Phase 3 — measure what actually matters. Fraud has delayed labels: chargebacks take weeks. So the canary can only guard the fast signals, and the real quality comparison happens in a held A/B run long enough for outcomes to mature. I would keep the split fixed for that window and be explicit with the business that "no alarms in the canary" is not the same as "the model is better".

Throughout: a kill switch that reverts to the previous version or to rules-only without a deploy; both model versions stamped on every logged decision so the analysis is possible at all; and a documented rollback owner. I would also agree the rollback criteria with the fraud team in writing before starting, because at 2 a.m. nobody wants to negotiate a threshold.

SENIORCanary or blue-green — how do you choose?
Canary when you can tolerate a small blast radius and want evidence at increasing exposure; blue-green when the two versions cannot coexist or partial exposure is unacceptable. The deciding questions are whether versions can run side by side, and how fast your signal is.

Canary suits ML by default. Model quality is probabilistic — you cannot prove it from a staging environment, only observe it under real traffic. Canary limits exposure to 1 % while you gather that evidence, and rollback is a traffic shift rather than a redeploy.

Blue-green wins when: the versions genuinely cannot coexist (a feature-store schema change, a different online-store layout); consistency matters more than gradualism, e.g. a regulatory rule where two populations must not be treated by different models; the signal is so slow that a staged canary buys nothing anyway; or the rollout must be atomic and instantly reversible.

The ML-specific complication with canary, which is the real content of this answer: the guard metrics available in minutes are engineering metrics. Model quality needs labels, and in fraud those are weeks away. So a canary validates that the model runs correctly and that its output distribution is sane — not that it is better. I use proxy guards for the fast decision (score distribution vs the incumbent, approval-rate delta, agreement rate on overlapping traffic) and defer the quality verdict to a properly powered A/B.

In practice I would combine them: blue-green infrastructure for instant rollback, with traffic weighting on top for staged exposure. They are not exclusive, and saying so is usually the answer the interviewer is waiting for.

SENIORYour model is 3 % better offline. It ships. Nothing improves. Why?
The usual suspects, in order: the offline metric is not the business metric, the evaluation leaked or was optimistic, the deployment changed the data-generating process, or 3 % was inside the noise all along.

1 — Metric mismatch. AUC improved; the business cares about fraud caught at a fixed false-positive budget. A model can gain AUC by improving ranking in a region of the score distribution nobody operates in. Always evaluate at the operating threshold, not on the aggregate curve — this is module 25's point, and it is the most common cause.

2 — Optimistic offline evaluation. Point-in-time leakage (module 51), a random rather than temporal split, or having tuned against the test set across many iterations. If the offline gain does not reproduce on a fresh, untouched period, it was never real.

3 — Feedback loops. Your training labels come from decisions the old model made. The new model's better ranking changes which transactions get approved, which changes which labels you observe — the population it now faces is not the one it was evaluated on. This is why the randomised holdout from module 52 matters.

4 — Nothing downstream changed. A better score that feeds the same threshold, the same review queue with the same analyst capacity, and the same customer flow may produce identical outcomes. The model was never the constraint — the review capacity was. This is the finding that most often surprises the team, and it is worth checking early because it is cheap.

5 — Underpowered measurement. With a low base rate, detecting a 3 % relative improvement needs a lot of events. If you did not compute the required sample size before the test, "no improvement" may just be "no statistical power" — a different problem with a different fix.

How I would diagnose it: compare the offline metric computed on the exact online population during the test window. If the offline gain vanishes there, it is a generalisation or leakage problem; if it holds while the business metric does not move, the gap is between the model output and the decision it feeds — and that is a systems problem, not a modelling one.
