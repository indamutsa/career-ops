Part F · Interview rounds · Module 62

Round 3 · Deep learning Neural networks, CNNs, RNNs and RL. The bridge round: it establishes that you understand the machinery underneath transformers rather than only the transformer.

How to run this round

45 minutes. The debugging question is the one that most reliably separates people who have trained models from people who have read about training them. Rehearse it until the order of checks comes out without thinking.

Rapid fire — 20 seconds each

BASICWhy do we need a non-linear activation?
Without one, a stack of linear layers collapses into a single linear layer — depth buys nothing. The non-linearity is what makes composition expressive.
MIDWhy ReLU over sigmoid?
Sigmoid saturates at both ends, where its gradient is nearly zero, so deep stacks starve. ReLU has gradient 1 on the positive side, is cheap, and produces sparse activations. Its cost is dead units, which is what leaky ReLU and GELU address.
MIDWhat causes vanishing gradients?
Backprop multiplies derivatives along the chain. If those factors are consistently below 1, the product shrinks exponentially with depth and the early layers stop learning. Residual connections, normalisation and careful initialisation are all responses to this.
MIDWhat does a residual connection do?
It adds the input to the output, so the gradient has a path that multiplies by 1. The layer learns a correction rather than a whole transformation, which makes very deep networks trainable.
MIDBatchNorm versus LayerNorm?
BatchNorm normalises each feature across the batch — so it depends on batch size and behaves differently at inference. LayerNorm normalises across features within one example, which is batch-independent and therefore what sequence models use.
BASICWhat is dropout doing?
Randomly zeroing units during training so the network cannot rely on any single one — an implicit ensemble that reduces co-adaptation. It is off at inference, with activations scaled to match.
MIDWhy does Adam usually beat plain SGD for getting started?
It keeps a per-parameter step size from running estimates of the gradient's mean and variance, so parameters with small or noisy gradients still move. It is far less sensitive to the initial learning rate — though well-tuned SGD with momentum often generalises slightly better.
MIDWhy do we warm up the learning rate?
Early in training the gradients and Adam's variance estimates are unreliable, so a full-size step can throw the model somewhere it will not recover from. Warmup is especially necessary for post-norm transformers.
MIDWhat are the two properties that make convolution work?
Locality — a pattern is defined by nearby pixels — and parameter sharing, since a feature detector useful in one place is useful everywhere. Together they cut parameters by orders of magnitude and give translation equivariance.
SENIOREquivariance or invariance — which does a convolution give you?
Equivariance: shift the input and the feature map shifts with it. Invariance comes from pooling and from global pooling at the end. And it is only approximate — strided convolutions alias, which is why shifting an image by one pixel can change the prediction.
MIDWhat does a 1×1 convolution do?
Mixes channels at each spatial position without mixing space — used to change channel depth cheaply, add non-linearity, and build bottlenecks. It is exactly a position-wise fully connected layer, which is what a transformer's FFN is.
MIDWhy does an LSTM help with long sequences?
The cell state is updated additively and gated, so ∂c_t/∂c_{t−1} is the forget gate rather than a product of weight matrices. When the gate is near 1 the gradient flows almost unattenuated — the same multiply-by-1 highway idea as a residual connection.
MIDWhat problem was attention originally invented to solve?
The seq2seq bottleneck: compressing an entire source sentence into one fixed vector, whose quality collapsed as sentences got longer. Attention let the decoder look back at all encoder states, weighted by relevance.
BASICSupervised learning versus reinforcement learning, in one sentence?
Supervised learning is told the correct answer for each input; RL is only told how good the outcome was, possibly much later, and must work out which of its actions deserved the credit.
MIDWhat is the advantage function?
A(s,a) = Q(s,a) − V(s): how much better this action is than the state's average. Subtracting the baseline leaves the gradient unbiased while cutting its variance substantially.
SENIORWhat does PPO's clipping actually prevent?
A destructively large policy update from one batch. Once the probability ratio leaves [1−ε, 1+ε] the objective flattens, so the gradient vanishes and the policy cannot move further in that direction on that batch — a trust region enforced by the loss shape rather than by a constraint solver.

Deep questions — 5 minutes each

SENIORYour model trains but does not generalise. Walk me through it.
Confirm it is genuinely overfitting rather than a broken evaluation, then work in order: data leakage, then more or better data, then augmentation, then regularisation, then capacity. Most "overfitting" I have seen turned out to be a split problem.

First, confirm the diagnosis. Training loss low, validation loss high and rising — that is overfitting. But before accepting it, check that the validation set is actually representative and that the split respects structure: grouped rows split across sides, or a temporal problem split at random, produce a validation score that is meaningless in either direction.

Then check for leakage in reverse: a suspiciously good validation score is as alarming as a bad one, and I would rank feature importances and interrogate the top ones for availability at prediction time.

Then the interventions, in this order: more data always wins if it is available; augmentation is data you already have, and it is where domain knowledge pays; then regularisation — weight decay, dropout, early stopping — tuned on the validation curve; then reduce capacity, which I put last because it is the bluntest tool and, per the double-descent story, not always the right direction.

The diagnostic I would actually run first, though, is the learning curve: plot train and validation error against training-set size. If both are high and converged, the model is underfitting and more data will not help. If there is a large gap that is still narrowing, more data will help. That plot answers "which problem do I have" in one experiment, and it saves days of guessing.

SENIORYour loss is NaN at step 400. What happened?
Almost always one of five things: a learning rate too high, a log or division by zero in the loss, fp16 overflow, exploding gradients, or bad input data. Bisect by reproducing on one batch with the same seed.

The checks, in the order I would do them:

  • Learning rate. Drop it 10× and rerun. If the NaN moves later or vanishes, it was divergence. Fastest single test.
  • The loss function. log(0) and division by a near-zero are the classic culprits — an unclamped log-probability, a softmax computed unstably, an epsilon missing from a denominator. Use the framework's numerically stable fused versions rather than composing them yourself.
  • Precision. fp16 overflows at 65,504, and attention logits get there easily. bf16 has the range and is the reason it is now the default; with fp16 you need loss scaling.
  • Gradient explosion. Log the gradient norm every step — it usually spikes for several steps before the NaN appears, which both confirms the diagnosis and gives you a clip value.
  • The data. A NaN or an inf in one input, an unnormalised outlier, or a corrupted sample. Assert on the batch rather than trusting the loader.

How to localise it fast: make it reproducible — fixed seed, deterministic loader — so you can find the exact batch, then run forward with hooks that check every activation for non-finite values. That tells you which layer produced the first NaN, which usually names the bug outright.

The production-side answer is that a large run needs this handled automatically: gradient clipping on by default, non-finite loss detection that skips the batch and logs it, and frequent checkpoints so you can rewind and continue with different data ordering — which, as module 43 covers, is standard practice for loss spikes at scale.

SENIORExplain RLHF to someone who knows supervised learning but not RL.
You cannot write the ideal answer for every prompt, but you can say which of two answers is better. So you train a model to predict those preferences, and then optimise the language model against that predictor — with a leash keeping it near where it started.

The framing: supervised learning needs a target per example. For "write a helpful reply", there is no single target — many good answers exist, and writing one by hand for every prompt does not scale. Comparison does scale, because judging is cheaper than generating.

The three stages in that language: fine-tune on demonstrations to get a model that answers rather than continues; train a reward model on preference pairs, which is just a classifier with a Bradley–Terry loss; then optimise the language model to produce responses the reward model likes.

Why that last stage is RL and not supervised: the model generates its own training data — you score what it produced, not a fixed dataset — and the reward is a single number for a whole sequence, so the credit assignment across tokens is exactly the RL problem. That is the sentence to land.

And the leash: a KL penalty against the starting model. Without it the policy drifts into a region where the reward model is confidently wrong and exploits it — Goodhart's law, and the reason "optimise the proxy harder" is not the right instinct.

The natural follow-up, which I would pre-empt: DPO removes the RL loop entirely by deriving the optimal policy in closed form, so you train on the preference pairs directly. Same objective, much simpler pipeline — which is why most open models now use it, and why "RLHF" is increasingly a description of the goal rather than of the algorithm.

The design prompt — 15 minutes

Talk through this out loud

"Design a model that detects fraudulent sequences of transactions rather than scoring each one independently. What architecture, and why?"

What a strong answer covers: why the sequence framing helps at all — fraud is a pattern over time (a small test transaction followed by a large one), invisible per transaction → the candidate architectures and the honest trade-off between them: a GBDT over hand-built velocity features (strong baseline, cheap, interpretable), an LSTM/GRU over the card's recent history (natural fit, sequential inference), a small transformer over the sequence (parallel training, attends to the relevant earlier transaction directly, needs more data) → the practical constraints: sequence length and truncation, the latency budget for a synchronous decision, and the fact that a transformer over 50 events is cheap while over 5,000 is not → and the point most candidates miss: whatever the architecture, the sequence must be built with a point-in-time-correct history, or you have simply built a more sophisticated leak.

The strongest close is to propose the GBDT-plus-velocity-features baseline first and treat the sequence model as the thing that must beat it — and to say what evidence would decide.
