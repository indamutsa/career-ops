Part A · Maths · Module 14

Optimisation Module 10 fitted the house price rule w by solving an equation exactly. Module 11 showed that the gradient is the local slope of a loss surface. This module is what happens when you cannot solve the equation and have to walk downhill instead — and what happens when walking downhill is not enough, because a rule says how far you are allowed to go. Same four houses throughout.

From a solved equation to a moving one

Section 10.7 found the house weights w = [3, 8, −0.4, −12] in one shot, by inverting a matrix: four sold houses, four unknown prices-per-feature, four equations, one exact answer. That trick only works because the system was small and square. A real model can have billions of weights, and no inverse exists at that size even in principle. So instead of solving for w directly, you guess a starting value, measure how wrong it is, and nudge it in the direction that reduces the error. Repeat a few thousand times. That is the whole of this module.

The picture to hold

You are standing in fog on a hilly landscape. Height is the loss — how wrong your current guess at w is. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a step downhill, feel again, step again. Momentum, Adam, schedules, clipping — every refinement below is an answer to how big a step and in exactly which direction, nothing more exotic than that.

Gradient descent — one real update

fitted weight w (10.7, solved by matrix inverse) → no inverse exists at scale → follow the local slope instead → gradient descent
WhatRepeatedly subtract a small multiple of the gradient from the current weight.
WhyIt finds a good weight without ever solving the fitting equation exactly.
Where in MLTraining every model in this course — linear, logistic, and every neural network.
?The general rule, before it gets a house in it

Write the update once in general form, using θ — the generic symbol for any single number the model is learning, whether that is one weight, one bias, or one embedding entry:

θ ← θ − η∇L new value = old value − (step size) × (slope at the old value)

∇L is the gradient of the loss with respect to θ — section 11's compass needle, pointing uphill. η (eta) is the learning rate: how much of that slope you actually subtract. The minus sign is the entire idea — the gradient points uphill, and you want to go down.

For the rest of this page θ is one specific number: the bedrooms weight in w, which section 10.7 already told us is 8. Pretend, for this section only, that you do not know that yet — you only have a bad guess, w = 5 — and you will use gradient descent to find your way back to 8.

?Where the loss curve for this one weight comes from

Freeze the other three weights at their known-correct values — area 3, age −0.4, km −12 — and leave only the bedrooms weight, call it w, unknown. For any house h, the predicted price is 3·areah + w·bedsh − 0.4·ageh − 12·kmh, and because w = 8 already reproduces every price in the 10.1 table exactly (no noise in this dataset), the error at any other w is clean:

errorh(w) = bedsh · (w − 8) the three frozen weights cancel out of the error entirely

Square that error and average it over the four houses — the same "mean of squared errors" cost from 10.7 — and bedsA,B,C,D = 3, 2, 5, 3 gives:

L(w) = ¼(3² + 2² + 5² + 3²)(w − 8)² = ¼(47)(w − 8)² = 11.75(w − 8)²

A single bowl-shaped curve in one weight, exactly the shape 11.4 calls a minimum: flat at w = 8, curving up on both sides.

L(w) = 11.75(w − 8)²   ⇒   ∇L(w) = 23.5(w − 8)
start: w = 5  ·  ∇L(5) = 23.5(5 − 8) = 23.5(−3) = −70.5
choose η = 0.02
w ← w − η∇L(w) = 5 − 0.02×(−70.5) = 5 + 1.41 = 6.41
second step: ∇L(6.41) = 23.5(6.41 − 8) = −37.365
w ← 6.41 − 0.02×(−37.365) = 6.41 + 0.7473 = 7.1573
Two steps closed most of the gap from 5 to 8. Nothing here used the inverse from 10.7 — only the slope at the current guess and a fixed step size.
what each symbol is L(w)the loss as a function of the one weight we are pretending not to know ∇L(w)its slope — how the loss changes for a small change in w ηthe learning rate — how much of the slope to subtract each step w ← w − η∇Lthe update rule from the deep box above, with θ replaced by w
Interview takeawayGradient descent is one arithmetic step, repeated: compute the slope where you stand, subtract a fraction of it, repeat.

Batch, mini-batch, and stochastic

WhatHow many houses you average the gradient over before taking one step.
WhyAveraging over every house is exact but expensive; averaging over fewer is cheap but noisier.
Where in MLFull-batch is rare past toy problems; mini-batches of tens to thousands of examples are what every real training run uses.
Full batch

What: average the gradient over every training example before stepping.

Effect: the exact gradient of the true loss — no noise, but one step costs a full pass over the data.

Use when: the dataset is small enough that a pass is cheap, or you need a noise-free gradient to debug against.

Mini-batch / stochastic

What: average over a small subset (mini-batch), or a single example (stochastic).

Effect: a noisy estimate of the same gradient, but hundreds of steps for the price of one full-batch step.

Use when: always, past a handful of examples — this is what "SGD" means in every framework, even though most people run mini-batches, not single examples.

?The same step at w = 5, three different ways

14.2's step used every house. Break that same computation apart. The per-house contribution to ∇L at w = 5 is 2·bedsh²·(w − 8):

housebedsgradient contribution
A32(3²)(−3) = −54
B22(2²)(−3) = −24
C52(5²)(−3) = −150
D32(3²)(−3) = −54

Full batch (all four): mean = (−54−24−150−54)/4 = −282/4 = −70.5 — exactly the gradient 14.2 used.

Mini-batch (A and B only): mean = (−54−24)/2 = −39 — a real number, in the right direction, but not the same number.

Stochastic (house C alone): −150 — massively overstates the step, because C alone happens to have the most bedrooms.

Every one of these is an honest estimate of "which way is downhill". None but the full batch is exactly right, and the smaller the subset, the more it can differ from −70.5 on any given step. That variance is the price of speed.

Interview takeawayA mini-batch gradient is an unbiased but noisy estimate of the full-batch gradient — cheaper per step, and the noise itself can help escape flat regions (11.4's saddle argument).

Epoch vs. step

WhatA step is one weight update. An epoch is one full pass over the training data.
WhyThey are the same thing only for full-batch training; everywhere else you take several steps per epoch.
Where in MLLLM pretraining is usually reported in steps and tokens, because the data is often seen barely once.

Four houses, mini-batches of two — say {A, B} then {C, D} — means 4 / 2 = 2 steps make one epoch. Step 3 starts epoch 2 by revisiting {A, B}, usually reshuffled first.

Interview takeawaySteps per epoch = examples ÷ batch size; never assume "epoch" and "step" are interchangeable when someone quotes a training-run number.

The learning rate — why it dominates everything

WhatThe multiplier η on the gradient in every update.
WhyToo small and training crawls; too large and it overshoots and can diverge — on the exact same loss curve.
Where in MLThe single hyperparameter to tune first, in every model on this page.
?The same loss, two learning rates, four steps each

Reuse 14.2's exact loss, L(w) = 11.75(w − 8)², starting again at w = 5. Only η changes.

η = 0.09  ·  start w = 5, ∇L(5) = −70.5
w ← 5 − 0.09×(−70.5) = 5 + 6.345 = 11.345
∇L(11.345) = 23.5(11.345 − 8) = 78.6075
w ← 11.345 − 0.09×78.6075 = 11.345 − 7.0747 = 4.2703
Distance from the true value 8 went 3 → 3.345 → 3.730 — growing by the same factor (1.115) every step, flipping side each time. Compare η = 0.02 in 14.2, where the distance shrank from 3 to 0.8427 in the same two steps. Same loss, same starting point, opposite outcome — the only thing that changed is η.
what each symbol is η = 0.09a learning rate almost three times 14.2's 0.02 — chosen here to cross this loss's stability limit growth factor|1 − 2×11.75×η|; below 1 the steps shrink toward 8, above 1 they grow away from it — at η = 0.09 that factor is 1.115
Symptom in the loss curveCauseFix
Loss falls, then flatlines high, foreverη too small, or a genuine plateau (11.4) Raise η; add momentum (14.7)

Interview takeawayThe same loss surface can converge or diverge depending only on η; if you tune exactly one hyperparameter, tune this one first.

Schedules and warmup

WhatChange η over training instead of holding it fixed — usually a short linear rise (warmup), then a smooth decay.
WhyAdam's early updates are badly scaled (14.8 explains why); a large η then is exactly the 0.09 failure above.
Where in MLStandard for transformer pretraining: linear warmup over 1–2% of steps, then cosine decay.

Warmup keeps η small — nowhere near 14.5's diverging 0.09 — while the model's first few updates settle, then raises it into the productive range once it is safe. Decay does the opposite job at the end: a rate that safely explores early is too large to settle into a narrow minimum late, so it is brought back down, typically along a cosine curve.

Interview takeawayWarmup answers "what if the first step overshoots before the model has any signal"; decay answers "what if the last step overshoots the minimum it finally found".

Momentum

gradient descent (14.2) → each step forgets every earlier step → remember a running average of past gradients instead → momentum
WhatKeep a running average of gradients, v, and step along that instead of the raw gradient.
WhyConsistent gradients build up speed; inconsistent ones average out.
Where in MLSGD+momentum is still a strong, well-tuned baseline for vision models trained from scratch.
?Why "heavy ball" is the right picture

Plain gradient descent recomputes its direction from nothing every step — a cautious walker who stops, feels the ground, and decides fresh each time. Momentum instead keeps a velocity v, updates it a little every step, and moves along v rather than the instantaneous slope — a heavy ball that keeps rolling. If the last several gradients all point the same way, the ball speeds up in that direction; if they point opposite ways in alternation, the contributions cancel and the ball barely moves sideways at all.

v ← βv + ∇L(w)      w ← w − ηv
β = 0.85, η = 0.02  ·  start w = 5, v = 0
step 1: ∇L(5) = −70.5  ·  v ← 0.85(0) + (−70.5) = −70.5
w ← 5 − 0.02×(−70.5) = 6.41  — identical to plain GD's first step, since v started at 0
step 2: ∇L(6.41) = −37.365  ·  v ← 0.85(−70.5) + (−37.365) = −97.29
w ← 6.41 − 0.02×(−97.29) = 6.41 + 1.9458 = 8.3558
Plain gradient descent's second step reached 7.1573 (14.2); momentum reached 8.3558 — it has already overshot the true value 8, because the accumulated velocity from step 1 is still pushing forward. It will need a step or two to settle back, in exchange for the faster early progress.
what each symbol is vvelocity — a running average of gradients, not the gradient itself βhow much of the old velocity survives each step; 0.85 keeps 85% 1/(1−β)the rough number of recent steps v is averaging over; for β = 0.85 that is 1/0.15 ≈ 6.7 steps
Interview takeawayMomentum trades a chance of overshoot for faster progress along a direction the recent gradients agree on — say the trade-off out loud, don't just call it "faster".

Adaptive steps — RMSProp, Adam, AdamW

momentum's v (14.7, remembers direction) + a running average of squared gradients (remembers magnitude) → combine both → Adam
WhatRescale each parameter's step using its own recent gradient history, instead of one shared η for every weight.
WhyDifferent weights need different step sizes; one global η is a compromise that suits none of them well.
Where in MLAdam/AdamW is the default optimiser for transformers and most modern architectures.
?RMSProp first, since Adam is built on it

RMSProp keeps a running average s of the squared gradient, and divides the step by √s:

s ← β₂s + (1 − β₂)g²      w ← w − ηg/(√s + ε)

A parameter whose gradients have been consistently large gets a smaller effective step; one whose gradients have been consistently tiny gets a relatively larger one. ε is a tiny constant (typically 1e-8) that only matters when s is near zero, so division never blows up.

m ← β₁m + (1−β₁)g      s ← β₂s + (1−β₂)g²
m̂ = m/(1−β₁ᵗ)      ŝ = s/(1−β₂ᵗ)      w ← w − ηm̂/(√ŝ + ε)
β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, η = 0.001, ε = 1e-8  ·  t = 1, w = 5, g = ∇L(5) = −70.5
m = 0.9(0) + 0.1(−70.5) = −7.05  ·  s = 0.999(0) + 0.001(−70.5)² = 4.97025
m̂ = −7.05/(1−0.9¹) = −7.05/0.1 = −70.5  ·  ŝ = 4.97025/(1−0.999¹) = 4.97025/0.001 = 4970.25
√ŝ = √4970.25 = 70.5  ·  w ← 5 − 0.001×(−70.5)/(70.5+ε) ≈ 5 + 0.001 = 5.001
At t=1, bias correction always makes m̂ equal to the raw gradient exactly and ŝ equal to its square exactly, whatever β₁ and β₂ are — so m̂/√ŝ is always exactly sign(g) = −1 here. The step size is therefore just η, regardless of how large the raw gradient (−70.5) was. That is the entire point of adaptive scaling: a huge or tiny gradient produces a step of about the same, controlled size.
what each symbol is mthe first-moment average — the same idea as momentum's v, using its own decay β₁ sthe second-moment average — mean squared gradient, RMSProp's accumulator β₁, β₂the two memory factors; 1/(1−β) recent steps, same formula as momentum m̂, ŝbias-corrected versions — both start at 0, which underestimates the true average until enough steps accumulate; dividing by (1−βᵗ) corrects that εkeeps the division safe; negligible here since √ŝ = 70.5 is far from 0
?Why large models often use β₂ = 0.95, not the 0.999 default

1/(1−β₂) is roughly how many recent steps s is averaging over — the same formula 14.7 used for momentum's β. At the default β₂ = 0.999 that span is 1/0.001 = 1000 steps: the second-moment estimate reacts slowly to a genuine change in gradient scale. Lowering it to β₂ = 0.95 shrinks the span to 1/0.05 = 20 steps — the per-parameter scale adapts much faster, which large-model training pipelines find worth the extra noise.

?AdamW — the Ridge idea, applied in a different place

10.11 penalised large weights by adding ½αw² to the loss — Ridge. Plain Adam-with-an-L2-penalty does exactly that, which means the penalty's own gradient, αw, gets folded into g and divided by √ŝ along with everything else. A weight with a large gradient history gets almost no decay; the one you most want to shrink is shrunk least.

AdamW applies the same shrinkage directly to the weight, after the adaptive step, bypassing √ŝ entirely:

w ← (1 − ηd)w − ηm̂/(√ŝ + ε) this page calls the decay rate d to avoid a clash with the Lagrange multiplier λ introduced in 14.12; the figure below labels the same quantity λ

Reusing 14.2's numbers with d = 0.1: the Adam step above gave w = 5.001. AdamW instead gives w = (1 − 0.001×0.1)(5) − (−0.001) = 4.9995 + 0.001 = 5.0005 — a small difference after one step, because η is small by design. Weight decay's effect compounds over the thousands of steps of a real run; it does not need to be dramatic in one step to matter over the whole of training.

The one thing people get wrong about Adam

Adam is not "the smart one that always wins". Well-tuned SGD with momentum still generalises better than Adam on many vision benchmarks. Adam's real advantage, shown numerically above, is that it produces a controlled step size across a huge range of gradient magnitudes with almost no tuning — decisive when a training run costs a fortune and you get one attempt.

Interview takeawayAdam = momentum's m + RMSProp's s + bias correction; AdamW just moves the weight-decay term from inside the loss (Ridge-style) to directly on the weight, so every parameter decays at the same rate regardless of its own gradient history.

Gradient clipping

WhatCap the L2 length (10.4) of the whole gradient vector; if it is over a threshold, shrink the vector to exactly that length.
WhyOne bad batch can produce a gradient hundreds of times the usual size; unclipped, that single step can destroy a training run.
Where in MLStandard in LLM and RNN training, on by default.
?Why this is a norm question, not a new idea

Every update so far touched one weight. A real model updates many weights per step, so the gradient is a vector — one entry per weight — and 10.4 already defined how to turn a vector into one number: its norm. Clipping measures the gradient's L2 norm and, if it exceeds a threshold, rescales the whole vector by the same factor in every entry. The direction never changes — only how far you travel along it.

g = 64.5  — gradient components for the bedrooms and km weights on one noisy batch
‖g‖ = √(6² + 4.5²) = √(36 + 20.25) = √56.25 = 7.5
threshold = 1.0  ·  scale = 1.0/7.5 = 0.1333
g ← g × 0.1333 = 0.80.6  — check: √(0.8² + 0.6²) = √1.0 = 1.0, exactly the threshold
Same direction (the ratio 6∶4.5 equals 0.8∶0.6), a tenth of the length. Below the threshold, clipping is a no-op — it is a safety rail, not a normaliser.
what each symbol is gthe gradient vector across the weights being updated this step ‖g‖the L2 norm from 10.4 — the vector's straight-line length scalethreshold ÷ ‖g‖, applied to every entry equally
Interview takeawayClipping caps the damage of the worst step you will ever take without touching direction — a speed limiter, not a steering correction.

The optimizer family, as one chain

Each one below is the previous one plus exactly one fix, and every piece is now something you have computed by hand:

  1. GD (14.2) — the true, full-batch gradient. Correct, and one step costs a full pass over the data.
  2. SGD / mini-batch (14.3) — estimate the gradient from a subset. Noisier per step, far more steps per second.
  3. + Momentum (14.7) — average gradients over time with v. Fixes slow, zig-zag progress when the recent gradients keep agreeing on direction.
  4. + RMSProp (14.8) — divide by a running average of squared gradient magnitude, s. Fixes "one learning rate cannot suit every parameter".
  5. Adam (14.8) = momentum's m + RMSProp's s, plus bias correction so both averages are trustworthy from step 1.
  6. AdamW (14.8) = Adam with weight decay applied directly to the weight instead of folded into the loss — the version that actually delivers the regularisation people think they configured.
Interview takeawayName the chain and the one problem each link fixes; that answers "why does Adam exist" better than any one-line definition.

Constrained optimisation — minimising subject to a rule

Ridge's penalty (10.11, L = cost + ½αw²) → what if instead of a soft penalty there is a hard rule → constrained optimisation
WhatMinimise a loss f(w) subject to a rule g(w) ≤ 0 that some choices are simply not allowed to violate.
Why10.11's regularisation charged a penalty for large weights; a constraint instead rules large weights out entirely.
Where in MLA maximum weight budget, a maximum prediction latency, a policy that must stay close to a reference model.
?Constraint vs. penalty — the distinction that matters

10.11's Ridge added ½αw² to the loss: a large weight is expensive, but nothing stops you from choosing one if the fit is worth it. A constraint like w² ≤ t is different in kind: choices with w² > t are not merely costly, they are not allowed. Section 14.12 shows these two framings are more closely related than they look.

Interview takeawayA constraint rules choices out; a penalty only makes some choices more expensive — say which one a given problem actually needs.

The Lagrangian and its multiplier

WhatAttach a multiplier λ to the constraint and add it to the objective, turning a constrained problem in w into an unconstrained one in (w, λ).
WhyUnconstrained problems are what gradient descent already solves (14.2); this converts one into the other.
Where in MLEvery regularisation coefficient — 10.11's α included — is secretly a Lagrange multiplier for a size budget you never wrote down.
?Setting up the constrained version of 10.11's junk feature

10.11 fit a single "day of month sold" weight against the four residuals and found the unpenalised answer a = 110/365 = 0.30137 (10.11 rounds this to 0.3014; this page keeps one more digit so a later check comes out exact). The unpenalised loss for this one weight, from 10.11, is f(w) = ½(w − a)².

Now put a hard rule on it instead of a soft penalty: the weight's square may not exceed some budget, g(w) = w² − t ≤ 0 — the same shape of constraint as ‖w‖² ≤ t in Ridge, applied to this one coefficient.

ℒ(w, λ) = f(w) + λg(w) = ½(w − a)² + λ(w² − t)
∂ℒ/∂w = (w − a) + 2λw = 0  ⇒  w(1 + 2λ) = a  ⇒  w*(λ) = a/(1 + 2λ)
compare to 10.11's Ridge: minimising ½(w−a)² + ½αw² gives w = a/(1+α)
so the two answers match exactly when 1+2λ = 1+α, i.e. λ = α/2
use 10.11's α = 0.30  ⇒  λ = 0.15  ·  w* = 0.30137/1.30 = 0.23182 — 10.11's Ridge answer, 0.2318, to four figures
the budget this λ is enforcing: t = w*² = 0.23182² = 0.05374
Ridge's α and a Lagrange multiplier λ for the equivalent hard constraint are the same idea, off by a factor of 2 that comes purely from the ½ in Ridge's own penalty definition. Tuning α in 10.11 was, without saying so, choosing a weight budget t.
what each symbol is f(w)the loss you actually want small — here, 10.11's ½(w−a)² g(w)the constraint, written so "allowed" means g(w) ≤ 0 λthe Lagrange multiplier — the price attached to violating the rule ℒthe Lagrangian — objective plus multiplier times constraint w*(λ)the w that minimises ℒ for a given λ tthe budget — how large w² is allowed to be
?What λ = 0.15 actually means, in euros

λ is a shadow price: the rate at which the best-achievable loss would improve if the budget t were relaxed by one unit. A large λ says the rule is really biting; λ = 0 says it is not binding at all — you could delete it and get the identical answer. 14.14 shows the case where the budget is generous enough that this λ falls to exactly 0.

Interview takeawayForm ℒ = f + λg, differentiate, set to zero — a constrained problem in w has become an unconstrained one in (w, λ), and λ is the constraint's shadow price.

The dual problem

WhatMinimise ℒ over w for a fixed λ, then choose λ to make that minimum as large as possible.
WhyFor any allowed w and λ ≥ 0, that minimum is a guaranteed lower bound on the true constrained loss — the dual asks for the best such bound.
Where in MLSVM training solves the dual rather than the primal, because the dual turns out to depend only on dot products between examples.
q(λ) = minw ℒ(w, λ) = ℒ(w*(λ), λ), using w*(λ) = a/(1+2λ) from 14.12
substituting and simplifying: q(λ) = λ[a²/(1+2λ) − t]
at λ = 0.15: a² = 0.30137² = 0.09082  ·  a²/1.30 = 0.06986
q(0.15) = 0.15 × (0.06986 − 0.05374) = 0.15 × 0.01612 = 0.00242
compare to the actual constrained loss: f(w*) = ½(0.23182 − 0.30137)² = ½(0.06955)² = 0.00242
The lower bound q(λ) exactly equals the true constrained loss at this λ — no gap. That is strong duality: for this convex problem, solving the dual gives you the exact primal answer, not merely a bound on it.
what each symbol is q(λ)the dual function — the best loss achievable once λ is fixed strong dualitywhen the best lower bound meets the true minimum exactly
Interview takeawayA matching lower bound proves you cannot do better; strong duality is when the dual's best bound and the primal's true minimum are the same number.

KKT conditions

WhatFour conditions that extend "slope = 0" to constrained problems.
WhyFor a convex problem, satisfying all four certifies the global optimum — not just a stationary point.
Where in MLComplementary slackness is exactly why an SVM's decision boundary depends on a handful of support vectors and ignores the rest.
1. Stationarity: ∂ℒ/∂w = 0  ·  at w*=0.23182, λ=0.15: (0.23182−0.30137)+2(0.15)(0.23182) = −0.06955+0.06955 = 0 ✓
2. Primal feasibility: w*² ≤ t  ·  0.05374 ≤ 0.05374 ✓ (tight — the wall is exactly reached)
3. Dual feasibility: λ ≥ 0  ·  0.15 ≥ 0 ✓
4. Complementary slackness: λ(w*² − t) = 0  ·  0.15×(0.05374−0.05374) = 0 ✓
now widen the budget to t = 0.10  — bigger than a² = 0.09082, so the unconstrained answer already fits
then w* = a = 0.30137, λ = 0  ·  slackness: 0×(0.09082−0.10) = 0 ✓ — satisfied trivially, because the rule never mattered
Same four checks, two different budgets. When the wall is reached exactly, λ > 0 and it is doing work. When the budget is generous, λ collapses to 0 and the constrained and unconstrained answers are identical — "you only feel the wall if you are pressed against it."
what each symbol is complementary slacknessthe multiplier times the constraint's signed gap is zero: unused budget must have zero price
?The same slackness argument gives an SVM its sparsity

An SVM attaches one multiplier αᵢ to each training point's margin constraint. Complementary slackness says exactly what it said above: a point either sits exactly on the margin (constraint tight, αᵢ > 0 — a support vector) or sits safely inside it (αᵢ = 0, contributing nothing). The model ends up defined by a handful of points for the identical structural reason our one-weight budget above either binds or does not.

Interview takeawayKKT is necessary and sufficient for the global optimum only when the problem is convex; complementary slackness is the condition that produces sparsity for free.

The Laplace approximation

the loss curvature computed for gradient descent (14.2, ∇L(w)=23.5(w−8)) → its own slope is the curvature, module 11's Hessian in one dimension → curvature also measures confidence in the fitted weight → Laplace approximation
WhatApproximate the distribution of a fitted weight with a Gaussian, centred at the fitted value, whose width comes from the loss's curvature there.
WhyA full Bayesian posterior over the weights is intractable for any real model; this gets an honest-looking uncertainty estimate almost for free.
Where in MLBolting calibrated uncertainty onto a network that is already trained, without retraining or an ensemble.
?Why curvature, and not the height of the loss, sets the width

Treat the squared-error loss L(w) as (up to a constant) the negative log-posterior over the bedrooms weight. Its minimum, w = 8, is the mode — the single most likely value. How sure we should be about that value is a separate question from where it sits, and it is answered by how sharply L curves upward around it: a steep bowl means nearby values are punished hard and are therefore unlikely; a shallow bowl means they are barely worse than the mode, so the weight is uncertain.

L(w) = 11.75(w − 8)²  ·  ∇L(w) = 23.5(w − 8)
curvature H = d/dw[∇L(w)] = 23.5  — constant, since the loss is an exact quadratic in this one-weight sub-problem
Laplace: variance = 1/H = 1/23.5 = 0.04255  ·  standard deviation = √0.04255 = 0.2063
approximate posterior: w ≈ Normal(mean 8, std 0.2063)
A wider bowl (smaller H) would give a larger variance and a less confident estimate of the bedrooms weight; this particular loss is fairly steep, so the approximation is fairly confident. In more than one weight at once, H becomes the Hessian matrix from module 11, and the covariance is its inverse.
what each symbol is Hcurvature — the second derivative of the loss at its minimum, module 11's Hessian in one dimension modethe fitted weight, 8 — where the loss is smallest 1/Hthe approximate variance: sharp curvature → small variance → confident
?Where this stops being free

For a real network, the Hessian is d×d for d parameters, so nobody forms it exactly — real implementations approximate it as diagonal, Kronecker-factored (K-FAC), or last-layer-only. The Hessian at a real minimum is also frequently not positive-definite numerically, which a valid Gaussian requires; the usual fix is the Gauss–Newton or Fisher approximation, both positive semi-definite by construction. And the whole approximation assumes one mode — for a deep network with many equivalent minima from permutation symmetry alone, that is a generous assumption; deep ensembles capture several modes and often beat Laplace on real out-of-distribution benchmarks for exactly that reason.

Interview takeawayLaplace approximation: put a Gaussian at the mode, get its width from the curvature — the cheapest honest uncertainty estimate available from a model you have already finished training.

Questions

BASICExplain gradient descent to someone non-technical.
You are on a foggy hillside trying to reach the valley. You cannot see far, but you can feel the slope, so you step downhill, feel again, and repeat.

Concretely: 14.2 started at a wrong guess for one house weight, w = 5 when the right answer was 8. The slope there was −70.5, so the update moved to 6.41, then to 7.1573. Height is how wrong the model is; the slope is the gradient; the step size is the learning rate.

Two follow-ups worth pre-empting: we go in the negative gradient direction because the gradient points uphill by definition; and real training uses a mini-batch rather than the full dataset (14.3), which makes each step's gradient slightly wrong but hundreds of times faster.

MIDSGD or Adam — which do you choose, and why?
Adam when I get few shots at tuning or gradient scales vary wildly (transformers, sparse features); SGD with momentum when I can afford to tune and want the last fraction of generalisation (vision).

Adam's real advantage is robustness, not speed. 14.8 showed the mechanism directly: a first Adam step moved by almost exactly η regardless of whether the raw gradient was huge or tiny, because m̂/√ŝ is close to sign(g) early in training. That is decisive when a pretraining run costs a fortune and you get one attempt.

SGD+momentum's advantage is generalisation. On CNN benchmarks a well-tuned SGD still beats Adam on test accuracy. The usual story is that Adam's per-parameter rescaling finds sharper minima; that explanation is contested, so I would state it as an observation with a plausible mechanism rather than a settled fact.

Practically: transformers get AdamW, cosine schedule with warmup (14.6), and often β₂ = 0.95 rather than 0.999 for faster adaptation (14.8). Vision from scratch gets SGD+momentum, cosine.

Trap

"Adam adapts the learning rate so I do not need to tune it." No — it changes the sensible range and flattens the curve, but the peak still matters, and it still needs a schedule.

MIDWhat is AdamW, and why is decoupled weight decay not just cosmetic?
In plain Adam-plus-L2, the penalty's gradient gets divided by √ŝ along with everything else — so parameters with large gradients get almost no decay. AdamW applies decay directly to the weight, after the adaptive step, so every parameter decays equally.

14.8 derived the mechanism from 10.11's Ridge penalty: L2 regularisation adds αw to the gradient, and Adam then divides the whole thing by √ŝ. The effective decay on a parameter is therefore proportional to 1/√ŝ — inversely tied to how large that parameter's own gradients have been. Exactly the parameters you most want to constrain get decayed least.

AdamW decouples them: w ← (1−ηd)w applied separately from the adaptive gradient step. Now the decay is uniform and its hyperparameter means what it says. This is why sensible weight-decay values differ by roughly an order of magnitude between Adam and AdamW, and a real reason a paper fails to reproduce when someone swaps the optimiser and keeps the same hyperparameters.

Trap

"L2 regularisation and weight decay are the same thing." Identical for plain SGD. Not identical for any adaptive optimiser — that difference is the entire content of the AdamW paper.

MIDWhy do transformers need learning-rate warmup?
Because Adam's second-moment estimate ŝ is built from almost no data in the first few steps, so a full-size early step can land the model somewhere it never recovers from.

14.8's worked step used t = 1, where bias correction makes m̂ and ŝ exactly equal to the single observed gradient and its square. The correction fixes the mean but the estimate is still based on one sample, so its variance is enormous. Warmup keeps η near zero — well inside the safe region from 14.5, nowhere near the diverging 0.09 — while enough steps accumulate for ŝ to be trustworthy.

Two contributing factors specific to transformers: with post-LN, gradients through the deep residual stack are badly scaled at initialisation; and attention logits early in training can be extreme, producing large gradients from near-one-hot attention.

Trap

"So just use a smaller η throughout?" That avoids the early spike and then trains far too slowly for the remaining run. Warmup gives you a small step exactly where you need one and a larger step everywhere else.

SENIORExplain Lagrange multipliers, and give a real ML example.
A Lagrange multiplier converts "minimise this subject to a rule" into "minimise this plus λ times the rule" — an unconstrained problem. λ is the price of the constraint: how much the objective would improve if the rule were relaxed by one unit.

14.12 worked this on 10.11's own numbers: minimising ½(w−a)² subject to w² ≤ t gives w*(λ) = a/(1+2λ), and choosing λ = 0.15 reproduces 10.11's Ridge answer, w = 0.2318, exactly — because every regularisation coefficient is quietly a Lagrange multiplier for a size budget nobody wrote down explicitly.

A second example worth having ready is PPO, because it shows the same idea running live in production: keep the updated policy within a KL-divergence budget of a reference model. Adaptive-KL PPO tunes its multiplier online — KL too high, raise it; too low, lower it — which is dual ascent (14.13), running inside a training loop.

Trap

The sign convention on λ trips people up. For a ≤ constraint, λ ≥ 0 is required — 14.14's dual feasibility condition. A constraint can only push you back inside the feasible region, never pull you out of it.

SENIORWhat are the KKT conditions, and what does complementary slackness buy you?
KKT generalises "set the gradient to zero" to constrained problems. The condition that does the work is complementary slackness — the multiplier times the constraint's signed gap is zero, never both non-zero.

14.14 checked all four on the same weight-budget problem: stationarity, primal feasibility (obeying the rule), dual feasibility (λ ≥ 0), and slackness. With a tight budget (t = 0.0537) the rule bites and λ = 0.15 > 0; with a generous one (t = 0.10) the rule never mattered and λ collapses to exactly 0. For a convex problem, all four together certify the global optimum, not merely a stationary point.

What slackness buys: sparsity, for free. An SVM has one multiplier per training point; slackness says each point either sits exactly on the margin (multiplier positive, a support vector) or comfortably inside (multiplier zero, contributing nothing). Delete every other training point and retrain — identical boundary.

Trap

KKT is only sufficient for the global optimum under convexity. On a neural network the conditions still characterise stationary points, but satisfying them tells you nothing about global optimality — and that is expected, since we never claim global optimality there anyway (module 11).

SENIORWhat is the Laplace approximation and when would you actually reach for it?
Approximate the posterior with a Gaussian centred at the mode, with variance equal to 1/curvature there. It is the cheapest way to get uncertainty out of a network you have already finished training.

14.15 worked this on the bedrooms weight's own loss: the mode is w = 8, the curvature is H = 23.5, and the approximate posterior is Normal(8, 1/23.5) = Normal(8, 0.0426). In more than one dimension, H is module 11's Hessian, and the covariance is its inverse.

When I would reach for it: a model is already in production, someone wants calibrated uncertainty or an out-of-distribution signal, and retraining five models for an ensemble is not on the table. Last-layer Laplace is close to free.

When I would not: when the posterior is genuinely multimodal — which for a deep network it always is, given permutation symmetries alone — a single Gaussian at one mode is a strong claim, and deep ensembles usually beat it on real OOD benchmarks by capturing several modes.

Trap

The curvature matrix must be positive-definite for this to give a valid Gaussian. At a real minimum of a real network it frequently is not, numerically — implementations use the Gauss–Newton or Fisher approximation instead, which is positive semi-definite by construction. Say that, and you sound like someone who has run it rather than read about it.

MIDYour loss suddenly spikes to NaN at step 40,000. Walk me through the debugging.
Check for a bad batch first, then numerical overflow, then the learning rate — in that order, because that is the order of decreasing frequency.
  1. Isolate. Restart from the last checkpoint with the same seed and data order. Reproduces at the same step → data. Does not → nondeterminism, likely numerical.
  2. The batch. Corrupted sample, an all-padding sequence, a label out of vocabulary range. Most common cause by a wide margin.
  3. Precision. In fp16, a logit or loss term can overflow to inf, and inf − inf produces NaN. bf16 keeps fp32's range and gives up precision instead, which makes this class of bug mostly disappear.
  4. Gradients. Log the global gradient norm — 14.9's ‖g‖ — every step. If it climbs over several steps before exploding, that is an optimisation instability rather than one bad example: clip (14.9), and lower η (14.5).
  5. Adam's state. If ŝ collapses toward zero for some parameters, the division in 14.8's update blows up. Raising ε from 1e-8 to 1e-6 is a known mitigation.
  6. Structural. A log or sqrt of something that reached zero, or log(softmax(x)) written by hand instead of log_softmax.

The prevention that costs nothing: gradient-norm logging on every run, clipping on by default, and checkpoints frequent enough that a restart costs minutes rather than a day.
