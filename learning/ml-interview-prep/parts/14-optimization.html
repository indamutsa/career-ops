Part A · Maths · Module 14

Optimisation Module 11 told you what a minimum is. This one is about actually getting there — the algorithms, the one hyperparameter that matters more than all the others combined, and the machinery for minimising something subject to a rule: Lagrange, the dual, KKT, and the Laplace approximation.

The whole of training, in one sentence

The picture to hold

You are standing in fog on a hilly landscape. Height = how wrong your model is. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. So you take a step downhill, feel again, step again. That is every optimiser in machine learning. Everything else — momentum, Adam, schedules — is a refinement of how big a step and in exactly which direction.

The refinements exist because the naive version has three specific problems: it is slow in narrow valleys, it stalls on plateaus, and the right step size differs wildly from parameter to parameter. Each named optimiser is an answer to one of those three.

The terms

Objective / loss / cost basic

The single number you are trying to make small. Loss is usually per-example, cost is the average over the batch, objective is the whole thing including regularisation.

If you cannot write your objective down, you do not have a machine-learning problem yet — you have a wish.

Gradient descent basic θ ← θ − η∇L

Step in the direction of steepest descent, which is the negative gradient. η is the learning rate.

Walking downhill in fog, one careful step at a time.

Batch / mini-batch / stochastic basic

How many examples you look at before each step. All of them (batch), a handful (mini-batch, what everyone actually does), or one (stochastic).

Polling the entire country before every decision, polling a thousand people, or asking one person on the street. The thousand-person poll is nearly as accurate and enormously cheaper.

Epoch vs step basic

A step is one parameter update. An epoch is one full pass over the training data.

LLM pretraining is usually reported in tokens and steps, not epochs, because you often see the data barely once.

Learning rate basic

Step size. Too small: training crawls or gets stuck. Too large: you overshoot the valley, bounce, and diverge to NaN.

The single most important hyperparameter. If you tune exactly one thing, tune this.

Schedule & warmup mid

Changing the learning rate over training — usually a short linear warmup from near zero, then a cosine decay down to a small final value.

Warming up a car engine before flooring it, then easing off as you arrive.

Momentum mid

Accumulate an exponentially-weighted average of past gradients and step along that instead of the raw gradient.

A heavy ball rolling downhill instead of a cautious walker. It powers through small bumps and ignores side-to-side jitter, because the oscillating components cancel in the average while the consistent downhill component accumulates.

RMSProp / Adam / AdamW mid

Give each parameter its own learning rate, scaled down for parameters whose gradients have been large and up for those whose gradients have been tiny.

A thermostat per room instead of one for the whole house.

AdamW is the default for transformers. Adam without the W has a subtly wrong weight decay — see the questions.

Gradient clipping mid

If the gradient's norm exceeds a threshold, rescale it down before stepping.

Standard in LLM and RNN training. It is the difference between one bad batch causing a loss spike and one bad batch destroying the run.

Constrained optimisation senior

Minimise something subject to a rule — "as small as possible, but the weights must not exceed this size" or "but the new policy must stay close to the old one".

Ridge and lasso, SVMs, PPO's trust region, fairness constraints, latency budgets. Most real production objectives are constrained; people just do not phrase them that way.

Lagrange multiplier senior λ

The trick that turns a constrained problem into an unconstrained one: add λ times the constraint to the objective, then solve normally. λ is the price of the constraint.

"You may spend as much as you like, but every euro costs you λ units of happiness." Set λ correctly and you will never want to overspend — the budget enforces itself.

Dual problem senior

Every minimisation has a shadow maximisation over the multipliers. Solving the dual solves the original when the problem is well-behaved (strong duality).

The SVM is always solved in its dual, which is what makes the kernel trick possible at all.

KKT conditions senior

The generalisation of "∇ = 0" to problems with inequality constraints. Four conditions; the memorable one is complementary slackness: either the constraint is tight, or its multiplier is zero. Never both non-zero.

You only feel the wall if you are pressed against it.

Laplace approximation senior

Fit a Gaussian to a distribution at its peak: centre it on the mode, and get the width from the curvature (the Hessian) there.

You cannot map the whole mountain, so you stand on the summit and measure how steeply it falls away in each direction. Steep in every direction → a narrow peak → you are confident. Gentle → a broad peak → you are not.

Cheap post-hoc uncertainty on an already-trained network, and the theoretical backbone of BIC and of Bayesian model comparison.

Learning rate — why it dominates everything

Drag the slider. There are three regimes and they are worth being able to name on sight from a loss curve alone:

The family tree of optimisers

Each one is the previous one plus a fix. Learn it as a chain and you can derive any of them from the one before:

  1. GD — use the true gradient. Correct, and far too slow: one step per pass over the entire dataset.
  2. SGD — estimate the gradient from a mini-batch. Noisier per step, vastly more steps per second, and the noise itself turns out to help escape saddles.
  3. + Momentum — average the gradients over time. Fixes slow progress in narrow ravines, where raw SGD ping-pongs across the valley rather than moving along it.
  4. + RMSProp — divide by a running average of gradient magnitude. Fixes the "one learning rate cannot suit every parameter" problem.
  5. Adam = momentum + RMSProp, plus a bias correction for the fact that both running averages start at zero and are therefore badly underestimated for the first few hundred steps.
  6. AdamW = Adam with weight decay decoupled from the gradient, which is the version that actually implements the regularisation people think they are getting.
The one thing people get wrong about Adam

Adam is not "the smart one that always wins". Well-tuned SGD with momentum still generalises better than Adam on many vision benchmarks. Adam's advantage is that it works acceptably across a very wide range of learning rates with almost no tuning — which is precisely what you want when a single training run costs six figures and you get one attempt.

Constrained optimisation — Lagrange, the dual, KKT

Everything so far minimised freely. Now the real version: minimise f(x) subject to g(x) ≤ 0.

The geometry is the whole insight, and it is one picture. Draw the contour rings of your loss. Draw the constraint region. The best allowed point is where a contour ring first touches the constraint boundary — and at the moment of touching, the two curves are tangent, which means their gradients point along the same line:

∇f = −λ ∇g at the optimum, the two gradients are parallel; λ is how many times bigger one is

So define the Lagrangian ℒ(x, λ) = f(x) + λ g(x), set all its derivatives to zero, and the constraint gets enforced automatically. A constrained problem in x has become an unconstrained problem in (x, λ).

What λ means, and this is the part interviewers press on: λ is the shadow price of the constraint — the rate at which your optimal loss would improve if you relaxed the constraint by one unit. Big λ: that constraint is really hurting you. λ = 0: the constraint is not binding at all, and you could delete it without changing the answer.

With inequality constraints you get the KKT conditions. Four of them, but you only need to recite them if pushed:

  1. Stationarity — ∇ℒ = 0 in x.
  2. Primal feasibility — g(x) ≤ 0, i.e. you obeyed the rule.
  3. Dual feasibility — λ ≥ 0. A constraint can only push you back inside, never pull you out.
  4. Complementary slackness — λ·g(x) = 0. Either you are pressed against the wall (g = 0) or the wall does not matter (λ = 0). Never both.

Condition 4 is where all the payoff is. In an SVM, it says that every training point either sits exactly on the margin (λ > 0 — a support vector) or is comfortably inside and has λ = 0, contributing nothing to the solution. That is why an SVM depends on a handful of points and ignores the rest. The sparsity is not a design choice; it falls out of complementary slackness.

The Laplace approximation

Bayesian inference wants a full posterior over weights. For any real network that integral is hopeless. Laplace's move is aggressively practical: assume the posterior is a Gaussian, centred at the mode you already found.

  • The mean is free — it is your trained MAP weights. You already have them.
  • The covariance is the inverse Hessian of the negative log-posterior at that point. Sharp curvature → small variance → confident. Flat → large variance → uncertain.

Why anyone cares: it bolts uncertainty onto an already-trained network with no retraining and no change to the architecture. Compare that to deep ensembles (train five models) or MC dropout (many forward passes at inference). The catch is that the Hessian is d×d, so real implementations approximate it — diagonal, Kronecker-factored (K-FAC), or last-layer-only — and the whole thing is only as good as the single-mode assumption, which for a deep network with many equivalent minima is generous.

Say it this way

"Laplace approximation: put a Gaussian at the mode, get its width from the curvature. It is the cheapest honest uncertainty estimate you can get from a model you have already trained — and it is also where BIC's penalty term comes from."

Questions

BASICExplain gradient descent to someone non-technical.
You are on a foggy hillside trying to reach the valley. You cannot see far, but you can feel the slope, so you step downhill, feel again, and repeat.

Height is how wrong the model is. The slope is the gradient. The size of each step is the learning rate. Training is thousands of small downhill steps.

Two follow-ups worth pre-empting: we go in the negative gradient direction because the gradient points uphill by definition; and we use a mini-batch rather than the full dataset, which makes each step slightly wrong but hundreds of times faster — and the noise turns out to be useful rather than merely tolerable.

MIDSGD or Adam — which do you choose, and why?
Adam when I get few shots at tuning or the gradient scales vary wildly (transformers, sparse features); SGD with momentum when I can afford to tune and I want the last fraction of generalisation (vision).

Adam's real advantage is robustness, not speed. It normalises each parameter's step by that parameter's own gradient history, so it works acceptably across two orders of magnitude of learning rate. That is decisive when a pretraining run costs a fortune and you get one attempt.

SGD+momentum's advantage is generalisation. On CNN benchmarks a well-tuned SGD still beats Adam on test accuracy. The usual story is that Adam's per-parameter rescaling finds sharper minima; that explanation is contested, so I would state it as an observation with a plausible mechanism rather than a settled fact.

Practically: transformers, AdamW, cosine schedule with warmup, β₂ = 0.95 rather than 0.999 for large models. Vision from scratch, SGD+momentum, cosine. And in both cases the learning rate gets tuned before anything else does.

Trap

"Adam adapts the learning rate so I do not need to tune it." No. It changes the sensible range and makes the curve less sharp, but the peak still matters, and it still needs a schedule.

MIDWhat is AdamW, and why is decoupled weight decay not just cosmetic?
In plain Adam, weight decay is added to the gradient and then divided by the per-parameter scale — so parameters with large gradients get almost no decay. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, after the adaptive step, so every parameter is regularised equally.

The mechanism: L2 regularisation adds λw to the gradient. Adam then divides the whole gradient by √v, the running gradient magnitude. So the effective decay on each parameter is λw/√v — inversely proportional to how large that parameter's gradients have been. Exactly the parameters you most want to constrain get decayed least. The regularisation you configured is not the regularisation you got.

AdamW decouples them: adaptive step first, then w ← w − ηλw separately. Now the decay is uniform and its hyperparameter means what it says.

Why it matters in practice: this is why the sensible weight-decay values differ by roughly an order of magnitude between Adam and AdamW, and it is a real reason a paper fails to reproduce when someone swaps the optimiser and keeps the hyperparameters. It is also standard to exclude biases and LayerNorm gains from decay entirely — decaying a normalisation scale toward zero is just fighting the layer.

Trap

"L2 regularisation and weight decay are the same thing." They are identical for plain SGD. They are not identical for any adaptive optimiser, and that difference is the entire content of the AdamW paper.

MIDWhy do transformers need learning-rate warmup?
Because Adam's second-moment estimate is computed from almost no data in the first few steps, so its variance is huge — and a huge, badly-scaled early step lands the model somewhere it never recovers from.

Adam divides by √v̂, a running average of squared gradients. At step 1 that average has seen exactly one sample. The bias correction fixes its mean but not its variance, so early updates can be wildly over-sized. Warmup keeps η near zero while v̂ accumulates enough samples to be trustworthy.

Two contributing factors specific to transformers: with post-LN, gradients through the deep residual stack are badly scaled at initialisation, which is a large part of why pre-LN architectures are more forgiving and need less warmup; and attention logits early in training can be extreme, producing near-one-hot attention and correspondingly large gradients.

Typical practice: linear warmup over 1–2 % of total steps, then cosine decay to about 10 % of the peak. The symptom when you skip it is characteristic — a loss spike in the first few hundred steps that either recovers slowly or does not recover at all.

Trap

"So just use a smaller LR throughout?" That avoids the spike and then trains far too slowly for the remaining 99 % of the run. Warmup lets you have a small step where you need one and a large step everywhere else.

SENIORExplain Lagrange multipliers, and give a real ML example.
A Lagrange multiplier converts "minimise this subject to a rule" into "minimise this plus λ times the rule" — an unconstrained problem. λ is the price of the constraint: how much your objective would improve if you relaxed it by one unit.

The geometry: at the constrained optimum, the loss contour is tangent to the constraint boundary, so ∇f and ∇g are parallel. λ is the ratio between them. Form ℒ = f + λg, set the derivatives to zero, and the constraint enforces itself.

The example I would give is ridge regression, because it reframes something everyone already uses. "Minimise squared error subject to ‖w‖² ≤ t" has Lagrangian MSE + λ‖w‖². That λ is your regularisation strength. Every time you tune a regularisation coefficient you are choosing a Lagrange multiplier for a weight budget you never wrote down — and the correspondence between t and λ is one-to-one and monotonic, so tuning either is the same search.

The one I would give second is PPO, because it shows the idea running live in production: keep the updated policy within a KL ball of the reference model. Adaptive-KL PPO tunes the multiplier online — KL too high, raise β; too low, lower it. That is dual ascent, being run inside a training loop.

Trap

The sign convention on λ trips people up. For inequality constraints λ ≥ 0 is required — a constraint can push you back inside the feasible region but never pull you out of it.

SENIORWhat are the KKT conditions, and what does complementary slackness buy you?
KKT generalises "set the gradient to zero" to problems with inequality constraints. The condition that does the work is complementary slackness: λ·g(x) = 0 — either the constraint is active or its multiplier is zero, never both non-zero.

The four: stationarity (∇ℒ = 0), primal feasibility (you obeyed the constraints), dual feasibility (λ ≥ 0), and complementary slackness. For a convex problem satisfying a constraint qualification, they are necessary and sufficient — a KKT point is the global optimum.

What complementary slackness buys: sparsity, for free. In an SVM there is one multiplier per training point. Complementary slackness says every point either sits exactly on the margin, in which case its α is positive, or lies comfortably inside, in which case α = 0 and it contributes nothing to the decision boundary. So the model is defined by a small subset of the data — the support vectors. Delete every other training point and retrain: you get the identical boundary.

That is a genuinely useful property, not just theory. It is why SVM inference cost scales with the number of support vectors rather than the dataset size, and it is the same structural argument as coresets and active learning: most of your data is not doing anything.

Trap

KKT is only sufficient for convexity. On a neural network the conditions still characterise stationary points, but a KKT point tells you nothing about global optimality — and that is fine, because we do not expect global optimality there anyway.

SENIORWhat is the Laplace approximation and when would you actually reach for it?
Approximate the posterior with a Gaussian centred at the mode, with covariance equal to the inverse Hessian there. It is the cheapest way to get uncertainty out of a network you have already finished training.

Take the log-posterior, Taylor-expand it around the MAP estimate. The linear term vanishes because you are at a stationary point, so the leading behaviour is quadratic — and a quadratic log-density is a Gaussian. The curvature matrix is the precision; invert it for the covariance.

When I would reach for it: a model is already in production, someone asks for calibrated uncertainty or an out-of-distribution signal, and retraining five models for an ensemble is not on the table. Last-layer Laplace is close to free and measurably improves calibration.

When I would not: when the posterior is genuinely multimodal — which for a deep network it always is, given permutation symmetries alone — a single Gaussian at one mode is a strong claim. Deep ensembles usually beat it on real OOD benchmarks precisely because they capture several modes. And the exact Hessian is d×d, so anything at scale is diagonal, K-FAC, or last-layer-only.

Worth knowing that BIC's k⁄2·log n penalty comes straight out of this approximation applied to the marginal likelihood. Same mathematics, different use.

Trap

The Hessian must be positive-definite for this to give a valid Gaussian. At a real minimum of a real network it frequently is not, numerically — implementations use the Gauss–Newton or Fisher approximation, which is positive semi-definite by construction. Say that and you sound like someone who has run it rather than read about it.

MIDYour loss suddenly spikes to NaN at step 40,000. Walk me through the debugging.
Check for a bad batch first, then numerical overflow in mixed precision, then the learning rate. In that order, because that is the order of decreasing frequency.
  1. Isolate. Restart from the last checkpoint with the same seed and data order. Reproduces at the same step → data. Does not → nondeterminism, likely numerical.
  2. The batch. Log the batch index. Corrupted sample, an all-padding sequence, a label out of vocabulary range, a division by a zero-count feature. This is the most common cause by a wide margin.
  3. Precision. In fp16, attention logits or a loss term can overflow to inf, and inf − inf produces NaN. Check the loss scaler is not stuck at a huge value; bf16 — a 16-bit format that keeps fp32's range and gives up precision instead — makes this class of bug mostly disappear.
  4. Gradients. Log the global gradient norm every step. If it climbs over several steps before exploding, that is an optimisation instability rather than one bad example — clip, and lower the LR.
  5. Adam's state. If v̂ has collapsed toward zero for some parameters, the division blows up. Raising ε from 1e-8 to 1e-6 is a known mitigation for large models.
  6. Structural. A log or a sqrt of something that reached zero, or a log(softmax(x)) written by hand instead of log_softmax.

The prevention that costs nothing: gradient-norm logging on every run, gradient clipping on by default, and frequent enough checkpoints that a restart costs minutes rather than a day.
