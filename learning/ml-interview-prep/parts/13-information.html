Information theory Four quantities — surprise, entropy, cross-entropy, KL divergence — that turn up as your training loss, your evaluation metric, your RLHF penalty, and your production drift alarm. Learn them once here and you will recognise them four more times.

The problem this was invented to solve

Claude Shannon, 1948, at Bell Labs, was asked a purely engineering question: how few symbols can you send down a wire and still convey the message? To answer it he had to define information as a measurable quantity, and the definition he chose was:

The founding idea

Information is surprise. A message that tells you something you already knew carries none. “The sun rose this morning” — zero bits. “The sun did not rise this morning” — an enormous number of bits. The rarer the event, the more information its occurrence carries.

This turns out to be exactly what you need for machine learning, because training a model is compression: a model that predicts the next token well is a model that can encode the text in few bits. That is not a metaphor. The equivalence is exact, and it is why your loss is measured in nats.

The terms

Surprise / self-information basic −log p

How startling one particular outcome is. Certain event (p = 1) → 0 surprise. Impossible-ish event (p → 0) → surprise → ∞.

Being told the coin landed heads: mildly interesting. Being told it landed on its edge: a story you retell for years.

Bits vs nats basic

Just the base of the logarithm. log₂ gives bits (yes/no questions); ln gives nats. 1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits.

Your training loss is in nats, because frameworks use natural log. Language-model papers often quote bits-per-character instead. Divide by ln 2 to convert.

Entropy basic H(P) = −Σ p log p

The average surprise of a distribution — the irreducible uncertainty in it. Maximum when everything is equally likely, zero when one outcome is certain.

The average number of yes/no questions a perfect player needs in twenty questions. Eight equally likely answers → exactly 3 questions → 3 bits.

The theoretical floor on your loss. If the true next-token distribution has entropy 2.1 nats, no model on Earth gets below 2.1.

Cross-entropy basic H(P,Q) = −Σ p log q

The average surprise you actually experience when reality is P but you believed Q. Always at least H(P), equal only when Q = P.

You packed for the weather forecast Q. The weather was P. Cross-entropy is how uncomfortable you were on average.

This is your loss function. Every classifier, every language model.

KL divergence basic D(P‖Q) = Σ p log(p/q)

Cross-entropy minus entropy — the extra cost of using Q instead of the truth P. Always ≥ 0, zero only when the distributions match. Not symmetric, and not a distance.

The wasted part of your discomfort — the part that was your forecast's fault rather than the weather's inherent unpredictability.

The RLHF/PPO penalty that stops a policy drifting from the reference model. The KL term in a VAE. And one of the standard production drift statistics.

Perplexity basic exp(cross-entropy)

Cross-entropy exponentiated back into a count: the effective number of options the model is choosing between at each step.

Perplexity 20 means the model is as confused as someone picking uniformly at random from 20 words.

The standard language-model number. Only comparable between models that share a tokenizer — see the trap.

Conditional entropy mid H(Y|X)

The uncertainty left in Y once you already know X.

A language model's loss is exactly this: the entropy of the next token given everything before it.

Mutual information mid I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X)

How much knowing X reduces your uncertainty about Y. Symmetric, zero exactly when they are independent, and it catches non-linear dependence that correlation misses entirely.

Feature selection that survives non-linearity. And under its other name — information gain — it is the split criterion in a decision tree.

Jensen–Shannon divergence senior

The symmetric, bounded repair of KL: average both directions against the midpoint distribution M = (P+Q)/2. Finite even when the supports do not overlap.

The original GAN objective is JS divergence — and the fact that it saturates when the distributions do not overlap is precisely the vanishing-gradient problem that Wasserstein GANs were built to fix.

Why the logarithm?

Interviewers ask this, and “because Shannon said so” is not an answer. There are three requirements you would want from any measure of information, and the log is the only function that satisfies all three:

  1. Certainty carries no information. p = 1 must give 0. And log 1 = 0. ✓
  2. Rarer means more informative — the function must decrease in p. ✓
  3. Independent information adds. Learning two unrelated facts should give you the sum of their information. But independent probabilities multiply. You need a function that turns multiplication into addition, and that is the defining property of the logarithm.

Requirement 3 is the whole answer. Information is additive; probability is multiplicative; log is the bridge. As a bonus, it is also what makes cross-entropy numerically tractable — products of ten thousand small probabilities underflow to zero, sums of their logs do not.

Cross-entropy, KL, and the one relationship to remember

H(P, Q) = H(P) + DKL(P ‖ Q) what you pay = what was unavoidable + what your model wasted

Read it and several things fall out at once:

  • Minimising cross-entropy is minimising KL. H(P) is a property of the data and does not depend on your parameters, so it is a constant during training. The only thing your optimiser can move is the KL term. Training a classifier is minimising the KL from the truth to your model — people just do not usually say it that way.
  • Your loss has a floor and it is not zero. If the data genuinely contains ambiguity — and language always does — H(P) > 0 and no model reaches below it. A training loss approaching zero on non-trivial data is evidence of memorisation or leakage, not of a great model.
  • KL ≥ 0 always (Gibbs' inequality). So cross-entropy is always at least entropy. You can never be less surprised than reality warrants.

KL is not a distance, and the asymmetry is load-bearing

D(P‖Q) ≠ D(Q‖P), and it also fails the triangle inequality, so calling it a distance is wrong. Worse, the asymmetry is not a technicality — the two directions produce qualitatively different models.

Forward KL — D(P‖Q)Reverse KL — D(Q‖P)
Weighted bythe truth Pyour model Q
PunishesQ ≈ 0 where P > 0 — assigning no probability to something real Q > 0 where P ≈ 0 — inventing probability where there is nothing
Resulting behaviourMode-covering. Q spreads out to cover every mode of P, including the gaps between them Mode-seeking. Q collapses onto one mode and ignores the rest
You meet it inMLE, ordinary supervised training, distillation on the full distribution Variational inference, VAEs, the PPO/RLHF penalty

The intuition worth carrying: forward KL is “never say impossible about something that happens”, because P weights the sum, so any point where P is large and Q is tiny produces a huge penalty. Reverse KL is “never claim something that does not happen”, because Q weights the sum, so Q can safely ignore whole regions of P as long as it puts no mass there. A generative model trained with reverse KL produces fewer but cleaner samples; forward KL produces blurry averages that cover everything. That is the same trade-off as “blurry VAE reconstructions” and it comes from this single choice of direction.

Questions

BASICWhat is entropy, in plain language?
The average amount of surprise in a distribution — equivalently, the average number of yes/no questions needed to pin down the outcome.

Eight equally likely outcomes: you binary-search, three questions, 3 bits. One outcome guaranteed: zero questions, 0 bits. A skewed distribution sits in between, because you can ask about the likely outcomes first and usually finish early.

Formally it is the expected value of −log p, which is why it is a sum of p·log p terms.

Where it earns its keep in my work: it is the floor on a model's achievable loss. If the true next-token distribution has entropy 2.1 nats, nothing gets below 2.1, and knowing that stops you chasing a loss target that is physically impossible.

Trap

Entropy is a property of the distribution, not of a sample. "This dataset has high entropy" is loose talk unless you say which distribution over what.

BASICWhat is the difference between cross-entropy and KL divergence?
Cross-entropy is the total cost of using the wrong distribution; KL is just the excess part — cross-entropy minus the entropy that was unavoidable anyway.

H(P,Q) = H(P) + D(P‖Q). Since H(P) belongs to the data and not to your parameters, it is constant during training. So minimising cross-entropy and minimising KL are the same optimisation — they differ by a constant that no optimiser can touch.

Why we still bother with both: cross-entropy is the practical loss because you never know H(P). KL is the right quantity when you are comparing two distributions you do both have, which is why the RLHF penalty and drift detection use KL and not cross-entropy.

Trap

"So loss should go to zero?" Only if the data is deterministic. On real text H(P) is substantially above zero, so a near-zero training loss is a symptom — memorisation, duplicated data, or leakage.

MIDWhy a logarithm? Why not just use 1/p or (1−p)?
Because information from independent events has to add, while their probabilities multiply — and the logarithm is the unique function that converts one into the other.

Write down the three properties you want: zero information at p = 1; more information for rarer events; and additivity for independent events, so I(p·q) = I(p) + I(q). That functional equation forces a logarithm up to a constant, and the constant is just your choice of units — base 2 gives bits, base e gives nats.

1/p fails additivity: it multiplies instead of adding. (1−p) fails it too, and also gives finite information to an impossible event, which is wrong.

There is a practical bonus that matters daily: the likelihood of a dataset is a product of thousands of terms below 1, which underflows to exactly 0 in float32. Logs turn that into a sum that is numerically fine. That is why every framework works in log-space and why log_softmax exists as a fused op rather than a log applied to a softmax.

Trap

Never compute log(softmax(x)) yourself. The softmax can underflow to 0 and the log then gives −inf. log_softmax uses the log-sum-exp trick with a max subtraction and is stable.

MIDWhat does perplexity mean, and what counts as a good value?
Perplexity is the exponential of cross-entropy — the effective number of equally likely options the model is choosing between at each token.

Perplexity 20 means the model is about as uncertain as someone picking uniformly among 20 words. Perplexity 1 would be perfect certainty; perplexity equal to the vocabulary size means the model has learned nothing at all, which is exactly why the step-0 sanity check is loss ≈ ln(vocab_size).

Rough anchors: word-level perplexity on English news was ~200 for a good n-gram model, ~60 for early LSTMs, and modern LLMs sit in the low teens or below on comparable data. But the number alone is close to meaningless without context.

The critical caveat, which is really the question being asked: perplexity is only comparable across models with the same tokenizer and the same test set. A model with a larger vocabulary packs more characters into each token, so it makes fewer predictions over the same text and gets a lower perplexity for free. Comparing a 32k-vocab model to a 100k-vocab model on perplexity is meaningless. If you must compare across tokenizers, normalise to bits per character or bits per byte.

Trap

Low perplexity does not mean the model is useful. It measures next-token prediction, not instruction-following, factuality or helpfulness. Instruction tuning frequently makes perplexity on raw text worse while making the model far more useful — which is the whole reason LLM evaluation moved to task benchmarks and human preference.

MIDWhat is mutual information, and how does it differ from correlation?
Mutual information is how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other — and unlike correlation it detects any dependence, not just linear ones.

I(X;Y) = H(Y) − H(Y|X). Zero exactly when X and Y are independent, which is a much stronger statement than zero correlation.

The canonical demonstration: let Y = X² with X symmetric around zero. The correlation is exactly 0, because the linear relationship genuinely is absent. But knowing X tells you Y precisely, so the mutual information is large. Any feature-selection procedure ranking by correlation would discard that feature.

Under the name information gain it is the split criterion in a decision tree: the reduction in label entropy achieved by splitting on a feature. That is the same quantity, computed per candidate split.

The catch is estimation. On continuous variables MI requires density estimation or binning, both of which are unreliable in high dimensions and sensitive to bin width, which is why correlation stays popular despite being weaker.

Trap

MI is not bounded above and has no natural scale, so "MI = 0.4" is uninterpretable on its own. Normalised variants exist for exactly that reason.

SENIORExplain forward versus reverse KL and why the direction changes what you get.
Forward KL is mode-covering and reverse KL is mode-seeking, because whichever distribution sits in front of the log is the one whose regions get penalised.

Forward, D(P‖Q): the expectation is taken under the true P. Wherever P has mass and Q does not, log(p/q) blows up. So Q is forced to put probability everywhere P does — it spreads out and covers all modes, including the low-density gaps between them. This is what MLE does, and it is why a Gaussian fitted to a bimodal distribution lands in the middle where neither mode is.

Reverse, D(Q‖P): the expectation is taken under Q. Regions where Q is zero cost nothing at all, no matter what P does there. So Q can safely ignore entire modes; what it cannot do is put mass where P has none. It collapses onto one mode and represents it well.

Where it shows up concretely:

  • Supervised training and distillation use forward KL — you want the student to reproduce the whole teacher distribution.
  • VAEs use reverse KL in the ELBO, which is a known cause of posterior collapse and of blurry, over-smoothed reconstructions.
  • RLHF/PPO penalises KL against the reference policy to stop the model drifting into gibberish that games the reward model. The direction and the estimator matter: implementations typically use a sampled estimate of the reverse direction, and getting the sign or the estimator wrong is a real, hard-to-spot bug.
Trap

If asked which is "correct" — neither. It is a modelling choice about which failure you can live with: covering everything badly, or covering one thing well. Say that and you have answered the actual question.

SENIORWhere does KL divergence appear in a production ML system?
In four places that look unrelated until you notice they are all the same quantity: the training loss, the RLHF penalty, knowledge distillation, and drift detection.
  • Training. Cross-entropy = entropy + KL, so every classifier you have ever trained was minimising a KL you did not name.
  • RLHF / GRPO. A KL term against the frozen reference policy is what keeps the tuned model from collapsing into reward-hacked nonsense. The coefficient is one of the few genuinely important knobs in the whole pipeline: too small and the model degenerates, too large and it never learns anything.
  • Distillation. The student matches the teacher's full soft distribution, which is a forward KL. The "dark knowledge" argument is that the teacher's relative probabilities over the wrong classes carry more information than the hard label — and KL is what transfers it.
  • Drift detection. Bin a feature's distribution today against a training-time reference and compute the divergence. In practice most teams use PSI, which is the symmetrised KL — Σ(a−b)·ln(a/b) — precisely so that it does not blow up when one side has an empty bin. That is a nice thing to point out, because it shows you know why PSI exists rather than just its threshold.

The practical warning that ties them together: KL is infinite when the second distribution assigns zero to something the first does not. Every real implementation therefore smooths, clips, or drops empty bins, and how you handle that choice changes your alert rate materially.

Trap

PSI's famous thresholds — 0.1 "moderate", 0.25 "significant" — come from credit-scoring folklore, not from theory, and they depend on your binning scheme. Quote them as conventions and say you would calibrate against your own historical drift, not as if they were laws.
