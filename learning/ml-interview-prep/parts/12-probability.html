Probability & statistics Machine learning is not the science of getting the right answer. It is the science of reasoning with incomplete information. A probability model describes how likely different outcomes are; a well-calibrated prediction of 30% should come true about 30% of the time.

The problem this was invented to solve

You cannot predict whether this transaction is fraud. Nobody can. The information simply is not there — two customers with identical features will behave differently, and you will never see the variable that separates them.

So the goal changes. Instead of “is it fraud”, the question becomes “out of all the transactions that look exactly like this one, what fraction turn out to be fraud?” That number — 0.03, say, for one particular transaction's own score — is something you genuinely can learn, and it is something you can act on: block above 0.9, review between 0.3 and 0.9, allow below 0.3.

This reframing is the whole of probability's role in ML. Uncertainty is not a defect in the model to be engineered away. It is the honest output.

Take one concrete case — the transaction this whole page will keep coming back to. Nothing about its numbers is arbitrary: each one is exactly the kind of pattern a fraud model is built to notice.

Separately from any single transaction's score, the whole transaction stream has a long-run rate: about 1 in 200 transactions (0.5%) turns out to be fraud. Call this the base rate. It is not this transaction's probability of being fraud — it is the average over everyone. Sections 12.7 and 12.9 use exactly this number, together with how well the model's flag tells fraud from non-fraud, to work out how much higher than 0.5% this transaction's own probability should be.

A random variable: is this transaction fraud?

WhatA quantity whose value depends on an outcome you have not seen yet, but which follows a known distribution — not lawless, just unresolved.
WhyIt lets you write down and reason about “is this fraud” as a mathematical object, before you know the answer.
Where in MLEvery model output, every label, every feature is a random variable before it is observed.

Write Y for “is this transaction fraud”: Y = 1 if it is, Y = 0 if it is not. Before you investigate, Y is unresolved — it could be either value. That is what makes it a random variable: not “random” in the sense of lawless, but unresolved, with some values more likely than others.

?The simplest possible random variable

A die roll is the cleanest version of the same idea: six faces, one number written on each, and until it lands you only know the six possibilities and how likely each one is. Step through the figure below — the die does not become “random” after it stops moving; the outcome was always going to be one specific face, you simply did not know which until you looked.

Interview takeawayA random variable is not mysterious — it is a label for “a number whose value you don't know yet, but whose possible values and their likelihoods you can describe.”

The distribution behind it: PMF and PDF

WhatThe full accounting of which values are how likely — a probability mass function (PMF) for discrete outcomes, a probability density function (PDF) for continuous ones.
WhyKnowing only the most likely value throws away exactly the information a threshold-based decision needs.
Where in MLEvery classifier output, every generative model, every continuous feature.
?Why the fraud label and the transaction amount need two different kinds of distribution

Y (fraud or not) only ever takes two values, so its distribution is a PMF: an actual probability for each value, summing to 1 — P(Y=1) + P(Y=0) = 1.

The transaction amount is different: it can be any positive number, so “the probability the amount is exactly €1,240.000…” is meaningless — it is zero, the same way no dart lands on an infinitely thin line. A PDF instead gives a density, and only an interval of amounts has a nonzero probability, equal to the area under the density curve over that interval. A density can exceed 1 as long as the total area is 1 — a PDF that is 0.02 per euro over a €0–€50 range still integrates to 1.

Interview takeawayDiscrete outcomes get real probabilities that sum to 1; continuous outcomes get a density that integrates to 1, and any single exact value has probability zero.

Expectation: turning a probability into a decision

WhatThe long-run average value of a random variable — every possible value weighted by how likely it is.
WhyA single probability is not a decision by itself; multiplying it by a stake turns it into one.
Where in MLTraining loss is an expectation over the data distribution; expected cost drives thresholds like block/review/allow.
E[loss] = P(fraud) × (amount lost if fraud) + P(not fraud) × 0
for a typical €200 transaction: E[loss] = 0.005 × €200 + 0.995 × €0
= €1.00 expected loss per transaction, if you approved everything with zero fraud checks
This is why a 0.5% base rate is not “basically zero”: multiplied across millions of transactions, and against the much larger amounts a fraud ring will actually attempt, that average adds up to real money — which is the entire reason a fraud model gets built.
what each symbol is P(fraud)the base rate from 12.1 — 0.005 E[loss]the expectation — the average loss per transaction if you ran this policy forever
Interview takeawayExpectation is how a probability becomes money: multiply the probability of the bad outcome by its cost, and you have a number a business can act on.

Variance and standard deviation: how spread out are the scores

WhatVariance is the mean squared distance from the mean; the standard deviation is its square root, back in the original units.
WhyTwo batches with the same average score can look completely different — one confidently separated, one uncertain everywhere.
Where in MLBatch normalisation, the bias–variance discussion, and reading what a model's output distribution is actually telling you.
four transactions scored: 0.0, 0.0, 0.8, 0.8
μ = (0.0 + 0.0 + 0.8 + 0.8) / 4 = 0.4
deviations: −0.4, −0.4, +0.4, +0.4 → squared: 0.16, 0.16, 0.16, 0.16
Var = (0.16 × 4) / 4 = 0.16　·　σ = √0.16 = 0.4
Every score sits 0.4 away from the mean — the model is confidently splitting these four into “clearly fine” and “clearly suspicious”, not hedging near the middle. A batch with the same mean 0.4 but every score close to 0.4 would have low variance and be far less useful, even at the same average.
what each symbol is μmu — the mean score in the batch Varvariance — the mean squared distance from μ σsigma — the standard deviation, √Var, back in score units
Interview takeawayHigh variance among a batch of scores is not automatically bad — for a well-separated classifier it means confident, decisive scoring rather than uniform hedging.

Covariance and correlation: do two features move together

WhatCovariance asks whether two variables rise and fall together; correlation rescales it into [−1, 1] so it is unit-free and comparable.
WhyTwo features that always move together carry a lot of the same information — worth knowing before you build a rule that treats them as separate evidence.
Where in MLFeature selection, the covariance matrix behind PCA, and the independence assumption in 12.8.
four transactions: amount = [20, 25, 90, 105] €　·　merchant-mismatch flag = [0, 0, 1, 1]
μamount = 240/4 = 60　·　μmismatch = 2/4 = 0.5
deviations amount: −40, −35, 30, 45　·　deviations mismatch: −0.5, −0.5, 0.5, 0.5
Cov = [(−40)(−0.5) + (−35)(−0.5) + (30)(0.5) + (45)(0.5)] / 4 = 75/4 = 18.75
σamount ≈ 37.91　·　σmismatch = 0.5
correlation = 18.75 / (37.91 × 0.5) ≈ 0.99
In this small sample the two features rise together almost perfectly: a category mismatch essentially only happens on the larger amounts. That is exactly why a fraud rule bundles “large amount” and “category mismatch” into one flag rather than scoring them as two unrelated pieces of evidence.
what each symbol is Covcovariance — average product of the two deviations from their means σstandard deviation of one variable, from 12.5 correlationCov divided by both standard deviations — unit-free, always between −1 and 1
correlated features → a table of pairwise covariances → its eigenvectors (module 10) → the directions PCA keeps
?Correlated marginally is not the same as independent given the class

Amount and merchant-mismatch are correlated overall, as just computed. A naive-Bayes-style fraud rule instead assumes something narrower: that, given whether a transaction is fraud or not, the features stop telling you anything about each other. That narrower assumption is module 23's territory and is almost never exactly true — the point for now is just that “correlated” and “conditionally independent given the label” are two different questions, and it is easy to conflate them.

A real fraud model with many correlated features can also use the covariance matrix's eigenvectors — introduced in module 10 — to compress those features into fewer, less redundant directions before scoring. That is PCA, and module 10 §10.9–10.10 already derived it in full.

Interview takeawayCovariance tells you two features move together; it does not by itself tell you whether they are independent given the class, which is the assumption naive Bayes actually needs.

Joint, marginal and conditional probability

WhatJoint P(A,B): both happen. Marginal P(A): sum the joint over everything else. Conditional P(A|B): the probability of A once B is already known.
WhyThese three questions about the same data give three different, useful numbers — and conflating them is the single most common probability mistake in ML.
Where in MLEvery confusion matrix, every precision/recall pair, and the base-rate reasoning in 12.9.

Imagine — this is a thought experiment, not something you could actually observe — that you could see the true outcome for 200,000 transactions from a period where the model was also run, so you know both whether each one was really fraud and whether the model's amount + timing + merchant rule flagged it. Given the 0.5% base rate, a model that is 95% sensitive (catches 95% of real fraud) and has a 0.5% false-positive rate (wrongly flags 0.5% of legitimate transactions), the counts sort into four cells:

200,000 transactionsflaggednot flaggedtotal
fraud950501,000
legitimate995198,005199,000
total1,945198,055200,000
Joint: P(fraud AND flagged) = matching cell / all = 950 / 200,000 = 0.00475
Marginal: P(flagged) = flagged total / all = 1,945 / 200,000 = 0.009725
Marginal: P(fraud) = fraud total / all = 1,000 / 200,000 = 0.005 — the base rate from 12.1, recovered from the table
Conditional: P(fraud | flagged) = joint / P(flagged)'s row = 950 / 1,945 ≈ 0.488
Joint picks one cell. Marginal adds across a whole row or column, “integrating out” the other variable. Conditional restricts to one row or column first, so the denominator shrinks from 200,000 to just the 1,945 flagged transactions. Section 12.9 gets this same 0.488 a second way — from only the base rate and the model's two error rates, without needing this full table.
what each symbol is P(A, B)joint — the probability both happen P(A)marginal — the probability of A alone, ignoring B P(A|B)conditional — the probability of A, restricted to cases where B is known to be true
Interview takeawayConditioning shrinks the denominator to only the cases where the condition holds — that single move is the difference between P(flagged|fraud) and P(fraud|flagged), and confusing the two is the mistake in 12.9.

Independence — and when it clearly fails

WhatKnowing B tells you nothing about A: P(A|B) = P(A), equivalently P(A,B) = P(A)P(B).
WhyIt is the assumption that makes many calculations tractable — and checking it against real numbers, rather than assuming it, is a large part of doing this well.
Where in MLNaive Bayes, and any calculation that multiplies probabilities together.

Are “fraud” and “flagged” independent, in the 200,000-transaction table above? Independence would require P(fraud | flagged) = P(fraud). From 12.7: P(fraud | flagged) ≈ 0.488, but P(fraud) = 0.005. These are wildly different, so the two events are strongly dependent — which is exactly the point of building the flag in the first place. If flagging told you nothing about fraud, the model would be worthless.

?What independence looks like when it actually holds

Two fair coin flips are the cleanest case: learning the first is heads tells you nothing about the second. The figure below shows this directly — removing the “first coin is tails” half of the table still leaves the second coin exactly half heads. Compare that to the fraud table, where restricting to “flagged” changes the fraud rate by a factor of almost 100.

Interview takeawayIndependence is a testable claim, not a default assumption — compare P(A|B) to P(A); if they differ, the variables are dependent, and dependence is often the whole reason a signal is useful.

Bayes' theorem — and the mistake almost everyone makes

WhatA rule for reversing a conditional probability: turning “how likely is the evidence, given the cause?” into “how likely is the cause, given the evidence?”.
WhyYou almost always know P(flag | fraud) — a model's measured sensitivity — but the question you actually care about is P(fraud | flag).
Where in MLNaive Bayes, MAP estimation, and any base-rate reasoning about a rare event and an imperfect test.

In 12.7, seeing the full 200,000-row table made P(fraud | flagged) a matter of reading off one cell and one row total. In practice you rarely have that table — you were never able to inspect every transaction's true outcome. What you do have is the base rate and the model's two measured error rates from a validation set: P(fraud) = 0.005, P(flag | fraud) = 0.95, P(flag | not fraud) = 0.005. Bayes' theorem gets you to the same 0.488 from just those three numbers.

P(fraud | flag) = P(flag | fraud) · P(fraud) / P(flag) provided P(flag) is not zero
P(flag) = P(flag|fraud)·P(fraud) + P(flag|not fraud)·P(not fraud)
= (0.95 × 0.005) + (0.005 × 0.995) = 0.00475 + 0.0049750 = 0.0097250
This is the same 0.009725 the table's flagged column gave in 12.7 — the formula reconstructs the marginal without needing the table.
P(fraud | flag) = (0.95 × 0.005) / 0.0097250 = 0.00475 / 0.0097250
= 0.4884 ≈ 48.8%
Same answer as 12.7's 950/1,945, reached without ever needing the true outcome of all 200,000 transactions — only the base rate and two error rates a validation set actually gives you.
what each symbol is P(fraud)the prior — 0.5%, before this transaction's flag is considered P(flag|fraud)the likelihood — how often the model catches real fraud, 95% P(flag)the marginal — how often anything gets flagged at all, fraud or not P(fraud|flag)the posterior — the updated probability once you know it was flagged

Here is the mistake almost everyone makes: hearing “95% sensitive, 99.5% specific” and assuming a flagged transaction is about 95% likely to be real fraud. It is 48.8% — barely better than a coin flip, on a genuinely good model. The reason is the base rate: fraud is rare enough that the 0.5% false-positive rate, applied to the huge pool of 199,000 legitimate transactions, produces almost as many false alarms (995) as the 95% sensitivity produces true catches (950) out of a much smaller fraud pool. Rare events plus an imperfect test means false positives swamp true positives — this is the arithmetic behind “what is the base rate?” being the first question in any fraud, spam, or medical-screening design discussion.

?A smaller version of the same arithmetic

The figure below runs the identical shape of calculation on 100 spam-filtered messages instead of 200,000 transactions, small enough to check every step by hand: a 10% base rate for spam, and a word that appears in 80% of spam but also 10% of normal mail. The base-rate-swamps-the-test mechanism is exactly the one above — only the numbers are small enough to verify without a calculator.

Interview takeawayP(flag|fraud) and P(fraud|flag) are different questions, and the base rate is what separates them — a 95%-sensitive test on a 0.5% base rate still leaves you under 50% confident in a single flag.

Prior, likelihood, posterior — naming what Bayes just did

WhatPrior: what you believed before the evidence. Likelihood: how well a candidate explanation fits the evidence you saw. Posterior: the updated belief afterwards.
WhyThese three names for the three pieces of Bayes' theorem let you talk about updating any belief, not only “fraud or not”.
Where in MLMAP estimation (next), Bayesian hyperparameter search, and any penalty term that can be read as a prior preference.

12.9 already used all three, just without naming them: P(fraud) = 0.005 was the prior; P(flag | fraud) = 0.95 and P(flag | not fraud) = 0.005 were the two likelihoods — how well each candidate explanation (“it was fraud” vs. “it wasn't”) predicts the observed flag; and P(fraud | flag) = 0.488 was the posterior — belief, updated.

The figure above runs the same update on two competing explanations directly — “this coin is fair” versus “this coin is biased toward heads” — after seeing two heads in a row, so you can watch a prior belief get multiplied by a likelihood and renormalised, the same three moves Bayes' theorem made in 12.9.

a posterior distribution over possibilities → its single most likely value → maximum a posteriori (MAP)
Interview takeawayA likelihood is a score for how well an explanation fits the data — it is not itself a probability over explanations; only after multiplying by a prior and renormalising does it become one.

Maximum likelihood: where the 0.5% base rate came from

WhatPick the parameter value that makes the data you actually observed as probable as possible.
WhyThe 0.5% base rate used since 12.1 was not handed down — it has to be estimated from real audits, and MLE is the estimate you get by asking “what rate would make what I saw most likely?”.
Where in MLEstimating any unknown rate or parameter from observed data, and — as the deep dive below shows — training a classifier's weights.

The fraud team hand-audited 600 transactions and found 3 fraud. For an assumed fraud rate θ, and treating each audited transaction as one independent draw at that same rate — the assumption 12.13 will shortly name i.i.d. — the probability of seeing exactly 3 fraud out of 600 is θ³(1−θ)⁵⁹⁷. Maximum likelihood asks: which θ makes that number largest?

log-likelihood → its derivative (module 11) → set the slope to zero → the maximum-likelihood estimate
L(θ) = θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ　　ln L(θ) = k ln θ + (n−k) ln(1−θ)
d/dθ [ln L(θ)] = k/θ − (n−k)/(1−θ)
set to 0: k/θ = (n−k)/(1−θ)  ⇒  k(1−θ) = θ(n−k)  ⇒  k = nθ  ⇒  θ = k/n
θMLE = 3/600 = 0.005
This is exactly the 1-in-200 base rate assumed since 12.1 — now you know where it came from: it is the observed fraction, and that fraction is provably the value of θ that maximises the likelihood of having seen 3 fraud in 600 audits.
what each symbol is θthe unknown true fraud rate — what MLE is solving for kfraud cases found — 3 ntransactions audited — 600 L(θ)the likelihood — how probable this exact outcome is, as a function of θ
?The same argmax-likelihood idea trains the model itself

MLE is not only for estimating one rate — it is also exactly how the fraud classifier's own weights get trained. Assuming the training examples are independent, the probability of the whole training set is the product of each example's probability under the model. Products of thousands of small numbers underflow to zero in floating point, so take a log to turn the product into a sum; maximising a sum of logs is the same as minimising its negative.

maximise ∏ P(yᵢ | xᵢ; w) ⟺ maximise Σ log P(yᵢ | xᵢ; w) ⟺ minimise −Σ log P(yᵢ | xᵢ; w) the last expression is cross-entropy loss

Cross-entropy is not a separately-invented formula — it is negative log-likelihood for a fraud/not-fraud (Bernoulli) label, the exact same argmax-likelihood move used above for one parameter, now applied across every training transaction and every weight at once. The mini-batch training in 12.16 minimises exactly this sum.

Interview takeawayMLE is “the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable” — for a simple rate it is just the observed fraction, and the identical argmax-likelihood logic, applied to model weights instead of one rate, is cross-entropy loss.

Maximum a posteriori: what to do with too little data

WhatChoose the parameter with the highest posterior probability — like MLE, but the likelihood is multiplied by a prior first.
WhyMLE trusts the data completely, which fails badly when there isn't much of it.
Where in MLAny small-sample rate estimate, and — as the deep dive shows — the probabilistic reading of ridge and lasso regression.

A new merchant segment has only 20 audited transactions, and none were fraud. The MLE estimate is 0/20 = 0 — “fraud is impossible here” — which is an overconfident conclusion to draw from 20 data points, especially when every other segment sits around 0.5%.

?What a Beta prior actually says

A Beta distribution is not a distribution over data — it is a distribution over a probability itself, here the unknown fraud rate θ ∈ [0,1]. Beta(α, β) has mean α/(α+β) and, when both parameters exceed 1, a peak at (α−1)/(α+β−2). Choosing Beta(2, 398) encodes: “my best guess for this segment's rate is 2/400 = 0.5%, the same as everywhere else — but I am only as confident as if I had already seen 400 transactions' worth of evidence for that guess.”

posterior ∝ likelihood × prior = θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ × θ^(α−1)(1−θ)^(β−1)
= θ^(k+α−1) (1−θ)^(n−k+β−1)　　— the same shape as Beta(k+α, n−k+β)
mode = (k+α−1) / (k+α + n−k+β − 2)　　with k=0, n=20, α=2, β=398
θMAP = (0+2−1) / (2+418−2) = 1/418 ≈ 0.0024 (0.24%)
MLE said “0% — impossible here”; MAP says “about 0.24%, pulled toward the 0.5% everyone else sees, because 20 audits is not enough to override that prior.” Neither number is the truth — but MAP's is the one you would actually want to act on with this little data.
what each symbol is α, βthe Beta prior's two shape parameters, chosen above ∝“proportional to” — has the same shape up to a constant that does not affect where the peak is modethe single most likely value — what MAP reports
a Gaussian prior on a weight → a squared penalty term on the loss → ridge regression (module 10)
?Prove MAP-with-a-Gaussian-prior really is ridge, using module 10's own numbers

Module 10 §10.11 fit a junk feature's weight by least squares and got a = 0.3014, then applied an L2 penalty with α = 0.30 to get wL2 = 0.3014/1.30 = 0.2318. That penalty can be read as a Gaussian prior on the weight. With a Gaussian-noise likelihood, the unpenalised fit minimises (w−a)²; a zero-mean Gaussian prior with variance σ² adds a term proportional to w²/(2σ²) to that same objective.

minimise (w−a)² + w²/(2σ²)
d/dw: 2(w−a) + w/σ² = 0  ⇒  w(2 + 1/σ²) = 2a  ⇒  w = a / (1 + 1/(2σ²))
set 1/(2σ²) = α = 0.30  ⇒  w = 0.3014 / (1 + 0.30) = 0.2318
Exactly module 10's ridge answer — MAP with this Gaussian prior and ridge regression are not merely analogous, they are the identical minimisation, with the prior's variance fixing λ.

The Lasso side of the same idea uses a Laplace prior instead of a Gaussian one, giving a Σ|wᵢ| penalty rather than Σwᵢ² — module 10 §10.11 already worked out why that particular shape produces exact zeros and a Gaussian-shaped penalty does not.

Trap

MAP is still a single point estimate, not a full Bayesian posterior. It reports the peak — 0.24% here — and says nothing about how wide or narrow the posterior actually is around that peak. Getting a usable measure of that spread without computing the whole posterior exactly is what the Laplace approximation (module 14) is for: it fits a Gaussian to the posterior's shape right at the MAP peak, using the curvature there to estimate spread.

Interview takeawayMAP is MLE multiplied by a prior; a Gaussian prior on a weight is exactly ridge regression's penalty, and a Laplace prior is exactly lasso's — same objective, written two ways.

i.i.d. — and the fraud ring that breaks it

WhatIndependent and identically distributed: each sample drawn separately, from the same underlying distribution.
WhyIt is the assumption 12.11 leaned on without naming — “each audit is independent, at the same underlying rate” — and production breaks it constantly.
Where in MLTrain/test splits, most uncertainty calculations, and anywhere a model's confidence quietly stops meaning what it claims to mean.

12.11's derivation multiplied 600 individual probabilities together — valid only because each audited transaction's outcome was assumed independent of the others, and all 600 were assumed to share the same underlying rate. That two-part assumption now has a name: i.i.d.

Here is a concrete case where both halves fail at once. A stolen-card ring runs 40 test purchases within six minutes, across three compromised card numbers, at the same two online merchants, deliberately keeping each purchase under €50 to dodge amount-based flags. These 40 transactions are not independent — one coordinated actor is behind all of them, so learning that transaction #1 is fraud makes it far more likely #2 through #40 are too. Treating them as 40 independent draws, the way 12.11's arithmetic requires, overstates how much you have actually learned: the effective sample size here is closer to “one coordinated event” than “forty separate transactions”.

Production breaks the assumption in more ways than a fraud ring. Naming which half broke is a strong seniority signal:

What breaksLooks likeWhat it does to you
Not independent — time A random train/test split on time-series data puts tomorrow in train and today in test. Leakage. Offline metrics look wonderful and production is a disaster. Split by time, always.
Not independent — groups Multiple transactions from the same customer, or the same fraud ring, land on both sides of the split. You are measuring memorisation, not generalisation. Use grouped splits.
Not identical — covariate shift P(x) moves; the input mix changes. New country, new device, Christmas. Your model is extrapolating.
Not identical — concept drift P(y|x) moves; the same inputs now mean something different. Fraudsters adapt. The model is simply wrong now, and no amount of input monitoring alone will tell you — you need labels.
Feedback loops You block the transactions you predict are fraud, so you never learn their true label. The training distribution is now shaped by your own model. The nastiest one, because it is invisible in every metric.
?What PSI and a KS test actually check

Both ask the same covariate-shift question — “does the input look different now than when the model was trained?” — without needing any labels. PSI (population stability index) buckets a feature into ranges and compares the proportion of transactions falling in each bucket now versus at training time, then sums a weighted measure of that gap across buckets. A KS test instead compares the two periods' entire distributions directly, without binning first. Module 54 covers both properly; the idea to take away here is only that this class of check needs no labels, unlike concept drift.

Interview takeawayi.i.d. has two separate promises — independent, and identically distributed — and naming exactly which one broke, and whether it needs labels to detect, is what separates a senior answer from a vague “the data changed”.

The law of large numbers: how many audits are enough

WhatFor independent samples from the same distribution with a finite mean, the sample average converges to that true mean as the sample count grows.
WhyIt is the reason MLE's estimate gets more trustworthy with more data — and a formal name for what went wrong with the 20-audit segment in 12.12.
Where in MLWhy a large test set gives a trustworthy accuracy, and a 20-row audit does not.

12.12's 20-audit sample gave 0/20 = 0, wildly off from the true ≈0.5% rate most segments share — not because anything was done wrong, but because 20 i.i.d. draws of a rare event have not yet had a chance to converge. 12.11's 600-audit sample gave 3/600 = 0.005, already close to the rate every other segment shows. The law of large numbers is the formal promise behind that difference: more i.i.d. samples at the same rate, and the running average gets arbitrarily close to the truth. It says nothing about how fast that happens, and rare events — like a 0.5% fraud rate — converge slower than a 50/50 coin does, because most small samples contain zero occurrences at all.

Interview takeawayThe law of large numbers is why more data helps, but it says nothing about how much — for a rare event, “enough” audits is a much larger number than intuition from a fair-coin example suggests.

The central limit theorem: why averages go Gaussian

WhatFor independent samples from the same distribution with finite, nonzero variance, the average — centred and scaled — approaches a Gaussian shape, whatever the original distribution looked like.
WhyIt lets you run standard significance tests on metrics that are nowhere near bell-shaped themselves.
Where in MLA/B testing two fraud models against each other.

The dollar loss per confirmed fraud case is a nasty distribution: mostly small, with an occasional enormous case from a coordinated ring. Comparing two candidate fraud models by their average daily loss, over 30 days each, does not need that daily-loss distribution to be Gaussian — it needs the sampling distribution of the 30-day average to be approximately Gaussian, and the central limit theorem is exactly the guarantee that it will be, given enough independent days.

Interview takeawayA/B testing a skewed metric works because you are testing its mean, and means of many independent samples go Gaussian regardless of the underlying shape — but only if the underlying variance is finite and the samples are genuinely independent.

Stochastic: the noise that trains the model itself

WhatInvolving randomness — in training specifically, a gradient computed on a random mini-batch rather than the whole dataset.
WhyThe fraud classifier's own weights are fit this way, and the noise is not merely tolerated, it is often useful.
Where in MLEvery neural network and most large linear models are trained with stochastic gradient descent.
true gradient over every historical transaction → an expectation (12.4) → estimated from a random mini-batch → a noisy sample of that expectation

Training the fraud classifier means minimising the cross-entropy sum from 12.11's deep dive over every historical transaction. Computing that exactly every step is expensive, so instead a random mini-batch's average gradient is used as a stand-in — a sample estimate of the same expectation, not the thing itself.

Module 14 covers the optimiser mechanics — momentum, learning-rate schedules — in full; the point for this page is only that the noise is a byproduct of estimating an expectation from a sample, exactly as in 12.4, and that this noise can help escape flat, uninformative regions of the loss surface rather than only being a nuisance to tolerate.

Interview takeawayMini-batch noise is a sample estimate of the true expected gradient — cheaper to compute, and useful precisely because it is not identical every step.

The distributions worth knowing

WhatA handful of named shapes cover most of what shows up in ML: Bernoulli, Binomial, Categorical, Gaussian, Poisson, and — already met in 12.12 — Beta.
WhyRecognising which shape applies tells you which loss and which likelihood are the right ones, instead of guessing.
Where in MLBinary classification, batch counts, multiclass classification, continuous noise, and rare-event rates.
  • Bernoulli — one yes/no outcome with probability p. This is Y from 12.2: fraud or not, one transaction at a time.
  • Binomial — how many successes in n independent Bernoulli trials at the same rate. 12.11's likelihood, θᵏ(1−θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, is exactly this shape — the audit count of 3-out-of-600 is a Binomial observation.
  • Categorical — one outcome from several named options, generalising Bernoulli beyond two choices — for instance, sorting a flagged transaction into a fraud type (stolen card, account takeover, synthetic identity) rather than a plain yes/no.
  • Gaussian — the bell shape, what sums and averages of many small effects turn into, per the CLT in 12.15. Also a common noise model for a continuous feature like amount.
  • Poisson — counts of rare events in a fixed window, such as fraud attempts per hour on one merchant.
  • Beta — a distribution over a probability itself, already used in 12.12 as the MAP prior over the fraud rate θ.
Interview takeawayBinary classification is Bernoulli, so the loss is binary cross-entropy; multiclass is Categorical, so it is cross-entropy over several outputs — the loss follows from naming the right distribution first.

Bayes' theorem, stress-tested

The 48.8% in 12.9 was not a special property of fraud — it is a general fact about rare events and imperfect tests. The figure below is the same three-slider calculation (prevalence, sensitivity, specificity) on a general population; its defaults are not our numbers, so drag prevalence to 0.005, sensitivity to 0.95, and specificity to 0.995 to reproduce 48.8% exactly. Then push prevalence down toward the classic 1-in-1,000 medical-screening case, holding the same test quality, and watch the posterior collapse further — the rarer the event, the harder even a good test has to work.

Interview takeawayThe base-rate mistake is not fraud-specific — any rare-event screening problem (disease testing, spam filtering, anomaly detection) has the identical arithmetic, and the fix is always to ask for the base rate before trusting a single flag.

Calibration: does 0.8 mean 8-in-10

WhatA model is calibrated when, among all the transactions it scores 0.8, about 80% really are fraud.
WhyEvery threshold in 12.1 — block above 0.9, review 0.3–0.9, allow below 0.3 — only means what it claims to mean if the scores are calibrated.
Where in MLAny time a model's output is used as a probability rather than just a ranking.

The claim is checkable. Take four transactions the model scored and whose true outcome is now known: 0.9 (confirmed fraud), 0.5 (confirmed fraud), 0.5 (confirmed legitimate), 0.1 (confirmed legitimate). The two 0.5-scored cases split exactly 50/50 in reality — precisely what a calibrated 0.5 should mean.

Brier score = mean[(predicted − actual)²]
(0.9−1)² + (0.5−1)² + (0.5−0)² + (0.1−0)² = 0.01 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.01 = 0.52
Brier = 0.52 / 4 = 0.13
0 is perfect; always guessing 0.5 regardless of outcome gives 0.25. 0.13 is a genuinely informative model, not a perfectly calibrated one — the Brier score bundles calibration and sharpness together, so it alone can't tell you which is lacking.

A reliability diagram separates that out: bucket every scored transaction by predicted probability, and compare each bucket's mean prediction against its observed fraud rate.

bucket 0.6–0.8: 40 transactions, predicted mean 0.70, 22 confirmed fraud → observed 22/40 = 0.55, gap = 0.15
bucket 0.0–0.2: 500 transactions, predicted mean 0.10, 15 confirmed fraud → observed 15/500 = 0.03, gap = 0.07
ECE = (40 × 0.15 + 500 × 0.07) / (40 + 500) = (6 + 35) / 540 = 41/540 ≈ 0.076 (7.6%)
The 0.6–0.8 bucket is overconfident by 15 points — this model systematically overstates fraud probability in its middle range, which is exactly the kind of miscalibration that quietly breaks a 0.3–0.9 review threshold.
what each symbol is ECEexpected calibration error — the size-weighted average gap across buckets

Two standard fixes recalibrate a score without retraining the model:

Platt scaling parametric

What: fit a one-dimensional logistic regression that maps the raw score to a calibrated probability.

Use when: the miscalibration is roughly S-shaped and the calibration set is modest — the usual case for SVM-style scores.

Isotonic regression non-parametric

What: fit a flexible, non-decreasing step function mapping score to calibrated probability.

Use when: the miscalibration shape is unknown or irregular and there is enough held-out data to fit it.

?Which model families miscalibrate, and how
  • Modern neural networks are typically overconfident — accuracy has improved over the years while calibration has often gotten worse.
  • SVMs output distances, not probabilities, and need Platt scaling before the number means anything at all.
  • Random forests are pulled toward the middle, because averaging many trees rarely produces a unanimous 0 or 1.
  • Any resampling — oversampling fraud, undersampling legitimate transactions — deliberately changes the base rate the model sees, and therefore its calibration. Correct the intercept back afterwards, using the true base rate, or every downstream probability is wrong by a known, fixable amount.

Fit any of these recalibration methods on a held-out calibration set, never on the same data used to train the model.

This is why calibration is not academic: a calibrated posterior can be multiplied directly by the transaction value to get an expected loss, exactly as in 12.4. The flagged transaction from 12.1, with posterior 0.488 from 12.9 and value €1,240, has an expected loss if allowed of 0.488 × €1,240 ≈ €605 — comfortably inside the 0.3–0.9 “review” band from 12.1, and a number a fraud-review team can actually act on. An uncalibrated 0.488 would make that arithmetic meaningless, and every threshold built on top of it would be guesswork wearing the costume of a number.

Interview takeawayCalibration is a testable claim, not an assumption — bucket scores against outcomes, measure the gap with ECE or Brier, and only then trust a threshold or an expected-loss calculation built on the raw score.

Questions

BASICExplain Bayes' theorem to someone non-technical.
It is the arithmetic of updating a belief when evidence arrives — how strongly the evidence should move you depends on how rare the thing was to begin with.

Take the fraud example: a 0.5% base rate, a model that catches 95% of real fraud and wrongly flags 0.5% of legitimate transactions. Out of 200,000 transactions, 1,000 are fraud and 950 of those get flagged; 199,000 are legitimate and 995 of those get flagged anyway. So of 1,945 flagged transactions, only 950 are real fraud — about 48.8%.

You can get that number two ways that must agree: build the full table and read off 950/1,945, or — the way you'd actually do it in practice, without ever knowing every transaction's true label — use Bayes' formula with just the base rate and the model's two known error rates. Both give 48.8%.

The lesson is that P(flag | fraud) and P(fraud | flag) are different questions, and the base rate is what separates them. Rare things stay fairly unlikely even after suggestive evidence.

Trap

“95% sensitive, 99.5% specific” sounds like it should make a flag 95% trustworthy. It does not — sensitivity and precision are different quantities, and the gap between them is exactly the base rate. Asking “what fraction of flags are real?” rather than “how good is the test?” is itself the signal an interviewer is listening for.

BASICWhat is the difference between a PDF and a PMF?
A PMF gives actual probabilities for discrete outcomes and sums to 1; a PDF gives a density for continuous ones, integrates to 1, and any single exact point has probability zero.

“Is this transaction fraud” has two outcomes, so it gets a PMF: P(Y=1)=0.005, P(Y=0)=0.995, summing to 1. “What is the transaction amount” is continuous — asking for the exact probability the amount is €1,240.000000… is meaningless, it is zero. Only an interval of amounts, say €1,200–€1,300, has a nonzero probability, equal to the area under the density curve over that range.

Where it matters in practice: continuous likelihoods are densities, so a "likelihood" of 4.7 is not a bug — densities can exceed 1 as long as the total area under them is 1.

Trap

A density above 1 is not an error. If someone reports a probability of 3 for a continuous quantity, first ask whether it is actually a density.

MIDWhat is maximum likelihood estimation, and how does it connect to the loss that trains a classifier?
MLE picks the parameter that makes the observed data most probable — for a simple rate that's just the observed fraction, and applying the same argmax-likelihood logic to a classifier's weights gives you cross-entropy loss.

For the fraud rate itself: 3 fraud found in 600 independent audits gives a likelihood θ³(1−θ)⁵⁹⁷; setting its derivative to zero gives θ=k/n= 3/600=0.005 — the observed fraction, provably the maximiser.

Do the same thing across every training transaction and every weight: assume the examples are independent, take the log of the product of their probabilities to turn it into a sum, negate to get a minimisation, and for a fraud/not-fraud (Bernoulli) label the result is exactly cross-entropy loss. Do it instead with a Gaussian noise assumption on a continuous target and everything but the squared error becomes a constant — so MSE is MLE under Gaussian noise, which tells you exactly when it is the wrong choice: heavy-tailed targets, outliers, or asymmetric costs, where Gaussian is not the right noise model.

The practical version: choosing a loss is choosing a probabilistic model of your labels. Say what distribution the noise has, and the loss follows.

Trap

MLE is not unbiased in general — the MLE for a Gaussian's variance divides by n rather than n−1, because it measures spread around the same data used to estimate the mean, which slightly underestimates the true spread (dividing by n−1 corrects for this). With few samples, MLE also overfits hard — the 20-audit segment's 0/20 estimate is exactly that failure, and it is what the prior in MAP is there to prevent.

MIDMLE versus MAP — what is the difference, and where have you used it?
MAP is MLE multiplied by a prior — and that prior is exactly your regulariser.

MLE maximises P(data | θ). MAP maximises P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ)·P(θ). With 0 fraud found in 20 audits of a new merchant segment, MLE says the rate is 0 — implausible given every other segment sits near 0.5%. A Beta(2, 398) prior encoding that 0.5% belief pulls the estimate to (0+2−1)/(20+398+2−2) = 1/418 ≈ 0.24%, which is far more defensible with only 20 data points.

Two concrete equivalences worth knowing cold:

  • A zero-mean Gaussian prior on a weight gives a penalty proportional to w² — that is ridge / L2 / weight decay. Module 10's own junk-feature weight (0.3014, penalised with α=0.30 to 0.2318) checks out exactly under this algebra: solve MAP's optimum symbolically and it reduces to the identical ridge formula.
  • A zero-mean Laplace prior gives Σ|wᵢ| — that is lasso / L1, and its sharp peak at zero is the probabilistic reason it produces exact zeros.

Why I like framing it this way: it turns “what λ should I use?” from a grid-search question into a question about belief. λ → 0 means “I have no prior opinion, let the data decide” — fine with a million rows, reckless with twenty.

Trap

MAP is still a single point estimate, not a full posterior. It gives you the peak — 0.24% — and tells you nothing about the width around it, so it provides no uncertainty by itself. The Laplace approximation (module 14) fills that gap by fitting a Gaussian to the posterior's local curvature at the MAP peak.

MIDWhat does i.i.d. mean and where does it break in production?
Independent and identically distributed — and production breaks both halves routinely, which is where most silent model failures come from.

Independence goes first. A fraud ring running 40 coordinated purchases within six minutes is not 40 independent data points — one actor is behind all of them, and treating them as independent overstates how much you've learned. Time series has the same problem structurally: a random split puts the future in training and the past in test, and offline metrics become fiction; split by time instead.

Identical distribution goes second. Covariate shift means P(x) moved — a new market, a new device mix, a seasonal spike; you can detect it from inputs alone with PSI (compare bucketed proportions across two time windows) or a KS test (compare the two full distributions directly), with no labels needed. Concept drift means P(y|x) moved — the same inputs now imply a different label, the normal state of affairs in fraud since the adversary adapts — and that is invisible without labels.

And the worst one is the feedback loop. Block the transactions you score as fraud and you never observe their true outcome; your next training set is now shaped by your own previous decisions, and every offline metric looks fine while the model quietly narrows. The fix is deliberate: hold out a small random control group that bypasses the model, so you keep seeing unbiased labels.

Trap

Cross-validation assumes i.i.d. too. Standard k-fold on temporal data leaks the same way a random split does. Use forward-chaining / rolling-origin validation instead — train on a growing prefix of time, validate on the period immediately after it, then slide that split forward, rather than shuffling folds randomly across time.

MIDWhy is the noise in SGD useful rather than merely tolerable?
Because a mini-batch gradient is a noisy sample estimate of the true expected gradient, and that noise escapes flat regions and biases the search toward flatter minima.

Three effects, in increasing order of interest:

  • Escaping saddle points. Exactly at a saddle the true gradient is zero and full-batch descent stops. A noisy estimate is not zero, so training gets pushed off it. Given how much saddle points dominate in high dimensions, this alone matters.
  • Implicit regularisation. The noise acts like a temperature. Narrow, sharp minima are unstable under it and get bounced out of; wide flat basins retain the search. Flat minima empirically generalise better, so this sloppiness is doing free regularisation.
  • It is also just cheaper. A batch of 256 transactions gives a gradient estimate good enough to make progress at a fraction of the cost of scoring every historical transaction, so far more steps happen per unit of compute.

The consequence that shows up in practice: batch size and learning rate are coupled. Larger batches average away more of the noise, so a larger learning rate is needed to keep the same amount of exploration — scale the learning rate roughly with batch size, and ramp it up gradually over the first steps (warmup) rather than jumping straight to the larger rate, so early noisy updates don't destabilise training. Simply increasing batch size while holding the learning rate fixed typically costs generalisation, and that surprises people.

Trap

The flat-minima story is a well-supported heuristic, not a theorem — sharpness is not reparameterisation-invariant, and Dinh et al. showed you can rescale a network to make a minimum arbitrarily sharp without changing the function it computes. Say “empirically” and you sound like you have read the literature rather than a blog post.

SENIORYour fraud model reports 0.8 for a transaction. What does that number mean, and how would you check?
It should mean that among all transactions the model scores 0.8, about 80% are actually fraud — a testable claim called calibration, which most models fail out of the box.

The score is a claim about a reference class, not this one transaction. Bucket every scored transaction by predicted probability and compare each bucket's mean prediction against its observed fraud rate — that is a reliability diagram, summarised as expected calibration error or the Brier score. On a worked check earlier in this module, a 0.6–0.8 bucket predicted 0.70 on average but was observed at 0.55 — a 15-point overconfidence gap.

Fixes are cheap once you know it's miscalibrated: Platt scaling (fit a logistic on the raw scores) for roughly S-shaped miscalibration, or isotonic regression (more flexible, needs more data) for an irregular shape — fitted on a held-out calibration set, never on training data.

Why it is not academic: if the number is calibrated, multiplying it by the transaction value gives an expected loss, and the block threshold can be set from a cost matrix instead of a guess. Uncalibrated, that arithmetic is meaningless and every downstream business decision built on it is guesswork.

Trap

Calibration and discrimination are independent properties. A model that outputs the base rate 0.005 for every single transaction is perfectly calibrated on average and completely useless — AUC 0.5, no ability to separate cases at all. You need both, and saying so before being asked is the senior signal.

SENIORWhy does the central limit theorem let you A/B test a metric that is nowhere near normal?
Because you are not testing the metric itself — you are testing its mean, and means of many independent samples become approximately Gaussian whatever the underlying shape.

Loss per confirmed fraud case is a horrible distribution: mostly small, with an occasional enormous case from a coordinated ring. But the sampling distribution of its daily average across 30 days is close enough to normal that a standard significance test's assumptions hold, because those assumptions are about the estimator, not the raw data.

The honest caveats, which is where the seniority shows:

  • Convergence rate depends on skew. With this kind of long tail, "30 days" may not be nearly enough, and a single large ring can dominate the variance. Winsorising or a bootstrap is often more trustworthy than trusting the asymptotics blindly.
  • Independence is required. Multiple flagged transactions from the same customer or ring break it — analyse at the customer or ring level, not the transaction level, or the variance estimate is too small and everything looks significant.
  • Infinite variance breaks it entirely. The CLT needs finite variance; genuinely heavy-tailed loss distributions do not converge usefully.
Trap

The CLT says nothing about individual predictions. It justifies inference about the average daily loss across two models; it does not make any single day's loss Gaussian, and it is not a licence to use squared error on a single skewed transaction's loss.
