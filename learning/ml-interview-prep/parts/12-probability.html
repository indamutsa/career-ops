Probability & statistics Machine learning is not the science of getting the right answer. It is the science of being calibratedly unsure. Every model in this course outputs a probability distribution, and every loss function is a statement about likelihood.

The problem this was invented to solve

You cannot predict whether this transaction is fraud. Nobody can. The information simply is not there — two customers with identical features will behave differently, and you will never see the variable that separates them.

So the goal changes. Instead of “is it fraud”, the question becomes “out of all the transactions that look exactly like this one, what fraction turn out to be fraud?” That number — 0.03, say — is something you genuinely can learn, and it is something you can act on: block above 0.9, review between 0.3 and 0.9, allow below.

This reframing is the whole of probability's role in ML. Uncertainty is not a defect in the model to be engineered away. It is the honest output.

The terms

Random variable basic X

A quantity whose value depends on an outcome you have not seen yet. Not “random” in the sense of lawless — it has a definite distribution.

The number on a die you are about to roll. Fixed rules, unknown result.

Distribution basic P(X)

The full accounting of which values are how likely. Discrete case: a PMF, actual probabilities that sum to 1. Continuous case: a PDF, a density that integrates to 1 — a single point has probability zero.

Your model's softmax output is a PMF over the vocabulary. It is the model's entire answer; taking an argmax throws most of it away.

Expectation basic E[X]

The long-run average: every value weighted by how likely it is.

Play a game a million times; the expectation is your average score per play.

Your loss is an expectation over the data distribution — you estimate it with a batch average, which is why bigger batches give a lower-variance estimate.

Variance & std basic Var, σ

How spread out the values are. Variance is the mean squared distance from the mean; the standard deviation is its square root, which puts it back in the original units.

Batch normalisation literally divides by a running σ. And the “variance” in bias–variance is exactly this: how much your fitted model would jump around if you resampled the training set.

Covariance & correlation basic

Covariance: do two variables move together? Correlation is covariance scaled into [−1, 1] so it is unit-free and comparable.

The covariance matrix is what PCA eigendecomposes. Correlation near ±1 between two features is the multicollinearity that makes linear coefficients unstable.

Joint · marginal · conditional basic

Joint P(A,B): both happen. Marginal P(A): sum the joint over everything else — you “integrate out” B. Conditional P(A|B): the probability of A once you already know B.

Marginalising is looking at the row totals in the margin of a table. That is literally where the word comes from.

Independence basic

Knowing B tells you nothing about A: P(A|B) = P(A), equivalently P(A,B) = P(A)P(B).

Naive Bayes assumes every feature is conditionally independent given the class. That is nearly always false and the classifier works anyway — see module 23.

Bayes' theorem basic

How to flip a conditional round: P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B). Read it as posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

You have a belief. Evidence arrives. Bayes is the exact arithmetic of updating the belief without over- or under-reacting.

Naive Bayes, MAP estimation, Bayesian optimisation for hyperparameters, and the base-rate reasoning that dominates any fraud or medical-screening discussion.

Prior · likelihood · posterior mid

Prior: what you believed before the data. Likelihood: how well a parameter value explains the data you saw. Posterior: the updated belief afterwards.

L2 regularisation is exactly a Gaussian prior on the weights; L1 is a Laplace prior. Regularisation is a prior — that equivalence is a senior-level answer.

MLE mid

Maximum likelihood estimation: pick the parameters that make the data you actually observed as probable as possible.

Cross-entropy loss is MLE. Minimising it is maximising the log-likelihood of the training data. Same equation, opposite sign.

MAP mid

Maximum a posteriori: like MLE but multiply by a prior first. MLE plus a regulariser.

MAP with a Gaussian prior is ridge regression. Literally the same objective, with λ set by the prior's variance.

i.i.d. mid

Independent and identically distributed: each sample is drawn separately, from the same distribution.

The assumption underneath train/test splits, cross-validation, and every confidence interval you will ever quote — and the one that production violates first. See the trap below; it is the highest-value thing in this module.

Law of large numbers basic

Average enough independent samples and the average converges to the true expectation.

Why a big enough test set gives a trustworthy accuracy, and why a 40-row holdout does not.

Central limit theorem mid

Sums and averages of many independent variables end up approximately Gaussian, whatever the original distribution was.

One die is flat. The total of thirty dice is a bell curve. The bell appears from adding, not from the dice.

This is why A/B test statistics work on wildly non-normal metrics: you are testing the mean, and means go Gaussian.

Stochastic basic

Involving randomness. In SGD it refers to one specific thing: the gradient is computed on a random mini-batch, so it is a noisy estimate of the true gradient.

That noise is not merely tolerated — it is useful. It knocks you off saddle points and biases training toward flatter minima, which generalise better.

The distributions worth knowing basic

Bernoulli — one coin flip, one probability p. Binomial — how many heads in n flips. Categorical — one roll of a k-sided die; this is what softmax produces. Gaussian — the bell, what sums turn into. Poisson — counts of rare events in a window, e.g. fraud attempts per hour.

Binary classification = Bernoulli, so the loss is binary cross-entropy. Multiclass = categorical, so it is softmax + cross-entropy. The loss follows from the distribution.

Bayes, and the mistake almost everyone makes

A test for a disease is 99 % accurate. You test positive. What is the chance you have the disease?

Nearly everyone says 99 %. The real answer, for a disease affecting 1 in 1,000 people, is about 9 %. Play with it:

The reason is that P(positive | disease) and P(disease | positive) are different questions, and the thing that separates them is the base rate. Among 100,000 people, 100 have the disease and 99 of them test positive. But 99,900 are healthy, and 1 % of those — 999 people — test positive anyway. So 1,098 positives, of which 99 are real. 9 %.

Why this is a fraud-detection question in disguise

Fraud is roughly 0.1 % of transactions. A model with 99 % precision on the fraud class and 99 % recall still floods your review queue, for exactly the arithmetic above. This is why accuracy is a useless metric on imbalanced problems, why you look at PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC, and why the first question in any fraud design round is “what is the base rate?”. Module 25 does the metrics; the reasoning starts here.

Why cross-entropy is the loss

This is worth deriving once, because it converts a memorised fact into something you can reconstruct under pressure.

Ask: which parameters make the data I actually observed most probable? Assuming the examples are independent, the probability of the whole dataset is the product of the per-example probabilities. Products of thousands of small numbers underflow to zero in floating point, so take the log, which turns the product into a sum. Maximising a sum of logs is the same as minimising its negative. And the negative log-likelihood of a categorical distribution, written out, is exactly the cross-entropy formula.

maximise ∏ P(yᵢ | xᵢ; θ) ⟺ maximise Σ log P(yᵢ | xᵢ; θ) ⟺ minimise −Σ log P(yᵢ | xᵢ; θ) The last expression is cross-entropy loss. MLE and cross-entropy are the same procedure written two ways.

The same move explains mean squared error: assume the noise around your prediction is Gaussian, write down the negative log-likelihood, and everything except the squared term is a constant. So MSE is MLE under a Gaussian noise assumption, which immediately tells you when it is the wrong loss — heavy tails, outliers, or asymmetric costs, where a Gaussian is simply not the right noise model.

The assumption production breaks first

Everything statistical you do rests on i.i.d.: samples drawn independently from one fixed distribution. Production violates it constantly, and being able to name which part broke is a strong seniority signal.

What breaksLooks likeWhat it does to you
Not independent — time A random train/test split on time-series data puts tomorrow in train and today in test. Leakage. Offline metrics look wonderful and production is a disaster. Split by time, always.
Not independent — groups Multiple transactions from the same customer land on both sides of the split. You are measuring memorisation of that customer. Use grouped splits.
Not identical — covariate shift P(x) moves; the input mix changes. New country, new device, Christmas. Your model is extrapolating. This is what PSI in module 54 detects.
Not identical — concept drift P(y|x) moves; the same inputs now mean something different. Fraudsters adapt. The model is simply wrong now, and no amount of input monitoring alone will tell you — you need labels.
Feedback loops You block the transactions you predict are fraud, so you never learn their true label. The training distribution is now shaped by your own model. The nastiest one, because it is invisible in every metric.

Questions

BASICExplain Bayes' theorem to someone non-technical.
It is the arithmetic of updating a belief when evidence arrives — how strongly the new evidence should move you depends on how rare the thing was to begin with.

The disease example carries it: a 99 %-accurate test, a disease affecting 1 in 1,000, a positive result. Out of 100,000 people, 100 are ill and 99 of them test positive; 99,900 are healthy and 1 % of those — 999 people — also test positive. So of 1,098 positives, only 99 are real. About 9 %.

The lesson is that P(positive | disease) and P(disease | positive) are different questions, and the base rate is what separates them. Rare things stay fairly unlikely even after suggestive evidence.

Where I use it: fraud is about 0.1 % of transactions, so exactly this arithmetic decides how large a manual-review queue a given precision produces.

Trap

“99 % accurate” is ambiguous and you should say so — sensitivity and specificity can differ a lot, and the answer changes completely depending on which one is 99 %. Asking that question is itself the signal.

BASICWhat is the difference between a PDF and a PMF?
A PMF gives actual probabilities for discrete outcomes and sums to 1; a PDF gives a density for continuous ones, integrates to 1, and any single point has probability zero.

Asking “what is the probability a person is exactly 180.000… cm tall” is meaningless — it is zero. Only intervals have probability, which is why you integrate. And a density can legitimately exceed 1, as long as the area under it is 1: a uniform distribution on [0, 0.1] has density 10 everywhere.

Where it matters: continuous likelihoods are densities, so a "likelihood" of 4.7 is not a bug. And this is why continuous latent-variable models report log-densities that can be positive.

Trap

A density above 1 is not an error. If someone says their model output a probability of 3, first ask whether it is a density.

MIDWhat is maximum likelihood estimation, and how does it connect to the losses you use?
MLE picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable — and both cross-entropy and MSE are MLE in disguise.

Assume the examples are independent, so the probability of the whole dataset is the product of the per-example probabilities. Take a log to avoid underflow and turn the product into a sum, then negate to get a minimisation. The result for a categorical output is literally the cross-entropy formula.

Do the same with a Gaussian noise assumption and everything but the squared error falls out as a constant — so MSE is MLE under Gaussian noise. That immediately tells you when MSE is the wrong choice: heavy-tailed targets, outliers, or asymmetric costs, where Gaussian is not the right noise model. For counts you would use a Poisson likelihood and get a different loss.

The practical version I care about: choosing a loss is choosing a probabilistic model of your labels. If you can say what distribution you think the noise has, the loss is determined for you.

Trap

MLE is not unbiased in general — the MLE for a Gaussian variance divides by n rather than n−1 and systematically underestimates. And with few samples it overfits hard, which is exactly what the prior in MAP is there to prevent.

MIDMLE versus MAP — what is the difference, and where have you used it?
MAP is MLE multiplied by a prior — and that prior is exactly your regulariser.

MLE maximises P(data | θ). MAP maximises P(θ | data) ∝ P(data | θ)·P(θ). Take logs and the prior becomes an additive penalty on the objective.

Two concrete equivalences worth knowing cold:

  • A zero-mean Gaussian prior on the weights gives a penalty proportional to Σwᵢ² — that is ridge / L2 / weight decay, with λ set by the prior's variance. A tight prior means a large λ.
  • A zero-mean Laplace prior gives Σ|wᵢ| — that is lasso / L1, and its sharp peak at zero is the probabilistic reason it produces exact zeros.

Why I like framing it this way: it turns “what λ should I use?” from a grid-search question into a question about belief. λ → 0 means “I have no prior opinion, let the data decide” — fine with a million rows, reckless with a thousand.

Trap

MAP is still a single point estimate, not a Bayesian posterior. It gives you the peak and tells you nothing about the width, so it provides no uncertainty. That is the gap the Laplace approximation in module 14 fills.

MIDWhat does i.i.d. mean and where does it break in production?
Independent and identically distributed — and production breaks both halves routinely, which is where most silent model failures come from.

Independence goes first. Time series: a random split puts the future in training and the past in test, and your offline metric becomes fiction. Grouped data: several rows from the same customer straddle the split and you measure memorisation. Both fixes are structural — split by time, split by group.

Identical distribution goes second. Covariate shift means P(x) moved — a new market, a new device mix, a seasonal spike. Concept drift means P(y|x) moved — the same inputs now imply a different label, which is the normal state of affairs in fraud because the adversary adapts. The distinction matters operationally: you can detect covariate shift from inputs alone with PSI or a KS test, but concept drift is invisible without labels.

And the worst one is the feedback loop. You block the transactions you score as fraud, so you never observe their outcome. Your next training set is shaped by your own previous decisions, and every offline metric will look fine while the model quietly narrows. The mitigation is deliberate: hold out a small random control that bypasses the model, so you keep seeing unbiased labels. That costs real money and it is the right call.

Trap

Cross-validation assumes i.i.d. too. Standard k-fold on temporal data leaks in exactly the same way a random split does. Use forward-chaining / rolling-origin validation instead.

MIDWhy is the noise in SGD useful rather than merely tolerable?
Because the gradient estimated on a mini-batch is a noisy version of the true one, and that noise escapes saddle points and biases the search toward flatter minima.

Three effects, in increasing order of interest:

  • Escaping saddles. Exactly at a saddle the true gradient is zero and full-batch descent stops. A noisy estimate is not zero, so you get pushed off. Given how much saddles dominate in high dimensions, this alone matters.
  • Implicit regularisation. The noise acts like a temperature. Narrow, sharp minima are unstable under it — you get bounced out — while wide flat basins retain you. Flat minima empirically generalise better, so SGD's sloppiness is doing free regularisation.
  • It is also just cheaper. A batch of 256 gives a gradient estimate good enough to make progress at a fraction of the cost of the full dataset, so you take vastly more steps per unit of compute.

The consequence that shows up in practice: batch size and learning rate are coupled. Larger batches mean less noise, so you need a larger learning rate to keep the same amount of exploration — the linear scaling rule with warmup. Simply increasing batch size while holding the learning rate fixed typically costs you generalisation, and that surprises people.

Trap

The flat-minima story is a well-supported heuristic, not a theorem — sharpness is not reparameterisation-invariant, and Dinh et al. showed you can rescale a network to make a minimum arbitrarily sharp without changing the function. Say “empirically” and you sound like you have read the literature rather than a blog post.

SENIORYour fraud model reports 0.8 for a transaction. What does that number mean, and how would you check?
It should mean that among all transactions the model scores 0.8, about 80 % are actually fraud — and that is a testable claim called calibration, which most models fail out of the box.

The score is a claim about a reference class, not about this transaction. Verifying it is straightforward: bucket every scored transaction by predicted probability, and for each bucket compare the mean prediction against the observed fraud rate. Plot it — that is a reliability diagram — and summarise with expected calibration error or the Brier score.

Which models miscalibrate, and how:

  • Modern neural networks are systematically overconfident — Guo et al. showed accuracy improved over the years while calibration got worse.
  • SVMs output distances, not probabilities, and need Platt scaling before the number means anything.
  • Random forests are pulled toward the middle, because averaging many trees rarely produces a unanimous 0 or 1.
  • Any resampling — SMOTE, undersampling the negatives — deliberately destroys calibration by changing the base rate. You must correct the intercept back afterwards, and people routinely forget.

Fixes are cheap: Platt scaling (fit a logistic on the scores) or isotonic regression (more flexible, needs more data), fitted on a held-out calibration set, never on training data.

And the reason it is not academic: if the number is calibrated I can multiply it by the transaction value to get an expected loss, and set the block threshold from a cost matrix instead of a guess. Uncalibrated, that arithmetic is meaningless and every downstream business decision is built on sand.

Trap

Calibration and discrimination are independent. A model that outputs the base rate 0.001 for every single transaction is perfectly calibrated and completely useless — AUC 0.5. You need both, and you should say so before they ask.

SENIORWhy does the central limit theorem let you A/B test a metric that is nowhere near normal?
Because you are not testing the metric — you are testing its mean, and means of many independent samples become approximately Gaussian whatever the underlying shape.

Revenue per user is a horrible distribution: a spike at zero, a long right tail, occasional enormous outliers. But the sampling distribution of its average over tens of thousands of users is close enough to normal that a t-test's assumptions hold, because those assumptions are about the estimator, not the data.

The honest caveats, which is where the seniority shows:

  • Convergence rate depends on skew. "n > 30" is folklore. For revenue-like distributions you may need tens of thousands, and a single whale can dominate the variance. Winsorising or a bootstrap is often more trustworthy than trusting the asymptotics.
  • Independence is required. Multiple sessions per user break it — randomise and analyse at the user level, not the session level, or your variance estimate is too small and everything looks significant.
  • Infinite variance breaks it entirely. The CLT needs finite variance; genuinely heavy-tailed (Pareto-ish) metrics do not converge usefully.
Trap

The CLT says nothing about individual predictions. It justifies inference about your average metric; it does not make your per-user residuals Gaussian, and it is not a licence to use MSE on a skewed target.
