Part G · Walkthrough · Module 74

Domain post-training pipeline Taking a general open-weights model and making it good at one organisation's work. The interesting content is not the training algorithm — it is the data pipeline that feeds it and the evaluation harness that decides whether the new model is allowed to replace the old one.

How to use this module

This is where module 44 cashes out. Two things carry the answer: the honest case for not training at all, and the evaluation harness. Candidates who open with "I would fine-tune it" and never mention what would prove it worked are marked down every time.

The prompt, and the first four minutes

The prompt

"We want a model that is genuinely good at our domain. Design the post-training pipeline."

Post-training is everything done to a model after the expensive general pre-training run — the stage that turns a model which merely continues text into one that follows instructions and behaves the way you want. You are almost never the one pre-training; you are almost always the one post-training.

What to askWhy it changes the design
What can the current model not do?The whole design depends on whether the gap is missing knowledge, wrong format, wrong tone, or wrong reasoning — and only some of those are fixed by training.
Is there an automatic way to check an answer is right?If yes, reinforcement learning with a verifier is on the table and is the strongest option available. If no, it is not.
How much labelled data exists, and who can produce more?Expert time is the real budget line, not GPU hours.
How often does the domain change?Fast-changing facts belong in retrieval, not in weights.
What must never regress?Defines the guard suite. Without it you will ship a model that is better at the new task and worse at three old ones.
Deployment constraints — size, latency, on-premise?Decides base-model size and whether adapters or a full fine-tune.
Say this before you design anything

Post-training is the last option, not the first. Prompting and few-shot examples cost hours; retrieval (module 71) fixes missing or changing knowledge and can be updated the same afternoon; fine-tuning costs weeks and produces an artefact you must then evaluate, version, serve and re-do every time the base model improves. Fine-tuning teaches behaviour — format, tone, task structure, domain reasoning. It is a poor and expensive way to teach facts. Establishing that boundary first is the difference between a senior answer and an enthusiastic one.

The loop at the bottom is the point of the whole diagram: production traces become the next round's training data. A post-training pipeline is not a project with an end date, it is a flywheel — and saying so reframes the answer from "how do I fine-tune" to "how does this system improve every month".

Data — where the quality actually comes from

Curation beats volume the headline

A few thousand carefully written examples routinely beat hundreds of thousands of scraped ones. Supervised fine-tuning teaches by imitation, so the model copies whatever is in the data — including its sloppiness, its hedging and its formatting mistakes.

It is apprenticeship, not reading. An apprentice who watches one excellent craftsman for a month learns more than one who watches a thousand mediocre ones for a year, because they cannot tell which habits to copy.

Synthetic data, with a grader senior

A stronger model generates candidate answers, and a grader — a rubric-driven model check, a test suite, or a human spot-check — keeps only the ones that pass. Generation is cheap; the grader is what makes the data worth training on.

Unfiltered self-generated data degrades the model over successive rounds: it narrows onto its own habits and loses the variety that made it useful. The filter is not an optimisation, it is the thing preventing collapse.

Decontamination the one people forget

Removing anything from the training set that also appears in the evaluation set — including near-duplicates, not just exact matches.

Skip it and your benchmark score rises while real performance does not, which is precisely the failure that gets caught in the interview and then, later, in production. It is the same disease as data leakage in classical ML (module 20), and worth naming as such.

Mixing in general data senior

Training only on domain data causes catastrophic forgetting: the model gets better at your task and measurably worse at everything else, including basic instruction-following.

The standard remedy is a replay mixture — keep a proportion of general instruction data in every training run — plus a guard suite that would catch the regression if the mixture is wrong.

The three training stages, and when each applies

RL with a verifier
RLVR, GRPO		Sample several answers, score each with an automatic checker, reinforce what scored wellOnly when correctness is machine-checkable: code that must pass tests, maths with a known result, SQL that must return the right rows, output that must match a schemaHigh — many samples per prompt

Why the verifier changes everything. Preference tuning learns from a model of human opinion, and any such model can be gamed — the policy discovers answers that score well without being better, which is reward hacking. A verifier is not an opinion: the tests pass or they do not. That removes the gaming surface, which is why verifiable domains have progressed so much faster. The honest follow-up is that most business tasks are not verifiable, and pretending otherwise by writing a crude checker just moves the hacking target — the model learns to satisfy the checker.

Adapters versus full fine-tuning. LoRA trains a small number of extra parameters and leaves the base weights untouched. It is cheaper, it forgets less because the original model is still intact underneath, and — the operational argument that usually decides it — many adapters can be served from one base model on one fleet (module 73). Reach for a full fine-tune only when adapters have been tried and measurably fall short.

Evaluation — the part that is actually the deliverable

SuiteWhat it answersHow it is built
TaskDid it get better at the thing we trained it for?A few hundred held-out real examples with expert-approved answers; scored automatically where possible, by a calibrated judge otherwise
GuardDid it get worse at anything else?General instruction-following, reasoning, refusals, format compliance — run unchanged on every candidate model, with a pass threshold agreed in advance
SafetyDid fine-tuning weaken the safety behaviour?Adversarial prompts and known jailbreak patterns; note that ordinary domain fine-tuning is known to erode safety behaviour as a side effect, so this suite is not optional
HumanDo the people who will use it prefer it?A blind side-by-side sample against the incumbent, on real prompts, every release
The trap: the judge that moves with the model

If a model scores the outputs, that judge is part of your measuring instrument, and an instrument that drifts measures nothing. Pin the judge model and its prompt by version, keep a fixed set of human-labelled examples to re-check agreement whenever either changes, and never compare a score produced under one judge version with a score produced under another. Judges also carry known biases — toward longer answers, toward their own writing style, toward whichever answer came first — so control for length and randomise the order.

State the reporting discipline too: report a confidence interval, not a single number. On a 200-example suite a two-point difference is noise, and shipping on noise is how a pipeline quietly drifts backwards over several releases while every individual release looked like a win.

Operations

  • Everything versioned together. A model artefact is only reproducible if the data snapshot, the mixture proportions, the base-model version, the hyperparameters, the evaluation suite version and the judge version are all recorded with it. Experiment tracking exists for this (module 52); "which data produced this checkpoint" must be answerable a year later.
  • The registry is the gate. A candidate model enters the registry with its full scorecard attached, and promotion to default is a deliberate, recorded decision — not whoever copied a checkpoint to the serving bucket last.
  • Rollout is the same as any model. Shadow for latency, canary for behaviour, keep the previous version warm and make rollback a single action (module 53).
  • Monitor the behaviour, not just the box. Output length distribution, refusal rate, format-compliance rate, tool-call validity, user edit and retry rates. These move long before anyone files a complaint.
  • Close the loop with judgment. Production traces feed the next round — but only after filtering, or you train on the model's own worst habits and amplify them.

If you only get five minutes

"First I would establish that training is warranted at all — prompting and retrieval are far cheaper, and fine-tuning teaches behaviour rather than facts, so if the gap is missing or changing knowledge it belongs in retrieval. Assuming it is a behaviour gap: the pipeline is a data stage, a training stage and an evaluation gate. Data is where the quality comes from — a few thousand curated examples beat hundreds of thousands of scraped ones, synthetic data is only useful behind a grader, I would decontaminate against the eval set, and I would keep general data in the mixture so the model does not forget everything else. Training is supervised fine-tuning first, preference tuning where quality is a judgment call, and reinforcement learning with a verifier only where correctness is machine-checkable — because a verifier cannot be gamed the way a learned reward model can. LoRA adapters unless proven insufficient, so one base model can serve every tenant. Then the part that is really the deliverable: four evaluation suites — task, regression guard, safety, and blind human comparison — with the judge model pinned by version and results reported with confidence intervals, because a two-point difference on 200 examples is noise. Everything versioned together in a registry, promoted by canary, and production traces filtered back into the next round."

Checkpoint

  • Give the argument for not fine-tuning, in two sentences, without sounding evasive.
  • State what fine-tuning teaches well and what it teaches badly.
  • Name the three training stages and the single criterion that selects each.
  • Explain why a verifier is stronger than a learned reward model, and where that stops applying.
  • List the four evaluation suites and say what each one alone would miss.
  • Explain why the judge model must be pinned, and what a two-point score difference means.
