Governance & risk Fairness, privacy, robustness and cost. In a regulated domain — and fraud is one — these are not a slide at the end of the deck; they are constraints that change the model you are allowed to build. Bringing them up unprompted is a senior signal.

Fairness — and why you must pick a definition

DefinitionRequiresReasonable when
Demographic parityEqual positive rate across groupsThe base rates should be equal, and a difference reflects historical injustice
Equal opportunityEqual true-positive rateMissing a positive is the harm you care about
Equalised oddsEqual TPR and FPRBoth error types carry real cost
Calibration within groupsA predicted 5 % means 5 % in every groupThe score is handed to a human who will act on its face value
The impossibility result — know this one

Chouldechova and Kleinberg et al. proved that when base rates genuinely differ between groups, you cannot satisfy calibration and equalised odds simultaneously (except in degenerate cases). This is mathematics, not a tooling gap.

So "make the model fair" is not a well-formed request. The real task is to choose which fairness criterion matches the harm you are trying to prevent, state the choice explicitly, and document what you gave up. A candidate who says that has understood the problem; a candidate who promises all of them has not.

The terms

Proxy variable senior

A feature that encodes a protected attribute without naming it — postcode for ethnicity, device type or first name for income and gender.

Removing the protected attribute does not remove the bias; it only removes your ability to measure it. "Fairness through unawareness" is the standard mistake, and in a rich feature set it is close to useless.

Model card mid

A short document per model version: intended use, out-of-scope use, training data, evaluation including per-group breakdowns, limitations, and ethical considerations.

Cheap to write, and it is the artifact that makes a handover or an audit survivable. Generate it from the tracking run so it cannot drift from the model it describes.

Right to explanation senior

Under GDPR Article 22, a person subject to a significant automated decision can demand meaningful information about the logic and contest the outcome.

Drives real architecture: a per-decision explanation (module 26), a stored record of the inputs used, and a human review path. Note the legal debate about how far Article 22 reaches — the defensible engineering answer is to build the capability rather than argue the scope.

Differential privacy senior

A formal guarantee: adding or removing one person's data changes the output distribution by at most eε.

The only privacy notion with a real proof attached, and it costs accuracy — the privacy budget ε is a dial between utility and protection, not a checkbox.

Membership inference senior

An attack that determines whether a specific record was in the training set, usually by exploiting the model's higher confidence on data it memorised.

Why "the model is anonymous because it only stores weights" is false. Overfitted models leak most, which makes regularisation a privacy control as well as a generalisation one.

Adversarial robustness senior

Fraud is the adversarial case by definition: the data distribution contains an opponent who is actively probing your decision boundary.

Consequences: prefer features that are costly for the attacker to change (account age, device history) over cheap ones (a self-reported field); expect probing behaviour — many small test transactions — and detect it as a pattern; and retrain frequently, because your decision boundary is being mapped.

Total cost of ownership mid

Training compute, serving compute, storage, the data pipeline, and the engineer-hours to keep it all alive.

Serving usually dominates over a model's life, which is why the inference-optimal argument from module 43 is a business decision. A 2-point AUC gain that triples serving cost is a trade to be justified, not an automatic win.

Questions

SENIORHow would you check whether your fraud model is fair?
Choose the definition that matches the harm, measure per group at the operating threshold, look for proxies, and document the trade-off you accepted. The measurement is straightforward; the definition choice is the actual work.

1 — Name the harm. For fraud, the harm to a customer is being wrongly declined: a legitimate person cannot pay. That is a false positive, so the relevant criterion is the false positive rate across groups — equalised odds territory — rather than demographic parity, which would demand equal decline rates even where genuine fraud rates differ.

2 — Measure at the threshold you actually use. Per group: FPR, TPR, and calibration. Aggregate AUC hides everything; the decision is made at a threshold, so that is where fairness lives.

3 — Handle the attribute problem. You often may not collect ethnicity, and inferring it to audit is itself fraught. Practical routes: aggregate proxies at area level, a consented sample used only for auditing, or bias-audit techniques that work without individual labels. Whichever you pick, be explicit about its limitations — this is a place where overclaiming is worse than a caveat.

4 — Hunt proxies. Check the correlation of top features with the protected attribute where you can measure it. Postcode is the classic; device and channel are close behind.

5 — Decide and write it down. If FPR differs materially, the options are per-group thresholds (legally fraught in some jurisdictions, and a decision for legal not for me), reweighting or constrained training, or accepting the gap with a documented justification. All three are defensible; doing it silently is not.

The framing I would close on: fairness is a product and legal decision informed by measurement, not a metric an engineer optimises alone. My job is to surface the trade-off honestly, quantify each option, and make sure the decision is recorded with the model — which is exactly what the model card is for.

MIDWhat does GDPR actually require of an ML system?
A lawful basis for the processing, data minimisation, purpose limitation, the subject's rights (access, erasure, explanation, contest), and — for significant automated decisions — a human review path. In practice it is mostly about lineage and deletion, both of which are architecture, not policy.

The parts that change engineering:

  • Purpose limitation. Data collected to process payments cannot be silently repurposed for marketing models. This is a real constraint on which tables a training job may read, and it should be enforced by access control, not by convention.
  • Erasure. You must be able to delete a person's data — which raises the uncomfortable question of the model trained on it. The pragmatic industry position is that deleting from the training store and removing them at the next retrain is proportionate; machine unlearning exists but is not mature. What matters is having a defensible, documented position rather than a vague one.
  • Automated decisions (Art. 22). A fraud decline is significant, so you need a human review route, an explanation of the logic, and a way to contest. That means per-decision explanations and stored feature vectors — the same logging that makes debugging possible.
  • Data minimisation. Collect what you need. "More features might help" is not a lawful basis, and this genuinely does constrain feature engineering.
  • Records of processing and DPIAs for high-risk processing — a document, but a document that requires you to know your lineage.

The observation worth making: almost every GDPR requirement above is satisfied by infrastructure you want anyway — lineage, per-prediction logging, explanations, access control, documented models. Teams that treat compliance as a bolt-on suffer; teams that build traceability for engineering reasons find compliance mostly falls out of it. And fraud prevention is generally a legitimate-interest basis, which is why it is permitted at all — but that basis is not unlimited, and it does not extend to reusing the data elsewhere.

SENIORHow does an adversary change how you build the model?
It changes feature selection, the retraining cadence, what you reveal, and what you monitor. In an adversarial domain the data distribution is not drifting by accident — it is being moved by someone with an incentive.

Feature economics. Prefer features that are expensive for the attacker to change. Account age, device reputation and behavioural history are costly to fake; email domain, a self-declared field or a user agent string are nearly free. A model leaning on cheap features has a boundary that can be walked around in an afternoon.

Do not leak the boundary. Precise decline reasons, consistent thresholds, and fast deterministic feedback all let an attacker binary-search your model. Vague messages, some randomisation near the threshold, and rate limiting on retries are cheap defences. This trades against explainability, and that tension is exactly the kind of thing to name rather than resolve glibly.

Expect probing. The signature is a burst of small transactions testing what gets through. That pattern is detectable in itself, at the entity and network level rather than the transaction level — which argues for velocity and graph features, and for an asynchronous path that can afford to compute them.

Retrain faster, and monitor differently. Concept drift here is adversarial and can be abrupt: a new technique appears and works until you notice. Sudden shifts in the score distribution or in the mix of declined patterns deserve investigation as attacks, not just as drift.

And assume the labels are contested. Unreported fraud is labelled legitimate, so your negatives are polluted in a direction the adversary controls — which biases training toward whatever currently evades detection. This is the strongest argument for the randomised holdout and for treating unlabelled anomalies (module 24) as a complement to the supervised model rather than as a curiosity.
