Monitoring & drift A model degrades silently. No exception, no 500, no alert — just decisions that get gradually worse while every dashboard stays green. PSI, KS and KL are how you see it, and the delayed-label problem is why you need them at all.

Four things that can change

NameWhat shiftsExampleDoes the model still work?
Data / covariate driftP(X)Traffic moves from desktop to mobileMaybe — the relationship may be unchanged
Concept driftP(y|X)Fraudsters change tactics; the same features now mean something differentNo — this is the one that hurts
Label driftP(y)Fraud rate rises from 0.1 % to 0.5 % Calibration breaks even if ranking holds
Upstream / schema changeThe pipelineA field switches from cents to eurosNo — and it looks exactly like sudden drift
The analogy

Data drift is your customers changing. Concept drift is the rules of the game changing. You can often survive the first; the second means the thing you learned is no longer true.

And the cruel part: you can detect data drift immediately, because it only needs inputs. Concept drift needs labels — which in fraud arrive weeks later. So the drift you can measure fastest is the one that matters least.

Watch a distribution drift

The measures

PSI — Population Stability Index mid

Bin both distributions the same way, then sum over bins:

PSI = Σ (ai − ei) · ln(ai / ei)a = actual proportion in bin i, e = expected (reference) proportion

Industry convention: < 0.1 stable · 0.1–0.25 moderate, investigate · > 0.25 significant, act. Ubiquitous in credit and fraud because it is interpretable per bin — you can see which part of the distribution moved.

PSI is symmetric KL senior

PSI is exactly the Jeffreys divergence: KL(a‖e) + KL(e‖a). Expand both and the cross terms give the (a − e)·ln(a/e) form.

Worth saying in an interview — it connects module 13's information theory to the production metric, and it explains the symmetry: PSI does not care which distribution you call the reference.

KS statistic mid

The largest vertical gap between two cumulative distribution functions.

Binning-free and it comes with a p-value. The catch: with millions of rows every trivial difference is "significant", so use the statistic as an effect size and ignore the p-value.

Delayed labels senior

The gap between a prediction and knowing whether it was right — days for a chargeback, months for a loan default.

The defining constraint of production ML monitoring. It is why you monitor proxies (input drift, prediction drift, agreement with rules) as leading indicators and treat true performance as a lagging one.

Prediction drift mid

PSI on the model's output score distribution rather than on its inputs.

The best single early-warning signal: one number, no labels needed, and it moves when either the inputs or the model change. If you can afford exactly one monitor, this is it.

Calibration drift senior

Ranking stays fine but the probabilities stop meaning what they say — predicted 3 % events now happen 6 % of the time.

Common when the base rate moves. Often fixable by recalibrating (Platt or isotonic) without retraining the model, which is far cheaper and faster.

Alert fatigue mid

Too many alerts, so all of them get ignored.

Every alert needs an owner and a runbook action. If nobody would do anything differently, it is a dashboard, not an alert — and shipping it as an alert degrades every real one.

Questions

MIDWhat would you monitor for a model in production?
Four layers: infrastructure, data quality, drift, and model performance — in that order of how fast the signal arrives, and in reverse order of how much it matters.

Layer 1 — infrastructure (seconds). Latency percentiles, error rate, throughput, saturation. Standard service monitoring; catches outages, not degradation.

Layer 2 — data quality (minutes). Per feature: null rate, range violations, cardinality, type conformance, and freshness — how old is the newest value in the online store. Staleness is the failure everyone forgets, and a stale feature produces a confident wrong answer rather than an error.

Layer 3 — drift (hours to days, no labels needed). PSI per important feature against the training reference, PSI on the prediction distribution, and the rate at which the model disagrees with the incumbent rules. These are the leading indicators, and they are the whole reason the layer exists.

Layer 4 — performance (days to weeks, needs labels). Precision, recall and calibration at the operating threshold, computed on matured labels, sliced by segment. Plus the business metrics: fraud losses, false-positive cost, analyst queue volume.

The two things I would add that get overlooked: monitor per segment, not just in aggregate, because a serious regression in one merchant category disappears in a healthy overall number; and log the full feature vector and the model version with every prediction, because that log is what makes every layer above debuggable — and it must exist before the incident, not after.

SENIORPSI on a feature is 0.31. What do you do?
Do not retrain reflexively. First establish whether this is a pipeline bug, a real population change, or a seasonal pattern — the three have completely different fixes, and retraining on a bug bakes the bug into the model.

Step 1 — look at the per-bin contributions. PSI is a sum, so open it up. A single bin dominating usually means a bug: a new category code, a default value appearing, a unit change. A smooth shift across all bins looks more like a genuine population change. This one plot usually decides the diagnosis.

Step 2 — check the shape in time. A step change on a specific day points at a deployment or an upstream release — go and look at what shipped that day. A gradual ramp points at the world changing. A repeating annual or weekly pattern is seasonality, and the correct action may be to fix the reference window rather than the model.

Step 3 — check whether it matters. Drift on a feature with negligible importance is noise. Drift on a top-five feature is serious. I would weight drift by feature importance rather than alert on every feature equally — otherwise you have built an alert-fatigue machine.

Step 4 — check the output. Has the prediction distribution moved? Has performance moved on the labels that have matured? Input drift with stable predictions and stable performance can be genuinely benign — the model may not be sensitive to that region of the input space.

Then act accordingly: pipeline bug → fix the pipeline and do not retrain; seasonality → widen or realign the reference; genuine population shift with degraded performance → retrain on recent data, with the temporal holdout confirming the improvement; genuine shift without degradation → note it, lower the alert, keep watching.

The trap is treating drift as an automatic retraining trigger. Retraining on drifted data caused by a broken upstream field teaches the model the bug, and then you have two problems and no baseline to compare against.

SENIORYour labels arrive 30 days late. How do you know the model is working today?
Proxies now, truth later. Build a leading-indicator layer from things observable immediately, calibrate those proxies against the lagging truth once, and use a small deliberately unfiltered holdout to keep an unbiased view.

The proxies I would use:

  • Prediction distribution vs the reference — the strongest single signal, and free.
  • Input drift weighted by feature importance.
  • Agreement rate with the previous model or the rules engine. A sudden change in disagreement is informative even without knowing who is right.
  • Fast partial feedback. Not all labels are equally slow: a customer calling to dispute arrives in hours, an analyst's review decision in minutes. Those are biased samples, but they are fast, and a bias you understand is usable.
  • Human review outcomes on the queue the model itself populates — the analysts are effectively labelling in near real time.

Calibrate the proxies once. Using history where you now have both, measure which proxy movements actually preceded performance drops. That converts "PSI is 0.3" into "a PSI of 0.3 on this feature preceded a 4-point precision drop twice last year", which is what makes a threshold defensible rather than arbitrary.

Handle the censoring correctly. With a 30-day lag, recent data looks artificially clean — the frauds have not been reported yet. Comparing last week's apparent fraud rate against a fully matured month is a classic mistake that manufactures a false alarm. Either compare only matured cohorts, or model the reporting curve and inflate accordingly.

And keep an unbiased window: a small random holdout that bypasses the model entirely. It costs money — you approve some transactions you would have blocked — and it is the only way to observe the outcomes your own decisions would otherwise censor. Both the feedback loop from module 52 and the counterfactual question depend on it.

MIDPSI, KS or KL — which would you use?
PSI for routine per-feature monitoring, because it is bin-interpretable and has conventional thresholds everyone recognises. KS when you want a binning-free continuous comparison. KL when you are reasoning in information-theoretic terms or comparing model outputs.

PSI wins operationally: bounded interpretation (0.1 / 0.25), symmetric, and the per-bin decomposition tells you where the shift is, which is the first question you ask during an investigation. Its weaknesses are that it needs binning — and the answer depends on the bins — and that it is undefined when a bin is empty, so an epsilon is added, which quietly makes small bins dominate.

KS avoids binning entirely and has a distribution theory. It is only for continuous univariate data, it is most sensitive near the median and weakest in the tails — which is exactly where fraud lives — and its p-value is useless at production sample sizes.

KL is the principled quantity and the one everything else derives from, but it is asymmetric, unbounded, and has no natural threshold, so it is awkward as an alert. JS divergence fixes the symmetry and boundedness and is a reasonable choice for comparing categorical distributions.

For categorical features none of the above is ideal — chi-squared or JS on the category proportions is the usual answer, and new unseen categories deserve their own alert because they often indicate an upstream change rather than drift.

What I would run in practice: PSI per feature and on the prediction distribution as the standing monitor, because it is what fraud and credit teams already read; KS as a secondary check on the continuous features that matter most; and a separate alert for unseen categorical values. The measure matters far less than having a stable reference window, sensible per-feature thresholds, and an owner for each alert.
