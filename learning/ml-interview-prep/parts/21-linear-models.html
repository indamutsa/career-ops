Linear models The oldest tools in the box, and still the ones a fraud or credit team ships when a regulator has to be able to read the model. Linear and logistic regression, generalised linear models, and the regularisation that turns out to be the Lagrange multiplier from module 14.

Why these are not a historical curiosity

An interviewer asking about logistic regression in 2026 is not testing whether you know it. They are testing whether you understand why anyone would still choose it, because the answer reveals how you think about production systems.

  • It is auditable. Every coefficient has a meaning you can defend in a sentence. In credit and insurance this is not a nice-to-have — under regimes like ECOA/Reg B in the US you must be able to state the specific reasons for an adverse decision, and "the gradient boosting said so" is not one.
  • It is naturally calibrated. Fit by maximum likelihood on the log-loss, and its outputs behave like probabilities out of the box. Most boosted-tree scores do not, and when your threshold comes from a cost matrix, calibration is the property that matters — see module 25.
  • It is fast and tiny. A dot product. Microseconds, no accelerator, trivial to deploy at the edge of a payment path.
  • It is the baseline that keeps you honest. If your deep model beats regularised logistic regression by two points of AUC, you have learned something important about whether the complexity is worth its operational cost.
  • It degrades gracefully. Extrapolation is a straight line rather than whatever a tree does off the edge of its training range, which is a real advantage under drift.

The terms

Linear regression basic ŷ = w·x + b

Predict a number as a weighted sum of the features.

Pricing a flat: start at a base price, add so much per square metre, add so much for a balcony, subtract so much per kilometre from the centre.

OLS / normal equation mid w = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy

Least squares has a closed-form solution — no iteration required.

Fine up to a few thousand features. Beyond that, or when XᵀX is near-singular, you solve it by QR or SVD rather than inverting anything.

Residual basic

Actual minus predicted, for one row. The bit the model got wrong.

Plotting residuals against predictions is the fastest diagnostic in classical modelling: any visible pattern means structure you failed to capture.

R² basic

The fraction of the target's variance the model explains. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the mean, and negative is possible on held-out data.

Never quote plain R² for model selection — it can only rise as you add features. Adjusted R² penalises the count; held-out error settles it properly.

Logistic regression basic p = σ(w·x + b)

Linear regression squashed through a sigmoid so the output is a probability between 0 and 1.

A dimmer switch, not a light switch: the score slides smoothly from "almost certainly not" to "almost certainly yes".

Sigmoid basic 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ)

Maps any real number into (0,1). Symmetric around z = 0, where it equals 0.5.

The binary special case of softmax. Its derivative σ(1−σ) peaks at 0.25 and vanishes at both ends — the saturation that causes vanishing gradients in module 30.

Log-odds / logit mid log(p/(1−p))

The quantity logistic regression is actually linear in. The inverse of the sigmoid.

Odds are the gambler's version of probability: 3-to-1 rather than 75 %. Taking the log makes the scale symmetric — twice as likely and half as likely become +log2 and −log2.

Odds ratio mid e^wⱼ

Exponentiate a coefficient and you get the multiplicative effect on the odds of a one-unit increase in that feature.

This is how a coefficient gets explained to a risk committee: "each additional failed login multiplies the odds of fraud by 1.8."

GLM & link function mid

The general recipe: a linear predictor, plus a link function connecting it to the mean of whatever distribution suits your target.

Identity link + Gaussian = linear regression. Logit + Bernoulli = logistic. Log + Poisson = count regression. One framework, three familiar models.

Ridge (L2) mid + λ‖w‖²

Penalise the sum of squared weights. Shrinks everything toward zero, in proportion, without reaching it.

A progressive tax on weight size — the biggest weights feel it most.

Lasso (L1) mid + λ‖w‖₁

Penalise the sum of absolute weights. Drives some coefficients to exactly zero, so it selects features as a side effect of fitting.

The diamond-shaped constraint region from module 10 — corners on the axes are why the solution lands on a zero.

Elastic net mid

Both penalties at once. Gets lasso's sparsity while handling correlated feature groups the way ridge does.

The default when features come in correlated clusters — lasso alone picks one member of each cluster arbitrarily, which makes the selection unstable across refits.

Multicollinearity mid

Two or more features carrying nearly the same information, so the model cannot tell which deserves the credit.

Two people pushing a car. It moves. You cannot tell from the motion who pushed harder — and tomorrow you might attribute it the other way round.

Coefficients become huge, unstable, and sometimes sign-flipped between refits. Predictions stay fine; interpretation is what breaks.

WOE & Information Value senior

Weight of Evidence: bin a feature, then replace each bin with the log-odds of the target inside it. Information Value sums their contribution to rank features.

Standard practice in credit scorecards, and worth knowing by name for any fraud or risk interview — it makes a non-linear feature usable by a linear model while keeping the whole thing explainable. It is also a leakage magnet: the bins must be fitted inside the cross-validation fold.

Logistic regression: the three things people get wrong

One — it is a classifier that is really a probability model. It does not output a class; it outputs P(y = 1 | x). The class comes from a threshold you choose, and 0.5 is a default, not a law. In fraud, where a false negative costs €200 and a false positive costs a customer thirty seconds of annoyance, the correct threshold is nowhere near 0.5. Module 25 derives it from the cost matrix.

Two — "linear" refers to the log-odds, not the probability. The relationship between a feature and the probability is an S-curve. What is linear is the log-odds:

log( p / (1 − p) ) = w·x + b this is the model; the sigmoid is just this equation rearranged for p

That single line answers a whole family of interview questions. Why is the decision boundary a straight line? Because the boundary is where p = 0.5, which is where the log-odds is 0, which is where w·x + b = 0 — a hyperplane. Why can it not solve XOR? Same reason.

Three — it is trained with log loss, and not by accident. Squared error on a sigmoid output gives a non-convex objective with a nasty additional problem: the gradient contains a σ′ factor that vanishes exactly when the model is confidently wrong. The worse the mistake, the slower the correction. Log loss cancels that factor — the gradient reduces to the beautifully simple (p − y)·x, so a confident mistake produces a large gradient. That cancellation is why cross-entropy is the loss for every classifier, not just this one.

The pattern worth internalising: pick the distribution that matches your target's shape, and the link follows. Fitting a linear regression to a count is a common mistake — it will happily predict −3 claims, and it assumes constant variance when count data has variance that grows with the mean. Insurance pricing is essentially a Gamma GLM for severity times a Poisson GLM for frequency, which is a genuinely useful thing to know if the company is in that space.

Regularisation — and the callback

Module 14 set this up; here is the payoff. "Minimise squared error subject to ‖w‖² ≤ t" has the Lagrangian MSE + λ‖w‖². Your regularisation strength is a Lagrange multiplier on a budget for how large the weights are allowed to get. Every value of the budget t corresponds to exactly one λ, so tuning either one is the same search.

Ridge (L2)Lasso (L1)
Constraint shapeCircle / sphereDiamond — corners sit on the axes
Effect on weightsShrinks all, proportionally, never to zeroDrives some to exactly zero
Correlated featuresSplits the credit between them — stablePicks one arbitrarily, drops the rest — unstable across refits
SolutionClosed form: (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹XᵀyNo closed form; coordinate descent or LARS
Bayesian readingGaussian prior on the weightsLaplace prior
Reach for it whenMany features all plausibly matter; multicollinearity You want a short, defensible feature list
Always scale first

Both penalties act on the raw magnitude of the coefficients, and a coefficient's magnitude depends on its feature's units. A feature in euros and the same feature in cents get penalised a hundred times differently. Standardise before regularising, every time — and do the scaling inside the pipeline so it is fitted on the training fold only. Ridge's closed form also shows exactly why it helps numerically: adding λI to XᵀX makes a near-singular matrix invertible again, which is the same fix as the condition-number argument in module 10.

Questions

BASICExplain logistic regression to a product manager.
It scores each case by adding up weighted evidence, then squashes that total into a probability between 0 and 100 %.

Each feature gets a weight learned from history — a failed login pushes the score up, an account that is five years old pushes it down. Add them up, run the total through an S-shaped curve, and you get a probability. Then you pick a cut-off for action, and that cut-off is a business decision about the relative cost of missing fraud versus annoying a good customer, not a modelling one.

The thing I would emphasise to a PM is why we might choose it over something fancier: you can read it. Every score decomposes into "these three features drove it", which is what you need for a customer explanation, for a regulator, and for debugging when it misbehaves.

Trap

"Why is it called regression if it classifies?" Because it regresses on the log-odds — it predicts a continuous quantity, and the classification is a threshold applied afterwards.

MIDWhy do we use log loss instead of MSE for logistic regression?
Two reasons: MSE on a sigmoid is non-convex, and its gradient vanishes exactly when the model is most confidently wrong. Log loss is convex and its gradient is proportional to the error itself.

Convexity. Squared error composed with a sigmoid gives a surface with local minima. Log loss with a sigmoid is convex in the parameters, so there is one global optimum and any reasonable optimiser reaches it.

The gradient, which is the more interesting reason. Under MSE, the gradient carries a factor of σ′(z) = σ(1−σ). When the model predicts 0.001 and the truth is 1 — as wrong as it is possible to be — that factor is about 0.001, so the update is almost zero. The model is maximally wrong and learns most slowly. Under log loss the σ′ cancels against the derivative of the log, and the gradient becomes (p − y)·x: the update is proportional to how wrong you were, which is what you wanted all along.

And the principled reason: log loss is the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli. So fitting with it is maximum likelihood, which is why the resulting probabilities are calibrated. MSE assumes Gaussian noise, which is simply the wrong model for a binary outcome.

Trap

The same σ′ cancellation is why the softmax + cross-entropy pair is implemented as one fused operation in every framework. Doing them separately reintroduces the numerical problem the cancellation was avoiding.

MIDA coefficient in your fraud model is 0.7. What does that mean?
Exponentiate it: e⁰·⁷ ≈ 2.0. A one-unit increase in that feature roughly doubles the odds of fraud, holding everything else constant.

Three qualifications I would state without being asked, because each one is a follow-up waiting to happen:

  • Odds, not probability. Doubling the odds moves a 1 % probability to about 2 %, but a 50 % probability to 67 %. The multiplicative effect is constant on odds and very much not constant on probability.
  • "One unit" depends on scaling. If I standardised the features, one unit is one standard deviation, and that is what I should say out loud. Quoting an odds ratio without saying what a unit is means nothing.
  • "Holding everything else constant" is doing a lot of work. With correlated features that condition is not physically achievable, and the individual coefficients get unstable. This is where I would check the correlation structure before quoting any single number to a stakeholder.

And the boundary: this is an association in the training data, not a causal effect. If asked "so if we reduce that feature, will fraud drop?", the honest answer is that nothing in the fit supports that claim.

MIDRidge or lasso — how do you choose?
Lasso when I want a short feature list and believe most features are irrelevant; ridge when I think many features contribute a little, or when they are correlated; elastic net when both are true, which in practice is often.

The geometric reason for the difference is worth stating because it shows you understand it rather than remember it: the L1 constraint region is a diamond with corners on the axes, and a loss contour expanding outward hits a corner first — and a corner is a point where one coordinate is exactly zero. The L2 region is a sphere with no corners, so the contact point almost never has a zero coordinate.

What decides it for me in practice:

  • Correlated feature groups → ridge or elastic net. Lasso picks one member of the group essentially at random, and the choice flips between refits, which makes for an embarrassing conversation when the feature list changes every month.
  • Feature list has to be defensible and short → lasso, then refit an unregularised model on the survivors to get honest coefficients, since lasso shrinks the ones it keeps.
  • p ≫ n → lasso caps out at n selected features; elastic net does not, which is one of the two reasons the elastic net paper exists.
  • No strong prior → elastic net and cross-validate both λ and the mix.
Trap

"Lasso does feature selection, so it is the more principled choice." It selects, but the selection is not stable and is not a causal statement. If stability matters, bootstrap the fit and look at how often each feature survives.

SENIORYou find high multicollinearity. Does it matter?
It depends entirely on what the model is for. For prediction, usually not. For interpretation, it is fatal.

Mechanically: near-duplicate features make XᵀX ill-conditioned, so the coefficient estimates have enormous variance. You can get a large positive weight on one feature and a large negative one on its near-twin, cancelling out, and a slightly different sample flips the pair. The fitted predictions stay stable — the pair still sums to the same thing — but no individual coefficient means anything.

So when do I care?

  • Pure prediction, model held fixed → largely ignore it. It costs some efficiency, not accuracy.
  • Anyone will read the coefficients — regulatory reasons codes, a risk committee, a feature post-mortem → I must fix it, because the numbers I would be quoting are arbitrary.
  • Features drift apart over time → now it matters for prediction too. Two features that were collinear at training time and diverge in production feed the model a combination it has never seen, and the enormous cancelling weights amplify the difference.

What I would do: compute VIF and look at anything above ~10; drop or merge the redundant feature; apply ridge, which is the cleanest fix precisely because adding λI restores the conditioning; or use PCA if interpretability is already lost. In a credit setting the conventional answer is to keep one representative feature per correlated block and document the choice, because a stable, explainable scorecard beats a marginally better one.

Trap

Multicollinearity is a property of the feature matrix, not of the model. It affects linear models' coefficients; trees are unaffected in prediction but split the importance arbitrarily between correlated features — which is the same interpretation problem wearing a different hat.

SENIORWhen would you ship logistic regression over gradient boosting?
When explainability is a hard requirement, when latency or footprint is extreme, when the data is small or badly non-stationary, or when the honest measured gap between them does not justify the operational cost.
  • Regulated decisions. Credit, insurance, hiring. If I must produce specific adverse-action reasons, a scorecard where each contribution is a coefficient times a binned value gives them exactly, and it survives a model-risk review that a boosted ensemble will not pass without substantial extra work.
  • Calibration matters more than ranking. When the threshold comes from a cost matrix, or the score feeds an expected-value calculation downstream, I need a probability rather than a ranking. LR gives that natively; GBDT needs a calibration layer, which is one more thing to maintain and to re-fit when it drifts.
  • Latency and footprint. A dot product in a synchronous payment path costs microseconds. Several hundred trees do not, and the tail latency is what breaks an SLO.
  • Small or drifting data. With a few thousand rows, or a target whose relationship shifts, the simpler model's higher bias is protective. Its extrapolation behaviour off the edge of the training distribution is also far more predictable than a tree's, which just returns the nearest leaf forever.
  • The gap is small. I would actually measure it. Two AUC points may not be worth a model that nobody can explain when it declines a customer.

The realistic answer for fraud specifically is that plenty of production systems run both: a boosted model for ranking and a transparent scorecard for the decisions that must be justified, or a linear model whose features were engineered with the help of a tree model's findings.

MIDHow do you feed a high-cardinality categorical — like merchant ID with 200,000 values — to a linear model?
Not one-hot. Options are target/WOE encoding fitted inside the fold, hashing, frequency encoding, grouping the long tail, or a learned embedding — and the choice is mostly about leakage and stability, not accuracy.
  • Target or WOE encoding. Replace each merchant with the log-odds of fraud for that merchant. Powerful and standard in credit scoring. It leaks aggressively if done wrong — the encoding must be fitted inside each CV fold, with smoothing toward the global rate for rare merchants, and ideally out-of-fold within the training set too. This is the answer they are usually fishing for.
  • Frequency / count encoding. Replace with how often the value appears. Crude, leak-free, and surprisingly useful for fraud, where "merchant we have barely seen" is itself the signal.
  • Hashing trick. Hash into a fixed number of buckets. Constant memory, handles unseen values at serving time for free, at the cost of collisions and lost interpretability. Good for extremely high cardinality in a streaming setting.
  • Group the tail. Keep the top few hundred merchants explicitly, bucket the rest as "other". Simple, stable, and often enough.
  • Embeddings. A learned dense vector per merchant, if there is a neural model in the stack. Best quality when there is enough data per category, but it changes the deployment story.

The operational point I would raise unprompted: whatever encoding I pick has to be reproducible at serving time from the feature store, and it has to have a defined behaviour for a merchant ID the model has never seen — which happens on day one in production. An encoding without a documented unseen-value policy is a production incident waiting for its date.

Trap

One-hot on 200k values gives a 200k-wide sparse matrix. It is not catastrophic for a linear model with L2 and sparse storage — but almost every level has a handful of rows, so the coefficients are noise, and it will not fit in a tree-based model at all.
