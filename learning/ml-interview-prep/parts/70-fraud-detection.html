How to use this module

Read it once for the arc. Then close it and draw the architecture from memory on one sheet of paper — boxes and arrows, twelve minutes. Then open it and see what you dropped. The parts people drop are always the same: point-in-time correctness, the delayed-label horizon, and the randomised holdout.

In a real interview you get 45 minutes for this. Section 70.12 has the timing plan.

The prompt, and the first four minutes

The prompt

"Design a real-time fraud detection system for a payments company."

Do not start drawing. The first four minutes are requirements, and they are worth more than the next twenty. Every downstream decision — synchronous or asynchronous, model family, feature store, threshold — falls out of the answers.

What to askWhy it changes the design
Card payments, account takeover, or merchant fraud?Completely different entities, features and label sources. Assume card transactions unless told otherwise, and say so.
What is the decision — block, hold for review, or flag?Blocking needs a synchronous path inside the authorisation window. Review or flag can be asynchronous, which relaxes everything.
What is the latency budget?The single hardest constraint. It determines which features are reachable at all.
Volume and peak?Sizes the ingestion, the online store, and the serving fleet.
Base rate of fraud?Drives the imbalance strategy and makes PR-AUC the right metric.
Cost of a false positive versus a false negative?This is the threshold. Without it there is no defensible operating point.
When do labels arrive?Determines the retraining cadence and how much of your recent data is censored.
Regulatory constraints — explanations, appeals?Changes model family and forces per-decision logging.
The assumptions I would state and design against
  • Card transactions, decision = approve / hold-for-review / decline.
  • 5,000 transactions per second average, 20,000 at peak — a Black Friday factor of four.
  • Latency budget 100 ms p99 for the whole decision, of which the model service owns about 50 ms.
  • Base rate ~0.2 % fraud — roughly one in 500.
  • Cost asymmetry: a missed fraud costs the average fraudulent amount, say €200; a false decline costs the margin on the transaction plus a churn risk, call it €20 in expectation. Ratio about 10:1.
  • Labels: confirmed chargebacks arrive over 30–90 days; manual review outcomes in hours.
  • Regulated: declines need a reason and an appeal route.

Stating numbers you invented is fine and expected — say "assume" out loud. What is not fine is designing without them.

Two loops matter, and naming them early makes the whole design legible: the fast loop across the top, which must answer in 100 ms, and the slow loop down the right-hand side, which learns from what happened and is measured in weeks because that is how long labels take.

Data gathering — what you actually need

Fraud signal lives in the relationships between events, not in a single transaction. A transaction row on its own is nearly useless; the same row with "this account's fourteenth attempt in ten minutes from a device seen on nine other accounts" is decisive.

SourceWhat it gives youFreshness needed
Transaction streamAmount, merchant, MCC, currency, channel, timeNow — it is the event being scored
Account historyAge, tenure, historical spend profile, prior disputesHours
Device & sessionDevice fingerprint, IP, geo, session behaviourSeconds
Auth eventsLogins, failures, password and detail changesSeconds — a detail change minutes before a payment is a strong signal
Merchant profileMerchant risk history, category, chargeback rateDaily
Chargeback / dispute feedThe labelsDays to months
Manual review outcomesFast, high-quality labels on a biased subsetHours
Consortium / bureau signalsKnown-compromised cards, shared blocklistsDaily, and contractually constrained

Say this about labels, early. There are two label sources with different properties. Chargebacks are authoritative but slow and incomplete — not all fraud is reported. Manual review outcomes are fast but only exist for cases the current system already flagged, which makes them a biased sample of exactly the region the model already understands. Using them naively for training is the feedback loop (module 52) in its purest form.

Ingestion — Kafka, and what backpressure means here

Every source publishes to Kafka. Not because Kafka is fashionable, but because of three specific properties (module 50):

  • It is a log, not a queue. Retention means the fraud model, the analytics warehouse and the rules engine all consume the same stream independently, and it means you can replay — which is how you rebuild features after fixing a bug, and how you build training data at all.
  • Ordering is per partition, and the key picks the partition. Key by account_id and every event for one account is ordered and lands on one consumer, which is exactly what a stateful velocity computation needs. Different accounts stay parallel.
  • Consumer lag is an observable. It is the health metric for the whole slow path, and it degrades gracefully instead of dropping data.
Backpressure — the answer that scores

Black Friday arrives and the feature pipeline cannot keep up. The wrong answer is to increase the buffer, which converts a visible slowdown into an invisible one and then an out-of-memory crash. The four legitimate responses, in the order I would use them:

  1. Scale out consumers — free, up to the partition count, which is why you over-partition from the start (say 64 partitions for a workload needing 8 consumers today).
  2. Shed selectively. Drop the expensive enrichments first — graph features, bureau lookups — and score with a reduced feature set. A slightly worse decision now beats a perfect decision after the payment settled.
  3. Degrade to rules. The rules engine has no dependency on the feature pipeline. If the model path is unhealthy, decisions still happen, conservatively.
  4. Drop events — last resort, and only for non-decision telemetry, never for the transaction stream itself.

The general principle worth stating: the system must have a defined behaviour when it is overloaded, chosen in advance. Systems without one do not stay slow; they fall over.

Synchronous or asynchronous — the decision that shapes everything

This is the question the whole design turns on, and it is not one choice but three paths running together:

PathBudgetWhat it can useWhat it decides
Synchronous — in the authorisation~100 ms p99The event itself, pre-computed features from the online store, a fast modelApprove / decline / hold
Near-real-time — seconds behind1–30 sEverything above plus expensive graph and sequence featuresReverse a decision, freeze the account, queue for review
Batch — nightlyHoursFull history, heavy models, cross-entity analysisRing detection, account-level risk, investigation leads

Why not do everything synchronously? Because the budget forbids it. A graph feature requiring a multi-hop traversal cannot be computed in 100 ms at 20,000 requests per second. The design move is to pre-compute asynchronously and look up synchronously — the streaming job maintains the aggregates, the online store serves them by key in single-digit milliseconds, and the synchronous path does one read and one forward pass.

The corollary people miss: pre-computed means slightly stale. If the aggregate is 30 seconds behind, a rapid-fire attack inside that window is scored on pre-attack features. That is exactly why the near-real-time path exists — it re-scores with fresh state and can reverse a decision — and saying this shows you understand the limit of your own architecture.

Features — and the four that carry most of the signal

No code here, just the shapes (module 51's vocabulary throughout):

Velocity the workhorse

Counts and sums over trailing windows per entity: transactions per account in 1 h / 24 h / 7 d, distinct merchants, distinct countries, failed attempts.

Cheap, powerful, and the single most useful family in fraud. The implementation is the standard sliding-window structure — a per-key deque, or a bucketed counter when the window is coarse — maintained by the streaming job and materialised to the online store.

Deviation from the entity's own baseline high value

Amount as a z-score against this account's own history; is this merchant category new for them; is this hour of day unusual for them; distance from their usual geography.

Fraud is much better defined relative to a person than absolutely. €800 is unremarkable for one account and a five-sigma event for another — and this is why per-entity features beat global thresholds.

Graph / network expensive, distinctive

How many accounts share this device; component size in the account–device graph; how new the device is; shared IP or shipping address.

Catches organised fraud that per-account features cannot see. Computed asynchronously — union-find over the shared-identifier graph — materialised as a small number of scalars, and read like any other feature. Treat component size as a signal, never a rule — families and public terminals look like rings.

Sequence senior

The pattern of the last N events, not just their aggregate: a small-value test transaction followed by a large one; a password change then a new device then a payment.

Where a sequence model earns its place — and the honest framing is that it is a second model on the asynchronous path, feeding a score into the fast path as one more feature, not a replacement for the GBDT inside 100 ms.

Point-in-time correctness — the thing you must not fumble

Every one of those features is an aggregate over history, which means building training data is an as-of join, not a lookup. The value of chargeback_count for a transaction on 3 March must be what was known on 3 March — not today's value, which includes the chargeback caused by that very transaction.

Get this wrong and you get a model with a spectacular offline AUC that fails on its first live day, because the feature it leaned on does not exist at decision time. It is the most common serious bug in fraud modelling and interviewers ask about it precisely because it is a clean test of whether you have built one.

Drag the event line below and switch the join mode — the naive join reads a value created by the event it is trying to predict.

The structural fix is not vigilance, it is architecture: define each feature once in the feature store, let the offline store serve the as-of join and the online store serve the current value from the same definition, and — best of all — train on the feature vectors you actually logged at serving time. A logged vector cannot leak, because it is what the model saw. This kills leakage and train/serve skew with one mechanism, at the cost of needing production traffic before you can train, which is why you build the historical pipeline too.

Class imbalance and the model choice

At 0.2 % positive, a model that predicts "legitimate" for everything is 99.8 % accurate and worthless. That is the module 25 lesson, and here is what I would actually do:

  • Metric first. PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC — with this imbalance ROC-AUC is dominated by the enormous negative class and looks flattering. Then, more importantly, the business metric: expected cost at the chosen threshold, plus recall at a fixed review capacity, because the review team has a finite number of cases per day and that is a real constraint.
  • Do not oversample reflexively. Modern GBDTs handle imbalance well with scale_pos_weight or class weights. Resampling distorts the predicted probabilities, which matters here because the threshold is chosen on a cost basis and needs calibrated scores. If you resample, recalibrate afterwards.
  • Model family: gradient-boosted trees. Tabular, heterogeneous, mixed-type features with non-linear interactions — this is precisely where GBDTs still beat deep networks (module 22, and Grinsztajn's result). They are also fast enough for the budget, robust to unscaled features, and produce usable SHAP explanations for the appeal requirement.
  • Plus an unsupervised component. An isolation forest or autoencoder over the same features catches novel attack patterns with no labelled examples (module 24). It does not decide alone — it raises review cases and its score feeds the supervised model as a feature.
  • Plus rules. Not as a baseline to be beaten, but permanently. Rules deploy in an hour when a new attack appears, are explainable to a regulator, and cover the known cases; the model covers the tail. The two together are the system.

Training, tracking, and the threshold

The training pipeline is an orchestrated DAG (Airflow or Kubeflow, module 52), and it must be idempotent and backfillable — every task takes the logical date as a parameter and never calls now(), or replaying history silently produces different data.

StageWhat it doesThe trap
Label assemblyJoin chargebacks and review outcomes onto transactionsThe label horizon: only transactions older than ~90 days have complete labels
Feature retrievalPoint-in-time join from the offline storeSilent leakage if the as-of condition is dropped
SplitTemporal: train on the past, validate on the following period A random split leaks the future and inflates every number
TrainGBDT with class weighting, early stopping on the validation windowTuning on the test window until it looks good
CalibrateIsotonic or Platt on a held-out sliceSkipping it, then choosing a threshold from uncalibrated scores
ThresholdMinimise expected cost on validation, swept over candidate cut-offsChoosing it on the test set, or never revisiting it as the base rate drifts
EvaluateOverall and per segment: card type, geography, merchant category, customer tenureReporting one aggregate number and missing a segment that got much worse
RegisterMLflow: model, metrics, params, data version, code version, threshold Registering the model without the threshold — they are one artifact
The cost matrix, made concrete

With €200 per missed fraud and €20 per false decline, and 0.2 % base rate, in a million transactions: 2,000 fraudulent, 998,000 legitimate. Declining nothing costs 2,000 × €200 = €400,000. A model catching 70 % of fraud at a 0.5 % false-positive rate costs (600 × €200) + (4,990 × €20) = €120,000 + €99,800 ≈ €220,000 — a saving of about €180,000 per million transactions.

Two things to say alongside the arithmetic. First, the €20 is the shakiest number in the model, since the real cost of a wrongly declined customer includes churn you cannot easily attribute — so I would show the sensitivity, not a point estimate. Second, look at how flat the cost curve is near the optimum: a sharp minimum means the threshold is fragile and will need re-tuning as the base rate moves, which makes it a monitoring concern and not just a training one.

Serving and rollout

The model server is boring by design: a container, horizontally scaled, autoscaled on request rate with enough headroom for the peak factor, behind the decision service. The interesting part is how a new model gets there (module 53).

  1. Shadow, for a week. The candidate scores every live transaction; its output is logged and discarded. This validates the plumbing — that features are available, latency fits, and the live score distribution matches the offline one. Zero blast radius. A mismatch between shadow and offline score distributions is the earliest detectable sign of skew, and this alone justifies the week.
  2. Canary: 1 % → 5 % → 25 % → 100 %, with each stage held long enough for the guard metrics to be statistically meaningful. With a 0.2 % base rate, 1 % of traffic contains few positives — so at the small stages I am watching system metrics and the score distribution, and only at the later stages the fraud metrics. Saying this shows you understand what a canary can and cannot detect.
  3. Guard metrics with automatic rollback: p99 latency, error rate, decline rate, score distribution PSI against the shadow baseline, and the manual-review confirmation rate — which is the fastest available proxy for precision, since real labels are months away.
  4. Alert per variant, never blended. At 1 % traffic a doubled decline rate moves the blended number by a rounding error and nothing fires. Drive the toggle below and watch the blast radius change.
  5. Blue-green for the infrastructure underneath, so a rollback is a routing flip rather than a redeploy — and rehearse it, because an untested rollback is not a rollback.

Monitoring — and the part nobody prepares

Four layers, in the order they detect problems (module 54):

LayerSignalLatency to detection
Systemp99 latency, error rate, consumer lag, online-store hit rateSeconds
InputPer-feature PSI against the training reference, null rates, range violations, feature freshnessMinutes to hours
OutputScore distribution, decline rate, alert volumeMinutes
OutcomePrecision from review outcomes (hours), recall from chargebacks (weeks to months)Hours to months

If you keep one, keep the output layer. The score distribution needs no labels, is available instantly, and moves for nearly every failure that matters — a broken feature, a bad deploy, an upstream schema change, a genuine population shift. It never tells you why, but it reliably tells you to look.

The shape of a drift, not just its size

PSI above 0.25 means "significant", but the diagnosis is in the shape. Mass collapsing into one bin — especially a zero or null bin — is an upstream failure, not a changing world. Mass spreading smoothly is a population shift. Same PSI, completely different response. Inject the bug in the visualisation below and compare the contribution strip against a genuine mean shift.

The feedback loop — the senior part of the answer

This is where most candidates stop, and it is where the interesting problems start. Three intertwined issues:

Delayed labels

Chargebacks arrive over 30–90 days, so the last three months of data are censored: they look cleaner than they are simply because the fraud has not been reported yet.

Consequences: never evaluate recent performance as if labels were complete; define an explicit label horizon and only train on data past it; and when you need a fast read, use review outcomes and the confirmation rate as an early proxy while being explicit that they are biased.

The blocked-decision blind spot

You only ever learn the outcome of transactions you approved. For every transaction the model declined, the counterfactual is unobservable — you cannot know whether it was fraud.

Train on that data and the model learns to reproduce its own boundary: it becomes confident about the region it already blocks and blind to whether that region was ever right. This is the single most important thing to say in this walkthrough, because it is a genuine statistical problem that architecture alone cannot solve.

The randomised holdout — the fix

Approve a small random slice of traffic — a fraction of a percent — regardless of score, and record what happens.

It costs real money in let-through fraud, and it is the only way to get an unbiased estimate of what the model is missing. Budget it explicitly, size it against the precision you need on the estimate, exclude very high-value transactions to bound the downside, and present it to the business as the price of knowing whether the system works. Reservoir sampling is how you select it in a single pass over the stream, without knowing the day's volume in advance.

The retraining gate, then, is not a schedule. It is: assemble labels past the horizon → point-in-time features → temporal split → train → evaluate the challenger against the currently deployed champion on the same fresh window, not against the champion's historical offline number → check per-segment results → register with lineage → shadow → canary. Retraining nightly with no gate is how a bad week becomes a bad model, and drift alone should never trigger a deploy.
