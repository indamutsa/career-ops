Rapid fire — 20 seconds each

BASICWhat is a derivative?
The instantaneous rate of change — the slope of the tangent. It answers "if I nudge the input a little, how much does the output move, and in which direction?"
BASICWhat is a minimum?
A point where the gradient is zero and the surface curves upward around it — the second derivative is positive, or in many dimensions the Hessian is positive definite. Zero gradient alone is not enough: maxima and saddle points have it too.
MIDWhy does gradient descent work?
The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase, so the negative gradient is the steepest local decrease. Take a small step that way and the loss falls — provided the step is small enough that the local linear approximation still holds.
MIDWhat does an eigenvector mean, intuitively?
A direction the matrix does not rotate — it only stretches it. The eigenvalue is the stretch factor. They are the natural axes of the transformation, which is why PCA and the Hessian's curvature analysis both live in eigenvector coordinates.
MIDWhat is the rank of a matrix?
The number of genuinely independent directions it maps to — the dimension of its output space. Low rank means information is being collapsed, which is exactly what LoRA exploits and what PCA measures.
BASICExpectation versus variance?
Expectation is the long-run average; variance is the average squared distance from it. One says where, the other says how spread out.
MIDState Bayes' rule and say what each term is called.
P(H|E) = P(E|H)·P(H)/P(E) — posterior equals likelihood times prior over evidence. In words: start from what you believed, multiply by how well the hypothesis explains what you saw, normalise.
MIDMLE versus MAP?
MLE maximises the likelihood of the data; MAP maximises the posterior, which is the likelihood times a prior. MAP with a Gaussian prior is L2 regularisation, and with a Laplace prior it is L1.
BASICWhat does "stochastic" mean in stochastic gradient descent?
The gradient is estimated from a random sample of the data rather than all of it. It is a noisy but unbiased estimate — vastly cheaper per step, and the noise itself helps escape saddle points.
MIDWhat is entropy?
The average surprise of a distribution — the expected number of bits needed to encode a sample from it. Maximal when everything is equally likely, zero when one outcome is certain.
MIDWhy is cross-entropy the loss for classification?
Because minimising cross-entropy between the true distribution and the predicted one is exactly maximum likelihood for a categorical model. It also has the convenient gradient (p − y) through the softmax, which is why it trains well.
MIDWhat is KL divergence, and is it a distance?
The extra bits you pay for encoding data from P using a code built for Q. Not a distance: it is asymmetric and violates the triangle inequality. KL(P‖Q) = H(P,Q) − H(P).
SENIORWhat is a Lagrange multiplier, in words?
The exchange rate between the constraint and the objective — how much the optimum would improve if the constraint were relaxed by one unit. At a constrained optimum the objective's gradient is parallel to the constraint's, and λ is the ratio.
SENIORWhat are the KKT conditions for?
They generalise Lagrange multipliers to inequality constraints. The key one is complementary slackness: either a constraint is tight and its multiplier can be non-zero, or it is slack and its multiplier is zero. That is precisely what makes SVM support vectors sparse.
MIDConvex versus non-convex — why does it matter?
In a convex problem every local minimum is global, so optimisation has a guarantee. Neural networks are non-convex, so there is no such guarantee — yet in high dimensions most critical points are saddles rather than bad minima, which is why it works anyway.
MIDWhat is the chain rule and where do you meet it?
The derivative of a composition is the product of the derivatives. Backpropagation is the chain rule applied over a computation graph — and because it is a product, many small factors give vanishing gradients and many large ones give explosion.
SENIORWhat is the Hessian, and what does it tell you?
The matrix of second derivatives — the curvature. Its eigenvalues classify a critical point: all positive is a minimum, all negative a maximum, mixed signs a saddle. Its condition number governs how badly gradient descent zig-zags.
MIDWhy do we normalise features?
Because features on wildly different scales make the loss surface an elongated valley, and gradient descent oscillates across it instead of running down it. Normalising conditions the problem so one learning rate suits every direction.
SENIORWhat is the Laplace approximation?
Approximate a posterior by a Gaussian centred at its mode, with covariance the inverse Hessian of the negative log posterior there. A cheap route to uncertainty estimates — and the origin of the Fisher-information view used in Bayesian neural nets.
MIDState the central limit theorem and why you care.
The mean of many independent samples is approximately Gaussian regardless of the underlying distribution. It is why confidence intervals on A/B tests work, and why Gaussian assumptions are less reckless than they look.

Deep questions — 5 minutes each

MIDExplain backpropagation to a smart person who does not know calculus.
It is blame assignment. The network makes a prediction, we measure how wrong it is, and then we work backwards through the layers asking each weight "how much of this error was your fault?" — then nudge every weight down its share of the blame.

The analogy I would use: a kitchen where a dish comes out too salty. You do not blindly change every ingredient. You ask the last cook what they added, then the one before, tracing the salt back through the chain. Each cook's responsibility is their own contribution multiplied by how much the later steps amplified it — that multiplication down the chain is the chain rule.

Two consequences worth stating unprompted: it is a product along the chain, so long chains of small factors vanish and large ones explode — the whole motivation for residual connections and normalisation. And the backward pass costs about twice the forward pass, which is where the 6 in the 6ND training-compute formula comes from.

SENIORDerive why L2 regularisation equals a Gaussian prior.
Maximise the posterior instead of the likelihood, take logs, and a Gaussian prior on the weights contributes a −λ‖w‖² term. Maximising that posterior is minimising the loss plus λ‖w‖² — which is ridge regression.

The steps: MAP maximises P(w|D) ∝ P(D|w)·P(w). Take the negative log: −log P(D|w) − log P(w). The first term is the ordinary loss. If P(w) = N(0, σ²I), then −log P(w) = ‖w‖²/(2σ²) + const. So the objective is loss + λ‖w‖² with λ = 1/(2σ²).

What the equivalence buys you. The regularisation strength is a statement about prior belief: a small σ is a strong belief that weights are near zero, and that is a large λ. It also tells you what L1 is — the same derivation with a Laplace prior gives ‖w‖₁, and the Laplace distribution's sharp peak at zero is why L1 produces exact zeros while L2 only shrinks.

And the connection back to constrained optimisation: the penalty form is the Lagrangian of "minimise loss subject to ‖w‖² ≤ C", with λ as the multiplier. Three views of the same object — a constraint, a penalty, and a prior — which is a good thing to be able to move between on demand.

SENIORYour loss stops decreasing. Diagnose it mathematically.
Look at the gradient norm first. Near zero means you are at a critical point or the gradients have vanished; large and erratic means the step size is too big; small but non-zero with a flat loss usually means a poorly conditioned surface or a learning rate that has decayed to nothing.

Gradient norm ≈ 0. Either a genuine minimum, a saddle (far more likely in high dimensions), or dead units — ReLUs stuck at zero pass no gradient at all. Check the fraction of activations that are exactly zero; if it is high, that is your answer, and the fix is the initialisation or the activation function.

Gradient norm large and oscillating. The step is overshooting the valley. The loss will bounce rather than plateau. Lower the learning rate or clip.

Gradient small but non-zero, loss flat. Usually conditioning: the Hessian has a huge condition number, so progress along the flat directions is glacial while the steep ones are already converged. Normalisation, Adam's per-parameter scaling, or a preconditioner all attack this.

Also check the non-mathematical causes, because they are more common than any of the above: the learning-rate schedule has decayed to ~0; the data loader is serving the same batch; the loss is being computed on the wrong tensor; or the model has simply converged and the remaining error is irreducible — which you test by checking whether it can overfit a single batch to near-zero loss. If it cannot, the bug is in the model or the data, not the optimisation.

SENIORExplain PCA from first principles.
Find the directions along which the data varies most. Centre the data, compute the covariance matrix, take its eigenvectors — those are the principal components, and their eigenvalues are the variance captured along each.

Two derivations that arrive at the same place, and being able to give both is the senior signal. Maximum variance: find the unit vector w maximising Var(Xw) = wᵀΣw subject to ‖w‖ = 1. Form the Lagrangian, differentiate, and you get Σw = λw — the eigenvector equation, with λ the variance captured. Minimum reconstruction error: find the k-dimensional subspace minimising the squared distance from the data to its projection. Same answer, because total variance is fixed, so maximising what you keep is minimising what you lose.

The relationship to SVD: PCA on centred X is the SVD of X. The right singular vectors are the components and the squared singular values are proportional to the eigenvalues — and in practice you compute it that way, because forming XᵀX squares the condition number and loses precision.

What to say about limitations, since it is usually the follow-up: PCA is linear, so it cannot unfold a curved manifold; it is scale-dependent, so unstandardised features let the largest-unit feature dominate; the components are orthogonal by construction, which is a mathematical convenience with no reason to match reality; and maximum variance is not the same as maximum usefulness — the discriminative direction can have small variance, which is precisely the case where PCA before a classifier hurts.

The whiteboard prompt

Do this on paper, out loud, in 10 minutes

"Derive the gradient of the logistic-regression loss, and explain what the result means."

You should reach ∇ = Xᵀ(σ(Xw) − y) and be able to say the sentence that matters: the gradient is the input matrix times the prediction error — the same form as linear regression's, which is not a coincidence but a property of the exponential family with its canonical link. Say what happens when the prediction is right (the error term vanishes, no update) and when the model is confidently wrong (the error saturates at 1, so the update is bounded — which is why cross-entropy trains where squared error stalls).
