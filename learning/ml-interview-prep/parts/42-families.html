The analogy

BERT is a proofreader: it sees the whole page at once, including what comes after, and fills in the blanked-out words. Excellent judgement, cannot write a page.

GPT is a writer: it only ever sees what it has written so far, and its whole job is the next word. Weaker at reading comprehension per parameter, but it can produce.

The terms

Masked language modelling (MLM) mid

Hide ~15 % of tokens and predict them from both directions. BERT's actual recipe replaced 80 % of those with [MASK], 10 % with a random token, 10 % unchanged.

That awkward 80/10/10 exists because [MASK] never appears at fine-tuning time — a train/serve mismatch patched by construction rather than by design.

Causal language modelling basic

Predict the next token, everywhere, from the left context only.

Every position yields a training signal from one forward pass, so a sequence of length n gives n supervised examples rather than BERT's 0.15n. That efficiency compounds enormously at scale.

Span corruption mid

T5's objective: replace contiguous spans with sentinel tokens and have the decoder emit the missing spans in order.

Harder than single-token masking, and it forces the model to produce variable-length output — closer to real generation than MLM.

Text-to-text framing mid

T5's other contribution: cast every task as string in, string out — classification, translation, regression, all of it.

The idea that made instruction-tuned models conceivable. Prompting is this framing taken to its conclusion.

[CLS] pooling mid

BERT prepends a [CLS] token whose final hidden state is used as the sentence representation.

Raw [CLS] and raw mean-pooled BERT vectors are poor for similarity search — worse than averaged GloVe on some benchmarks. SBERT exists because of this: it retrains BERT with a contrastive objective so that cosine distance actually means something.

Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder senior

Bi-encoder: embed query and document separately, compare with cosine — the document side is precomputable, so it scales to millions. Cross-encoder: feed the pair together through the model — far more accurate, and it must run once per candidate.

The standard production shape: bi-encoder retrieves the top ~100, cross-encoder reranks them. Encoder models are alive and well in this role.

Questions

MIDWhy did decoder-only models win?
Better training-signal efficiency, a single objective that covers every task through prompting, no fine-tuning infrastructure needed, and a simpler architecture that scales cleanly. Generation was the capability that turned out to matter most.

Signal efficiency. Causal LM gets a loss at every position from one forward pass; MLM gets one at 15 % of positions. Roughly 6× more learning per token of data, and when the binding constraint became "how much can we train on", that difference compounded.

One objective, every task. BERT needed a task-specific head and a fine-tuning run per task. GPT-3 showed the same weights doing translation, summarisation, classification and Q&A from prompts alone. That collapsed the deployment story from "a model per task" to "one model, many prompts" — an operational change as much as a research one.

Generation is a superset. A generative model can be asked to classify; a classifier cannot be asked to write. Once quality was adequate, the generative model absorbed the other family's use cases.

Simplicity scales. No encoder stack, no cross-attention, no asymmetry — one stack, one mask. Easier to shard, easier to optimise, fewer things to tune.

The honest qualifier: the encoder-only advantage was real and is not gone. Bidirectional context is genuinely better for understanding a fixed input, and for classification or retrieval a 110M-parameter DeBERTa still beats prompting a 70B model on both accuracy and cost. Decoder-only won the frontier, not every job.

MIDWhen would you still choose BERT over an LLM?
High-volume, fixed, well-labelled classification or extraction under a latency or cost budget — and embedding-based retrieval, where the whole architecture is encoder-based anyway.

Cost and latency. A fine-tuned DeBERTa-v3-base is ~180M parameters, runs on CPU in a few milliseconds, costs effectively nothing per call, and can be deployed inside your own network. Calling a frontier model for every one of ten million daily documents is orders of magnitude more expensive and slower, for a task where the small model is often more accurate because it was trained on your labels.

Accuracy when labels exist. With a few thousand in-domain labels, fine-tuned encoders typically beat zero- or few-shot prompting on narrow classification. Bidirectional attention over a fixed input is the right inductive bias for the job.

Determinism and auditability. A classifier returns a calibrated probability for a fixed label set — you can threshold it, monitor its drift, and explain it. An LLM returns text that must be parsed and can drift with a provider's silent model update.

Retrieval. Embedding models are encoders, and reranking cross-encoders are encoders. Every RAG system in production is running encoder models whether the team thinks of it that way or not.

The pragmatic hybrid I would propose: use the LLM to label — a few thousand examples cheaply — then distil into a small encoder for serving. You get the LLM's quality at the encoder's cost, and the distilled model is yours to monitor and version.

SENIORDoes encoder–decoder still have a reason to exist?
Yes, where input and output are genuinely different objects and the input is fully available up front — translation, speech recognition, some structured transformation. But decoder-only with a long context absorbs most of it.

The architectural case for it: the encoder processes the source bidirectionally, so every source token sees full context, which a decoder-only model cannot do for the prompt portion. Cross-attention gives the decoder a clean, dedicated channel to the source, and the source needs encoding only once regardless of how long the output is.

Why it lost anyway: concatenation works. Put the source and the target in one sequence and a decoder-only model does the same job with half the architecture, no cross-attention, and no encoder/decoder capacity split to tune. Since it also gets the prompting and scaling advantages from the previous question, the extra machinery stopped paying for itself.

Where it genuinely persists: Whisper for speech, because audio and text are different modalities and the asymmetry is real. Specialised translation systems. And, notably, the encoder–decoder shape reappears in multimodal models — a vision encoder feeding a language decoder through cross-attention is exactly this pattern under another name.

The empirical data point worth citing: Google's UL2 and the T5 line found encoder–decoder better at fixed compute for fine-tuned tasks, while decoder-only was better for few-shot prompting. Both results are real; the industry optimised for the second because that is where the product was.

MIDWhy can't you just use BERT for generation?
It was never trained to produce a sequence — only to fill blanks given both sides. Generating left to right means every step is out of distribution, and there is no mechanism for deciding how long the output should be.

Concretely: BERT's training always had right-hand context. Generation has none, so at inference it is being asked a question it never saw. People have tried — masked-LM decoding by iteratively unmasking — and it produces incoherent text, because each token is predicted independently given the current partial fill, with no factorisation of the joint distribution. Causal LM factorises the joint exactly, one conditional at a time; MLM does not factorise anything.

There is also no length mechanism. A causal model emits an end-of-sequence token when it decides it is finished; BERT fills a predetermined number of [MASK] slots, so you must know the answer's length before generating it.

The interesting exception is diffusion language models, which do generate by iterative denoising over the whole sequence at once. They are a live research direction precisely because parallel generation could beat sequential decoding on latency — so the idea is not wrong, it just needs a training objective built for it rather than BERT's.
