Metrics & evaluation The module you cannot skip. More interviews are lost on "what's your metric and why" than on any algorithm question, because it is where modelling meets the business — and because imbalanced problems like fraud make every naive metric lie to you.

Start here: accuracy is almost always the wrong metric

The 99.9 % model that is worthless

Fraud is 0.1 % of transactions. A model that outputs "not fraud" for everything is 99.9 % accurate and has caught zero fraud. It cost nothing to build and it is strictly worse than useless, because it consumes a serving budget to do nothing.

Accuracy weights every error equally. Real problems never do — a missed fraud costs €200, a false alarm costs thirty seconds of a customer's patience. Any metric that treats those as equal is measuring the wrong thing.

So the first question in any evaluation discussion is not "which metric" but "what does each kind of mistake cost?" Everything else follows from that answer.

The confusion matrix and everything derived from it

Predicted fraudPredicted legit
Actually fraudTP — caught itFN — missed fraud, you eat the loss
Actually legitFP — blocked a good customer TN — correctly left alone
Precision basic TP / (TP + FP)

Of everything you flagged, how much was actually fraud.

"When I raise the alarm, how often am I right?"

The metric your operations team feels. Low precision means analysts wading through junk and customers being blocked for nothing.

Recall / sensitivity / TPR basic TP / (TP + FN)

Of all the actual fraud, how much you caught.

"How much of the real problem did I find?"

The metric your finance team feels — it maps directly to loss avoided.

F1 basic

The harmonic mean of precision and recall. Harmonic, so it punishes imbalance between them — 0.9 and 0.1 gives 0.18, not 0.5.

A reasonable default when you have no cost information. As soon as you do, F1 is inferior to a cost-weighted metric, because it silently asserts that precision and recall matter equally, and they never do.

Specificity / TNR mid TN / (TN + FP)

Of all the legitimate traffic, how much you correctly left alone. FPR = 1 − specificity.

ROC-AUC basic

Plot TPR against FPR across every threshold; the area beneath is the probability that a random positive scores above a random negative.

Threshold-free, so it measures pure ranking ability. Its interpretation is genuinely useful and its behaviour under imbalance is genuinely misleading — see 25.4.

PR-AUC / average precision mid

Precision against recall across thresholds. Baseline equals the positive rate, so 0.001 for fraud rather than 0.5.

The right summary metric for a heavily imbalanced problem.

Log loss mid

Cross-entropy on the predicted probabilities. Punishes confident mistakes severely.

The only common metric that scores calibration and ranking together. A model can have excellent AUC and terrible log loss if its probabilities are systematically off.

Brier score mid

Mean squared error on the predicted probabilities. Like log loss but bounded and far less brutal about confident errors.

Decomposes into calibration plus refinement, which makes it a nice diagnostic rather than just a number.

Calibration senior

Whether a predicted 0.7 actually happens about 70 % of the time.

A weather forecaster who says 30 % rain and is right 30 % of the time is calibrated. One who says 30 % and it always rains has good discrimination in the sense of ordering days, and terrible calibration.

Non-negotiable whenever the score feeds a decision with money attached — an expected-value calculation, a cost-matrix threshold, or an amount to hold.

ECE — expected calibration error senior

Bin predictions by confidence, compare each bin's average prediction to its actual rate, average the gaps weighted by bin size.

Bin-count-sensitive and it can hide compensating errors in opposite directions, so show the reliability diagram alongside it rather than the number alone.

Precision@k mid

Precision restricted to the top k highest-scoring cases.

Often the real production metric: if analysts review 200 cases a day, only precision within the top 200 matters. Metrics over the whole distribution are academic when capacity is fixed.

Picking the threshold from a cost matrix

A classifier outputs a probability. The threshold is a business decision, and it has a derivable optimum rather than a conventional 0.5.

Flag a case when the expected cost of flagging is lower than the expected cost of not flagging. With p = P(fraud), CFP the cost of a false alarm and CFN the cost of a miss:

flag when  p · CFN > (1 − p) · CFP  ⟹  threshold = CFP / (CFP + CFN) 0.5 is only correct when the two costs are equal

Put fraud numbers in: a miss costs €200, a false alarm costs €2 of analyst time plus customer friction. Threshold = 2 / 202 ≈ 0.01. You should flag anything above a 1 % probability of fraud — fifty times more aggressive than the default. That single calculation is one of the highest-value things in this whole course, because it reframes "tuning the threshold" from guesswork into arithmetic.

Two caveats to say out loud

It requires calibration. The formula uses p as a real probability. If your model is a boosted tree with scale_pos_weight set, the number it emits is not a probability and the derived threshold is meaningless. Calibrate first, then threshold.

It ignores capacity. A 1 % threshold may generate 40,000 alerts a day for a team that can review 200. In practice you take the cost-optimal threshold as your ceiling and then set the operating point at whatever fills the queue — which is precisely why precision@k is the metric that gets reported.

ROC-AUC versus PR-AUC — the imbalance question

This is the single most common evaluation question for fraud, medical, and any rare-event role. The mechanism is worth understanding rather than memorising.

ROC uses FPR = FP / (FP + TN). With 0.1 % fraud, TN is enormous — around a million per thousand positives. So you can produce 1,000 false positives and move FPR by only 0.001. The huge denominator hides your false positives. A model can look excellent on ROC while its flagged queue is 90 % junk.

PR uses precision = TP / (TP + FP). No TN anywhere. Those same 1,000 false positives crush precision immediately. PR-AUC reports what the operations team will actually experience.

Report both, and know why each one is there.

The SMOTE trap — worth knowing in detail

SMOTE interpolates between a minority point and one of its neighbours. Three problems:

  • It destroys calibration. You have changed the class prior, so the model's output is no longer P(fraud) on real traffic.
  • It interpolates in feature space — which is meaningless for categorical or one-hot features, where the midpoint between "Spain" and "Poland" is not a country.
  • Fraud positives are not one cluster. They are many distinct attack patterns. Interpolating between two unrelated attacks produces a synthetic point that corresponds to no real fraud at all, and the model learns a boundary around fiction.

And the implementation error that invalidates results: applying SMOTE before the split, so synthetic points derived from training rows land in validation. Published comparisons repeatedly find that a well-tuned model with a properly chosen threshold matches or beats SMOTE. Saying that calmly, with the reasoning, is a strong signal.

Offline metrics are not the goal

The senior version of this whole module is knowing that AUC is a proxy. Three layers, and you should be able to connect them:

  1. Model metrics — AUC, PR-AUC, log loss. Fast, cheap, computed offline. Necessary for iteration, and they do not pay anyone.
  2. Decision metrics — precision@capacity, alert volume, review time per case, block rate. What the operating point produces once a threshold exists.
  3. Business metrics — fraud loss in euros, false-decline revenue lost, analyst cost, customer churn from wrongful blocks. The actual objective.

Two things follow. First, a model improvement that does not move layer 3 is not an improvement, and it is common for a 2-point AUC gain to change nothing because the operating point sits in a region the gain did not touch. Second, layers 1 and 3 can diverge: catching more fraud by blocking more transactions can lose more revenue in false declines than it saves in fraud. That is why fraud teams measure net value, and why the interview answer is "I would A/B test against the business metric," not "AUC went up".

Questions

BASICExplain precision and recall, and when you would favour each.
Precision is how often you are right when you raise the alarm. Recall is how much of the real problem you found. You favour precision when false alarms are expensive, recall when misses are.

The two examples that make it concrete and opposite:

  • Recall matters more: screening for an aggressive but treatable cancer. A missed case may be fatal; a false alarm costs a follow-up test. Set the threshold low, accept the false positives.
  • Precision matters more: automatically blocking a customer's card without review. Each false positive is a stranded customer and a support call, so you only act when you are confident.

They trade off through the threshold: lowering it always raises recall and lowers precision. So "improve precision" without saying what happens to recall is not a well-formed request — the only way to improve both at once is a better model, not a different threshold.

What I would add: in fraud we usually do not pick one. There are several thresholds — allow below one, step up to a 3-D Secure challenge in the middle, block or queue for review at the top — and each band has its own precision target because each has a different cost of being wrong.

MIDYour fraud model has 0.94 ROC-AUC. Is that good?
Unknowable from that number alone, and on a 0.1 % positive rate it is probably less impressive than it sounds. I would ask for PR-AUC and precision at our actual review capacity.

The reason: ROC's false-positive rate has all the true negatives in its denominator. At 0.1 % fraud, a million legitimate transactions swamp it, so ten thousand false positives shift FPR by 1 % while making the flagged queue almost entirely junk. ROC-AUC of 0.94 is compatible with a review queue that is 95 % noise.

What I would ask for instead: PR-AUC, whose baseline is the 0.001 positive rate rather than 0.5; precision at the top k where k is what analysts can actually review, since that is the number the operation experiences; recall at our chosen operating point, because that is the loss we avoid; and the calibration curve if any downstream decision uses the score as a probability.

And the framing question underneath: good compared to what? Compared to the current rules engine, compared to last quarter's model, compared to the theoretical ceiling given label noise. A number with no baseline is not evidence.

Trap

If someone reports an AUC above about 0.99 on a fraud problem, my first hypothesis is leakage, not brilliance — module 20's checklist applies before any celebration.

SENIORHow do you choose the classification threshold?
Derive it from the cost matrix — the optimal threshold is CFP/(CFP + CFN) — then constrain it by review capacity, and verify online.

The derivation: flag when the expected cost of flagging beats the expected cost of not flagging, i.e. when p·CFN > (1−p)·CFP. Rearranged, the threshold is CFP/(CFP + CFN). With a €200 miss and a €2 false alarm that is about 0.01 — nowhere near 0.5, and the gap between those two numbers is worth a lot of money.

Two conditions on using it:

  • The score must be calibrated, because the formula treats p as a real probability. If I used class weighting or resampling, it is not one, and I would fit isotonic regression or Platt scaling on a held-out set first.
  • Capacity binds. The cost-optimal threshold may generate far more alerts than the team can review. Then the effective operating point is set by capacity, and I would report precision@k rather than pretending the theoretical threshold is in use.

How I would refine it in practice: costs are not constant per case — a missed €5,000 transaction costs more than a missed €20 one — so the proper form is a per-transaction expected-value rule using the amount, not a single global threshold. That is a strong thing to raise unprompted. Then several bands rather than one cut: allow, challenge, review, block. And finally I would validate the choice with an A/B test on net business value, because the offline cost matrix is an estimate and the customer-friction cost in particular is usually a guess.

Trap

The threshold must be chosen on validation data and then held fixed for the test evaluation. Choosing it on the test set and reporting the resulting metrics is the same overfitting as any other hyperparameter tuned on test.

SENIORWhat is calibration, how do you measure it, and how do you fix it?
A model is calibrated when its predicted 0.7 events happen about 70 % of the time. Measure it with a reliability diagram plus ECE or Brier; fix it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression fitted on held-out data.

Why it is separate from accuracy: calibration and discrimination are independent. A model that outputs exactly half the true probability for every case has perfect ranking and perfect AUC, and every probability it emits is wrong by a factor of two. If the score only feeds a ranking, that is fine. If it feeds an expected-value calculation, a cost-matrix threshold, or a reserve amount, it is a direct financial error.

Measuring: a reliability diagram — bin by predicted probability, plot mean prediction against observed frequency, compare to the diagonal. ECE summarises the gaps into one number, and Brier score combines calibration and refinement. I would always show the diagram, because ECE can hide errors that cancel between bins.

What decalibrates a model: class weights and resampling, which change the implied prior; SMOTE; boosted trees, which tend to be over-confident at the extremes; SVMs, whose outputs are distances rather than probabilities; and modern deep networks, which are famously over-confident.

Fixing it: Platt scaling — fit a one-dimensional logistic regression on the model's scores — when the distortion is roughly sigmoidal and data is limited. Isotonic regression when there is plenty of held-out data and the distortion is an arbitrary monotone shape; more flexible, more prone to overfitting on small sets. Either way, fit it on a separate calibration split, never on the training data — a model's training predictions are over-confident in exactly the way you are trying to correct.

And in production, calibration drifts even when ranking does not: the base rate moves and the probabilities go stale. Monitoring predicted-versus-observed rates over time is a standard monitoring signal — module 54.

MIDHow do you handle a 0.1 % positive rate?
Fix the metric first, then the threshold, and only then consider touching the data. Most of the value is in the first two, and resampling is the last resort rather than the first move.
  1. Stop using accuracy. Switch to PR-AUC for model selection and precision@capacity plus recall for the operating point.
  2. Move the threshold, not the data. A model trained on the natural distribution with a threshold at 0.01 is often as good as anything resampling produces, and it keeps its probabilities meaningful.
  3. Stratify every split, so folds do not end up with a handful of positives and metrics that are pure noise. With very few positives I would also prefer repeated CV, since the variance across folds is what dominates the estimate.
  4. Then, if needed, class weights — and recalibrate afterwards, because they break the probabilities.
  5. Undersample the majority if training cost is the binding constraint. It works, it shifts the base rate, and it needs the same recalibration.
  6. Reconsider the framing at extremes. Below roughly 0.01 % there may not be enough positives to learn a boundary at all, and anomaly detection plus a review queue is the better structure — module 24.
  7. Get more positives. Longer history, better labelling, active learning on the uncertain cases. Rarely mentioned and frequently the highest-return option.

What I would avoid: SMOTE as a reflex. It breaks calibration, interpolates nonsensically across categorical features, and invents points between unrelated attack patterns. I would only reach for it after the threshold approach had been tried and measured.

SENIORYour offline AUC improved by 3 points but the A/B test shows no lift. What happened?
Most likely the gain is in a region of the score distribution that the operating point never touches — or the offline evaluation and the online system are not measuring the same thing.

Causes, roughly in order of how often they are the answer:

  • The gain is off the operating point. AUC integrates over every threshold. If the improvement is concentrated in the middle of the ranking and you operate at the top 0.5 %, nothing changes where it counts. Check precision@k at the actual threshold — the usual culprit.
  • Threshold not re-tuned. A new model has a different score distribution. The old threshold now corresponds to a different alert volume, and you may be operating somewhere much worse. Thresholds must be re-derived per model.
  • Train/serve skew. The features computed online differ from the offline ones — a different window, a null handled differently, a stale feature. The deployed model is not the model you evaluated. Log serving features and compare distributions against training; this is why feature stores exist.
  • Leakage offline. The offline gain was partly a feature that is not truly available at serving time, so it vanished in production.
  • The business metric is not the model metric. Catching more fraud while declining more good customers can be net negative. Check false declines and net value, not just fraud caught.
  • Underpowered test. With a 0.1 % base rate, detecting a small relative change needs a very large sample. Compute the power before concluding "no lift" — quite often the honest answer is that the experiment could never have detected the effect.
  • Feedback loop. Blocked transactions produce no label, so the online evaluation sees a censored population. Module 54.

The lesson I would state at the end: offline metrics are for iteration speed, online metrics decide. And the fact that they disagreed is itself information worth chasing down, because whatever caused the gap will cause it again on the next model.

MIDWhich regression metric do you use, and why?
MAE when outliers should not dominate, RMSE when large errors are disproportionately costly, MAPE only when the target is strictly positive and relative error is what matters — and I would always compare against a naive baseline.
  • MAE — average absolute error, in the target's own units, so it is directly explainable. Robust to outliers. Optimising it predicts the conditional median.
  • RMSE — squares the errors, so one large miss dominates. Correct when the cost of an error grows faster than linearly. Optimising it predicts the conditional mean. That mean-versus-median distinction is the crisp way to answer "what is the real difference".
  • MAPE — percentage error. Intuitive for demand forecasting, and it explodes near zero and asymmetrically punishes over-prediction. sMAPE and MASE exist to patch that.
  • R² — proportion of variance explained. Fine for communication, misleading for comparison across datasets since it depends on the target's variance.
  • Quantile / pinball loss — when you need an interval or a deliberately asymmetric penalty, such as inventory where a stockout costs more than overstock.

The habit that matters more than the choice: compare against a naive baseline — predict the mean, or for a time series predict yesterday's value. A great many production forecasts fail to beat "same as last week", and knowing that is what makes the metric meaningful.
