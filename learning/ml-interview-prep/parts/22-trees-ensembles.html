Part B · Classical ML · Module 22

Trees & ensembles Gradient-boosted trees are still the best model for tabular data, which means they are still the model behind most fraud, credit and churn systems in production. This module is about why that is true, and about the difference between bagging and boosting — which is the most reliably asked question in classical ML.

A decision tree is twenty questions with a rule for choosing the question

The founding idea

You are guessing an animal. You do not ask "is it a pangolin?" first — you ask "does it have fur?", because whatever the answer, it eliminates about half the possibilities.

A decision tree does exactly that, and the only real algorithmic content is how it decides which question is the most informative one to ask next. Everything else — pruning, forests, boosting — is built on top of that single greedy choice.

Concretely: at each node, try every feature and every threshold, score each candidate split by how much it reduces impurity, take the best one, recurse. That is CART. It is greedy — it never reconsiders an earlier split in the light of a later one — which is why a single tree is unstable and why the ensembles exist.

The terms

Node / split / leaf basic

A node asks a question, a split is the question, a leaf holds the prediction — the majority class, or the mean of the targets that landed there.

Gini impurity basic 1 − Σp²

The probability that two items drawn at random from the node have different labels. 0 when the node is pure.

A bag of marbles: how likely are two random draws to be different colours?

Information gain mid

The reduction in entropy achieved by a split — the mutual information from module 13, computed per candidate split.

Gini and entropy pick the same split the overwhelming majority of the time. Gini is marginally cheaper because it avoids a logarithm. Do not spend interview time on the difference; do know they are near-equivalent.

Pruning mid

Cutting branches back after growing. A fully grown tree fits every training row exactly and generalises terribly.

Modern practice is mostly pre-pruning via max_depth, min_samples_leaf and min_child_weight — cheaper and easier to tune.

Bagging mid

Train many models in parallel on bootstrap samples of the data, then average them. Reduces variance.

Asking a hundred people to guess the weight of an ox. Individually they are scattered; the average is famously close.

Random forest mid

Bagging plus one extra trick: at each split, consider only a random subset of features. This decorrelates the trees, which is what makes the averaging work.

Without the feature subsetting, every tree finds the same dominant feature first and they all look alike — and averaging near-identical models buys you nothing.

Boosting mid

Train models sequentially, each one focused on what the previous ones got wrong. Reduces bias.

A student reviewing only the questions they missed, over and over. Bagging is a committee of independent experts; boosting is one student improving.

Gradient boosting mid

Each new tree is fitted to the gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions — for squared error, that is literally the residuals.

The name is exact: it is gradient descent, where each step is a whole tree added to the ensemble rather than a nudge to a parameter vector.

Learning rate / shrinkage mid

Multiply each tree's contribution by a small factor (0.01–0.1) so no single tree dominates.

Lower learning rate plus more trees almost always generalises better, at the cost of training time. This is the main quality-versus-cost knob in boosting.

Weak learner mid

A model barely better than guessing. Boosting's theoretical claim is that you can combine arbitrarily many weak learners into an arbitrarily strong one.

In practice: depth-3 to depth-8 trees. Deeper "weak" learners overfit and the ensemble stops improving.

Monotonic constraint senior

Force the model's output to be non-decreasing (or non-increasing) in a chosen feature, enforced at split time.

Widely used in credit and fraud. "More prior chargebacks must never lower the risk score" is a business rule the model can be made to obey structurally, rather than hoped for. It also makes the model easier to defend and often more robust under drift.

Out-of-bag estimate mid

Each bootstrap sample leaves out about 37 % of rows; score each tree on its own leftovers and you get a free validation estimate.

Why 37 %? (1 − 1/n)ⁿ → 1/e ≈ 0.368. That derivation is a pleasant thing to have ready.

Isolation forest senior

Trees built on random splits; anomalies get isolated in very few splits because they sit apart from everything else.

Unsupervised anomaly detection for the cold-start phase of a fraud system, before you have labels — module 24 covers it properly.

The one-line version

Bagging averages away the noise; boosting chips away at the error. That is why bagging fixes variance and boosting fixes bias — and why boosting can overfit while bagging essentially cannot.

Why gradient boosting still wins on tabular data

This question comes up constantly, and "it just does" is not an answer. The real reasons are all about the shape of tabular data:

  • Tabular features are heterogeneous. Age, country, a currency amount, a count, a boolean. Trees split on one feature at a time and are completely indifferent to scale and to monotone transforms. A neural net needs every one of those normalised, embedded, and coaxed.
  • The true functions are often piecewise-constant with sharp thresholds. "Risk jumps above €500" is native to a tree and awkward for a smooth function approximator, which has to spend capacity building the step out of smooth pieces.
  • Trees are robust to irrelevant features. An uninformative column is simply never chosen for a split. A dense network has to learn to zero it out, and with limited data it partly fails.
  • The inductive bias matches. This is the module-20 point: CNNs assume locality, transformers assume sequence structure, and trees assume the target is well described by axis-aligned regions. Tabular data usually satisfies the last assumption and rarely satisfies the first two.
  • The data is small by deep-learning standards. Tens of thousands of rows is a large tabular dataset and a tiny deep-learning one.

Where neural networks do take over: when there is a high-cardinality categorical worth embedding, when the tabular data must be fused with text or images, or when you want one model serving many related tasks. Saying that boundary out loud is what distinguishes a considered answer from a slogan.

They are far more alike than different — all three are histogram-based gradient boosting with regularisation, and on most datasets a well-tuned instance of any of them lands within noise of the others. The differentiator worth actually knowing is CatBoost's ordered target statistics, because it is a principled solution to the exact leakage problem from module 21: it computes each row's category encoding using only rows that came before it in a random permutation, so a row never contributes to its own encoding.

Questions

BASICHow does a decision tree decide where to split?
It tries every feature and every threshold, scores each candidate by how much it reduces impurity, and greedily takes the best one — then repeats inside each child.

Impurity is Gini or entropy for classification, variance for regression. The score of a split is the parent's impurity minus the weighted average of the children's, so a split that separates the classes cleanly scores high and a split that leaves both children as mixed as the parent scores zero.

Two properties worth adding because they explain everything downstream: it is greedy, so it never reconsiders an early split in light of a later one and can miss a combination that only pays off two levels down; and it is unstable — change a few rows and the top split can change, which cascades into an entirely different tree. That instability is exactly the high variance that bagging was invented to average away.

Trap

"Gini or entropy — which is better?" Neither, materially. They agree on the chosen split the vast majority of the time. Gini is slightly cheaper as it avoids a log. An interviewer asking this is usually checking whether you will invent a difference that is not there.

MIDBagging versus boosting — explain the difference and when each wins.
Bagging trains many independent models in parallel and averages them to cut variance. Boosting trains models in sequence, each fixing the previous ones' errors, to cut bias.

Bagging. Bootstrap the data, fit a deep tree on each sample, average the predictions. Each tree is individually overfitted but wrong in a different direction, and the errors partially cancel. Random Forest adds per-split feature subsampling to decorrelate the trees further, because averaging highly correlated models buys almost nothing.

Boosting. Fit a shallow tree, look at what the ensemble still gets wrong, fit the next tree to that error, add it with a small learning rate, repeat. Each tree individually underfits; together they build up a precise function.

The consequence that gets asked as a follow-up: adding trees to a random forest cannot overfit — the average converges — whereas adding trees to a boosted model can, because each new tree is actively fitting the residual and will eventually start fitting noise. That is why boosting needs early stopping on a validation set and bagging does not.

When I pick which: Random Forest for a fast strong baseline, a very noisy target, or when I have no time to tune. Boosting when I want the best number and can afford to tune the learning rate, depth and regularisation. On tabular problems boosting usually wins by a meaningful margin.

Trap

"Boosting reduces bias, so use deep trees for even less bias." No — deep base learners make each step overfit the residual, and the ensemble stops improving. The whole method depends on the learners being weak.

MIDWhy is gradient boosting called gradient boosting?
Because each tree is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current predictions. It is gradient descent performed in function space rather than parameter space.

The chain: you have a current ensemble F(x). You want to reduce a loss L(y, F(x)). Ordinary gradient descent would nudge parameters; here you instead ask "in what direction should each prediction move?", which is −∂L/∂F evaluated at each training point. Those numbers are your new targets. Fit a tree to them, scale it by the learning rate, add it to F. Repeat.

For squared error, −∂L/∂F is exactly the residual y − F(x), which is why the original presentation of boosting is "fit the next tree to the residuals" — that is the special case, not the general rule. For log loss the pseudo-residual is y − p, which is the same clean expression that appeared in module 21.

The value of the general framing is that it makes the loss pluggable: swap in quantile loss for prediction intervals, Huber for robustness to outliers, or a ranking loss for search — same algorithm, different gradient. XGBoost extends this to a second-order approximation, using both gradient and Hessian to choose the leaf values, which is where its speed and stability advantage comes from.

SENIORWhy do gradient-boosted trees still beat deep learning on tabular data?
Because their inductive bias matches what tabular data actually is: heterogeneous features, sharp thresholds, many irrelevant columns, and not very many rows.

Point by point:

  • Heterogeneity. Trees split one feature at a time and are invariant to monotone transforms and to scale. A network needs each feature normalised and embedded, and gets that wrong more often than people admit.
  • Sharp decision boundaries. Real tabular targets often have genuine thresholds. Trees represent a step with one split; a smooth network has to approximate it and spends capacity doing so.
  • Irrelevant features. A tree simply never splits on a useless column. An MLP has to learn to suppress it, and with limited data it only partly does. There is published work isolating exactly this as a major part of the gap.
  • Data scale. 50k rows is a big tabular dataset and a small deep-learning one. Boosting's stronger prior is worth more than a network's flexibility in that regime.
  • Operational cost. Not an accuracy argument but a real one: boosting trains in minutes on CPU, tunes reliably, and needs no accelerator to serve.

Where the answer flips: when the tabular data must be fused with text or images; when there is a very high-cardinality categorical whose embedding is genuinely useful downstream; when you want one model to serve many related tasks; or when you need a differentiable end-to-end system. Also worth noting that the newer tabular transformer work has narrowed the gap on some benchmarks without clearly overturning the result — I would present it as the current state of an active question rather than as settled.

MIDYour XGBoost feature importance says transaction_id is the most important feature. What do you conclude?
Two things at once: gain importance is biased toward high-cardinality features, and an ID should never have been in the feature set in the first place. Both are bugs.

The bias. A continuous or high-cardinality feature offers thousands of candidate split points. With that many chances, some split will separate the training data well by luck alone. Default gain importance rewards that, so IDs, timestamps and long floats systematically float to the top even when they carry nothing.

The leak. Worse, IDs are frequently not random. If they are sequential, the ID encodes time, and the model has learned "recent rows are more likely to be fraud" — which is temporal leakage from module 20 arriving through the back door, and it evaporates the moment you deploy.

What I would do: drop the ID and any other identifier or timestamp-derived surrogate; recompute importance by permutation on the validation set, which does not suffer the cardinality bias; look at SHAP for the direction of the effect, not just the magnitude; and check whether validation performance actually falls once the ID is gone. If it collapses, the model was largely reading the leak.

Trap

Permutation importance has its own failure mode: correlated features cover for each other, so shuffling one alone under-states both. Permute correlated groups together when it matters.

SENIORWhat are monotonic constraints and why would a fraud team use them?
They force the model's output to move in only one direction as a feature increases — enforced at split-selection time. Fraud and credit teams use them to encode business rules structurally, so the model cannot contradict them on any input.

The mechanism: when evaluating a candidate split on a constrained feature, the library rejects any split whose child leaf values would violate the required direction, and propagates bounds down the tree. The result is a model that is provably non-decreasing in that feature everywhere, not merely on the training data.

Why it matters commercially:

  • Defensibility. "More prior chargebacks lowered this customer's risk score" is impossible to explain to a regulator or a customer. With a constraint it cannot happen.
  • Robustness under drift. Unconstrained trees learn local wiggles from sparse regions of the training data. Those wiggles are exactly what breaks first when the distribution moves. A constraint removes a whole class of them.
  • Gaming resistance. A non-monotonic score can sometimes be lowered by increasing a risk feature. An adversary who finds that region exploits it. A monotone model has no such region.
  • Cheap prior knowledge. When you genuinely know the direction, telling the model costs nothing and buys sample efficiency.

The trade-off: a small accuracy cost, usually a fraction of a point of AUC, and it is worth verifying rather than assuming. And it only applies where the direction is genuinely known — transaction amount is not monotone in fraud risk, since both very small card-testing transactions and very large ones are suspicious. Constraining that would actively hurt.

MIDHow do you tune a gradient boosting model — in what order?
Fix a low learning rate and let early stopping choose the number of trees, then tune tree complexity, then sampling, then regularisation. Learning rate and tree count are one coupled knob, not two.
  1. Set the learning rate low (0.05, or 0.01 if I can afford it) and set n_estimators very high with early stopping on a validation set. Now the tree count is chosen for me and I never tune it directly. This coupling is the single most useful thing to know here: halving the learning rate roughly doubles the trees needed for the same fit.
  2. Tree complexity. max_depth (typically 3–8) or num_leaves for LightGBM, plus min_child_weight. This is where most of the remaining gain is.
  3. Sampling. subsample and colsample_bytree around 0.6–0.9. Adds regularisation and speeds training.
  4. Explicit regularisation. reg_lambda, reg_alpha, gamma. Usually the smallest effect, worth a pass if overfitting persists.
  5. Re-lower the learning rate at the end if the compute budget allows, and let early stopping add the trees. Almost always a small free gain.

Method rather than grid: random or Bayesian search over the ranges above beats an exhaustive grid at equal budget, because most of these parameters matter far less than depth and learning rate and a grid spends equally on all of them. And the validation split must respect the structure from module 20 — for fraud that means time-based with a label-delay gap, or the early stopping is itself tuned on leaked information.

Trap

For imbalanced data, scale_pos_weight improves ranking but destroys calibration — the predicted probabilities are no longer probabilities. If your threshold comes from a cost matrix, prefer leaving it alone and setting the threshold instead, or recalibrate afterwards.
