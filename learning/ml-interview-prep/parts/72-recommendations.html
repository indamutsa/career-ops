Part G · Walkthrough · Module 72

Recommendation & ranking The classic ML system-design question, and the one with the most structure to recall: a funnel from millions of items to ten, with a different model at each stage because each stage has a different budget. The subtle content is all in the labels — they are biased by the system that produced them.

How to use this module

The funnel is the skeleton and it is worth memorising exactly. The parts candidates drop are position bias, the offline/online metric mismatch, and what the system does on day one for a new user.

The prompt, and the first four minutes

The prompt

"Design the recommendation system for our home feed."

What to askWhy it changes the design
What are we optimising — clicks, watch time, purchases, retention?The objective is the design. Clicks give you clickbait; long-term retention is what the business wants and is much harder to measure.
How many items, how many users?Millions of items forces two-stage retrieval; ten thousand does not.
How fresh must items be?News and social feeds are dominated by items minutes old, which breaks any design that relies on accumulated engagement.
What signals exist — explicit ratings or implicit behaviour?Almost always implicit, which means no true negatives.
Latency budget per request?Typically 100–300 ms for the whole funnel, which decides how heavy the ranker can be.
Business constraints — diversity, promoted items, creator fairness?These are hard constraints applied after ranking, and forgetting them makes the design naive.
Cold start: new users, new items, or both?Both, always, and they need different answers.
The assumptions I would design against
  • 10 M items, 50 M users, a few hundred requests per second at peak.
  • Implicit feedback only — impressions, clicks, dwell time, completion, saves.
  • p99 200 ms for the whole funnel.
  • Primary objective: long-session satisfaction, approximated by a weighted blend of engagement signals; retention as the north-star measured in A/B tests, not in training.
  • Freshness matters — a meaningful share of items are less than a day old.

Why a funnel at all. Scoring ten million items with a good model is impossible in 200 ms, and scoring a thousand with a cheap model is wasteful. Each stage trades recall for precision: candidate generation must be fast and high-recall, ranking must be accurate and can afford to be slow, re-ranking sees the whole list and can enforce properties no per-item score can express. Saying this principle before drawing the stages shows you understand why, not just what.

Candidate generation

Two-tower retrieval the workhorse

One encoder for the user (history, demographics, context), one for the item. Train them so the dot product is high for engaged pairs. Item vectors are indexed offline in an ANN index; at request time you embed the user once and take the approximate nearest neighbours.

The two towers must be separate so item vectors can be precomputed — that constraint is the whole reason the architecture looks like this, and it is the same bi-encoder trade-off as RAG retrieval (module 71). No user–item interaction features are possible here; that is what ranking is for.

Negative sampling senior

There are no true negatives in implicit feedback, so you sample them: in-batch negatives (cheap, popularity-biased), random negatives (easy, teach little), and hard negatives (items the user saw and did not engage with).

Getting this wrong is the most common two-tower failure. In-batch negatives need a popularity correction — otherwise the model learns to rank popular items down, because they appear as negatives constantly. A mix of random and hard negatives is the practical answer.

Co-visitation underrated

"Users who engaged with X also engaged with Y", computed from logs as an item→item table.

No model, no training, very strong for sessions with clear intent, and it covers items the embedding model has not learned yet. Always propose several candidate sources — a system with one source is fragile, and the union is nearly free because they run in parallel.

Cold start always asked

New item: no engagement history, so the item tower must accept content features — text, category, creator, embeddings of the content itself — and you deliberately allocate a small exploration budget to give it impressions.

New user: fall back to popularity within their inferrable segment, ask for a few explicit interests if the product allows, and update the user representation within the session from the first few interactions.

Ranking — and the label problem

The ranker sees a few hundred candidates and can afford real features: user history, item statistics, user × item interaction features (has this user engaged with this creator, this category, this language before), and context — time of day, device, session position, what they just watched.

Multi-task, not single-objective. One head per signal — click, long dwell, save, share, hide, report — because they disagree and the disagreement is the point. Clickbait scores high on click and high on hide; a single click objective cannot see that. The heads share a trunk, and the final score is a weighted blend whose weights are a product decision tuned by A/B test, not learned. Being explicit that the blend is a policy lever, not a model parameter, is a senior distinction.

Position bias — the thing you must bring up unprompted

An item at slot 1 gets far more clicks than the same item at slot 10. Train naively on logged clicks and the model learns "items that were ranked highly get clicked", which is a description of your old ranker, not of user preference. The system then reinforces its own past decisions.

Three standard treatments: include position as a feature at training time and set it to a constant at inference (the model absorbs the bias into that feature); inverse-propensity weighting, where each example counts for 1 ÷ the probability that the user actually looked at that slot — so a click down at slot 10, which was unlikely to be seen at all, is far stronger evidence of preference than a click at slot 1; or a small randomised slice of traffic with shuffled slots, which is what lets you measure those look-probabilities honestly in the first place.

Note the structural similarity to fraud: you only observe outcomes for what the system chose to show. Same disease as module 70's blocked-decision blind spot, same class of cure — deliberate randomisation you pay for on purpose.

Re-ranking — properties a per-item score cannot express

  • Diversity. Ten items on the same topic score well individually and make a bad list. Maximal Marginal Relevance — choose the next item by its score minus its similarity to what you have already placed — trades a little relevance for coverage; the weight is tuned against session-level metrics, because per-item metrics will always say diversity hurts.
  • Freshness and exploration. A slot or two reserved for items with little history — this is the exploration budget that keeps the catalogue from ossifying, and it is also how new items ever get their first impressions.
  • Fatigue and caps. At most N items per creator or topic; suppress what was shown recently and ignored.
  • Business rules. Promoted slots, editorial pins, compliance suppressions. Keep these declarative and outside the model, so they can be changed without a retrain and audited.

Evaluation — and why offline wins so often vanish

LayerMetricWhat it cannot tell you
RetrievalRecall@k of engaged itemsWhether the ranker can use them
RankingAUC, NDCG (a ranking score that gives more credit for a correct item placed near the top than for one buried further down), log loss on logged impressionsAnything about items you never showed
ListSimulated diversity, coverage, gini over creators (one number for how unevenly impressions are shared out: 0 is perfectly even, 1 is winner-takes-all)Whether users like it
OnlineA/B test: engagement, session length, retention, complaintsLong-horizon effects inside a two-week test

Offline metrics are computed on logged impressions, which your current system chose. A new model that would surface something better gets no credit, because that item was never shown and has no label. This is why offline gains routinely fail to reproduce online, and why the A/B test is the decision — offline metrics are a filter to decide what is worth testing, not evidence of value.

The A/B design questions to raise: the randomisation unit (user, not request, or the experience is incoherent), the minimum detectable effect and therefore the duration, novelty effects that inflate week one, network interference when users influence each other, and guardrail metrics — a watch-time win alongside a rise in "hide" or a fall in next-week return is not a win.

The judgment question that often closes this round

"Your engagement metric goes up 8 % and complaints go up 3 %. Do you ship?"

The answer is not a number. Engagement is a proxy for a thing you actually want — people finding the product valuable over months — and a proxy that rises while satisfaction falls is the classic optimisation failure. I would look at which signals moved (dwell up but saves down is very different from both up), check whether the gain is concentrated in a segment, look at next-week return rather than in-test engagement, and run it longer if the retention read is not yet significant. Then I would present the trade honestly and let the product own the call — while pointing out that the objective blend is the lever, and that if we do not want this behaviour we should change the weights rather than hope the model chooses not to exploit them.

If you only get five minutes

"A funnel, because the budget per item shrinks as the candidate set grows. Candidate generation from several parallel sources — a two-tower model over an ANN index, co-visitation from logs, trending, follows — gives about a thousand items with high recall. Hard filters remove seen and ineligible items. A multi-task ranker scores a few hundred with real user × item interaction features and separate heads for click, dwell, save and hide, blended with weights that are a product decision. Re-ranking enforces what per-item scores cannot — diversity, creator caps, an exploration slot. The two things that decide whether it works are position bias, because logged clicks describe the old ranker rather than user preference, and the fact that offline metrics only cover items we already chose to show — so the A/B test is the decision and offline is only a filter."
