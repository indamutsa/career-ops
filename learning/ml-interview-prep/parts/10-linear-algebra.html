The problem this was invented to solve

Suppose you want to describe a house. You write down: 120 (square metres), 3 (bedrooms), 1985 (year built), 4.2 (kilometres from the centre). You now have [120, 3, 1985, 4.2]. That list is the recorded house, as far as any model is concerned.

The price rule cares about age rather than the calendar number 1985, so it performs one visible conversion: age = 2026 − year built = 2026 − 1985 = 41. From section 10.2 onward, the model-ready house is therefore [120, 3, 41, 4.2]. Nothing was replaced by an unexplained number: 41 came directly from the 1985 you wrote down.

So the two questions linear algebra answers are: how do I compare two lists? and how do I transform a list into a different list? That is the whole subject. Compare = dot product. Transform = matrix multiply.

One object, four names

WhatA scalar is one number; a vector is one house; a matrix is a table of houses; a tensor adds more tables.
WhyThe shape tells code how the numbers are organised and which operations are valid.
Where in MLFeatures, datasets, image batches, embeddings and model weights are these same nested arrays.
?Why scalar, vector, matrix and tensor are one idea

Scalar, vector, matrix, tensor. Four words that sound like four subjects and are in fact one set of house measurements packed inside progressively larger containers.

The nesting boxes

Imagine a housing survey packed for storage:

  1. One measurement card can hold the number 120. That is a scalar.
  2. One house folder holds four cards: area, bedrooms, age and distance. That is a vector.
  3. One city box holds five house folders. That is a matrix.
  4. One survey crate holds three city boxes. That is a tensor.

Step through the tabs. The cards never change. We only place them inside a house folder, place house folders inside a city box, then place city boxes inside the survey crate.

?Shape, axes and indexing — open the nesting boxes

Shape is the inventory label written on the outside. [3, 5, 4] says: three city boxes, five house folders in each city box, four measurement cards in each house folder. Open the Madrid box and put only its contents on the desk. You now hold five house folders with four cards each, so the label becomes [5, 4]. Madrid was not destroyed; its outer box simply falls outside your current view.

Now attach the technical words

Data scientists call each nesting layer an axis. Choosing one box from a layer is called indexing. After you step inside that box, its outer layer falls away from your view, so the shape loses one number.

Interview takeawayState the shape and name what every axis counts; never describe a tensor by dimensions alone.

Pricing a house — the dot product

WhatMultiply matching vector entries, then add them to produce one number.
WhyIt combines a house’s measurements with their learned importance to make one prediction.
Where in MLLinear regression, neural-network neurons, attention scores and vector similarity.
?What the price weights mean

The market has a price list: so much per square metre, so much per bedroom, so much off for each year of age and each kilometre from the centre. That price list is itself four numbers, in the same order as the house. Call it w:

w = [ 3.0 k€ per m² , 8 k€ per bedroom , −0.4 k€ per year of age , −12 k€ per km ]

Two lists of four. Multiply them slot by slot and add up the four results, and out comes one number — the price. That operation is the dot product, and it is the reason the house had to become a vector in the first place.

x = 1203414.2 w = 38−0.4−12
xᵀw = 120(3) + 3(8) + 41(−0.4) + 4.2(−12)
= 360 + 24 − 16.4 − 50.4 = 317.2 k€
Each position keeps its meaning: area × area-price, bedrooms × bedroom-price, age × age-price, distance × distance-price.
?How one dot product becomes a matrix multiplication

The dot product gives this page its first complete journey: record four facts about a house, place four prices beside those facts, multiply matching positions, then add. The four-number house has become one predicted price. Section 10.6 will repeat exactly this calculation for every house in the table at once.

Interview takeawayThe dot product is weighted agreement: matching positions multiply, then collapse to one scalar.

How different are two houses — the norm

house vectors → subtract them → difference vector → measure its length with a norm
WhatA norm turns a vector into one non-negative length. L1 adds absolute differences; L2 uses Pythagoras.
WhyOne distance lets us compare, rank or constrain objects described by many features.
Where in MLNearest neighbours, clustering, regularisation, gradient clipping and error measurement.
?From two houses to one distance

Your house and house D are not the same house. How much not-the-same? Subtract them feature by feature and you get a difference vector — and then you need to turn those four differences into one number you can compare. A norm is any honest way of doing that, and there are three worth knowing.

?Why raw feature scales can make this distance wrong
Trap

The first answer this figure gives you is wrong, and it is wrong in the way real pipelines are wrong. Area is measured in a unit that runs to hundreds; bedrooms in a unit that runs to five. Add their squares and area decides everything, so “distance between houses” has silently become “difference in floor area”. Nothing warns you. This is why feature scaling is a step in every serious pipeline, and why k-nearest-neighbours on raw columns is a trap.

Interview takeawayL1 measures city-block distance; L2 measures straight-line distance. Scale features before either when their units differ.

The same kind of house — cosine

WhatCosine divides the dot product by both vector lengths, leaving only their angle.
WhyIt compares proportions or direction while ignoring overall magnitude.
Where in MLEmbedding retrieval and recommendations when direction carries meaning and length should not.
?Why dividing by both lengths removes size

Now a different question. Not “how far apart are these two houses?” but “are they the same kind of house?” — because a 60 m² two-bed and a 120 m² four-bed are far apart in size and identical in character.

Doubling every measurement makes the arrow twice as long and leaves it pointing in exactly the same direction. So if you want character and not size, throw the length away: divide the dot product by both lengths, and what is left is the cosine of the angle between them.

?Cosine versus Euclidean distance
Trap

“Cosine and Euclidean distance rank things the same way.” Only when every vector has already been normalised to unit length — then they are monotonically related and the ranking is identical. Before normalising, they answer different house questions: Euclidean distance measures absolute difference, while cosine ignores overall size and compares the proportions between features.

Interview takeawayUse cosine for direction and Euclidean distance for absolute separation; normalised vectors make their rankings equivalent.

Every house at once — matrix multiplication

WhatEach row of the first matrix takes a dot product with each column of the second.
WhyIt applies one transformation to many examples in one structured operation.
Where in MLDataset scoring, linear layers, batches, attention and nearly every neural-network operation.
?How stacking dot products creates matrix multiplication

You have priced one house by hand. Now price all five without repeating yourself. Stack the houses as the rows of a table and the whole calculation collapses to a single symbol: p = X w. Nothing about the arithmetic changed — each price in the result is the same multiply-then-add you did in 10.3, run once per row.

X w = p    [5, 4] × [4] → [5]
X = 1203414.2602516 w = 38−0.4−12
Xw = 120(3)+3(8)+41(−0.4)+4.2(−12)60(3)+2(8)+51(−0.4)+6(−12)
= 317.2103.6 k€
A 2×4 house table times four feature weights produces two prices. The shared 4 is consumed by each dot product.
?Transpose and the matrix shape rule

The button marked transpose the table flips it on its side. That looks like pointless bookkeeping until the next section, where fitting the model needs XᵀX — a square matrix with one row and one column per feature, no matter how many houses you own.

The only shape rule you need

(m×n)(n×p) → (m×p). The inner two numbers must match, and they vanish. Five houses and four features against four weights gives five prices. Read a shape error in a stack trace this way and it stops being mysterious.

Interview takeawayFor (m×n)(n×p), the inner dimensions must match and the output keeps the outer dimensions: m×p.

Fitting the price rule — projection, least squares and inverse

WhatLeast squares chooses the weights whose predictions are closest to the observed prices.
WhyReal datasets usually have more house rows than unknown weights, so no exact inverse solves every noisy equation.
Where in MLLinear regression, calibration and any best-fit system with more equations than unknowns.
ŵ = arg minw ‖Xw − p‖²₂
XᵀXŵ = Xᵀp
ŵ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀp
Xw is the reachable price vector closest to the observed prices p: geometrically, it is the projection of p onto the columns of X. In the clean square example below, this simplifies to w = X⁻¹p.
?What “running the equation backwards” means here

Where did w = [3, 8, −0.4, −12] come from? Not from an authority. Four houses were sold, so you know the table X and you know the four prices p; the price list is the unknown. Four equations, four unknowns.

?When an inverse exists — and the warning before it fails

The symbols now have concrete jobs. X is the house table, p is the list of known sale prices, and w is the price list we want to recover. The inverse solves this clean case because four independent house rows give four equations for four unknown prices. The next section shows exactly what breaks when the house table repeats information.

⚠ The inverse was already warning you

Look at the bedrooms row of X⁻¹: entries near ±17, ten times anything else. Big houses have more bedrooms, so those two columns nearly repeat one another and the inverse has to take a small difference between large numbers to tell them apart. That is ill-conditioning: the answer still exists, but a rounding error in the data moves it a long way. The next section is what happens when “nearly repeat” becomes “exactly repeat”.

Interview takeawayDescribe least squares as a projection. In practice, use a stable solver such as QR decomposition—which solves the system without forming an inverse—or SVD.

When the undo fails — rank and the determinant

WhatRank counts independent directions. A square matrix’s determinant is zero exactly when one direction has collapsed.
WhyDuplicate features make several weight vectors produce the same predictions, so the fitted answer is not unique.
Where in MLMulticollinearity, unstable regression, redundant features, PCA and low-rank compression.
?How the duplicate area column enters the house table

Someone adds a column to your table: the area again, this time in square feet. It is honest data, correctly measured, and it destroys the model. Every entry in the new column is 10.7639 times the entry beside it, so it carries no information the table did not already have.

D = 1201291.66860645.834 rows: houses A and B; columns: area in m² and the same area in ft²
det(D) = (120)(645.834) − (60)(1291.668)
= 77,500.08 − 77,500.08 = 0
The second column is 10.7639 times the first, so the two columns trace only one direction. A zero determinant means this square slice cannot be inverted.
?Connect rank, determinant, inverse and the moving weights

Drag that slider and watch the prices refuse to move. The market pays 3 k€ per square metre, and the fit cannot tell you whether that is “3 per m² and 0 per ft²”, “0 per m² and 0.2787 per ft²”, or any blend of the two — all of them are exactly right. The answer is no longer unique, and three pieces of vocabulary all describe that same failure:

Find the direction the houses actually vary in — eigenvectors

WhatAn eigenvector keeps its direction after a matrix transformation; its eigenvalue is the stretch factor.
WhyIt reveals the natural directions already present in correlated data.
Where in MLPCA, feature-covariance analysis and graph clustering built from eigenvectors.
?Where the house correlation matrix comes from

Area and bedrooms tell almost the same story: in these four records, bigger houses nearly always have more bedrooms. We first put them on comparable rulers. Area has mean 117.5 m² and sample spread 60.21 m²; bedrooms has mean 3.25 and sample spread 1.26. House A therefore lands at (120−117.5)/60.21 = 0.04 on the area ruler and (3−3.25)/1.26 = −0.20 on the bedroom ruler. The other houses are converted the same way.

Now compute how the two standardised columns move together. That produces the correlation matrix C. Its diagonal is 1 because each feature agrees perfectly with itself; its two off-diagonal entries are 0.98 because area and bedrooms rise together.

C = 1.000.980.981.00 v = 0.7070.707
Cv = 1(0.707)+0.98(0.707)0.98(0.707)+1(0.707)
= 1.4001.400 = 1.980.7070.707 = 1.98v
The output is longer but still points along “area and bedrooms rise together.” Therefore v is an eigenvector and 1.98 is its eigenvalue.
?Interpret the two directions and connect them to PCA

The diagonal direction v = [0.71, 0.71] means “area and bedrooms rise together” — the housing notion of bigness. Multiplying gives Cv = 1.98v: the arrow remains on the same line and becomes 1.98 times as long. A direction that survives a matrix without turning is an eigenvector; the length multiplier is its eigenvalue.

The second eigenvalue is 0.02, so almost none of the spread lies in the direction “more area but fewer bedrooms”. The two directions meet at 90°, which is what orthogonal means, and their dot product is zero. Keeping only bigness preserves 1.97897/(1.97897+0.02103) = 98.948% ≈ 98.95% of the two-feature spread. Those extra digits are shown here so the division matches what you can calculate. That final tab is PCA: replace area and bedrooms with each house’s one coordinate along the useful direction.

Say it in one line

“An eigenvector is a direction a matrix does not turn; its eigenvalue is the factor by which the matrix stretches that direction. For these houses, the useful direction is bigness.”

?Derive both eigenvalues with the characteristic equation

An eigenvector is non-zero and satisfies Cv = λv. Rearranging gives (C−λI)v = 0. A non-zero solution exists only when that matrix has determinant zero:

det(C−λI) = det1−λ0.980.981−λ
= (1−λ)² − (0.98)² = 0
1−λ = ±0.98  ⇒  λ₁ = 1.98, λ₂ = 0.02
Interview takeawayEigenvectors are invariant directions; PCA keeps the largest-eigenvalue directions from the table that records how feature pairs vary together.

Do that for the whole house table — SVD

WhatSVD factors any matrix into house scores U, direction strengths Σ and feature directions Vᵀ.
WhyIt sorts the table’s information from strongest to weakest so weak directions can be dropped safely.
Where in MLDimensionality reduction, recommendation systems, compression, denoising and stable least squares.
Xhouses×features = Uhouses×directions Σdirection strengths Vᵀdirections×features
X4×4 = U4×4 Σ4×4 Vᵀ4×4
Read right to left: Vᵀ mixes the four features into directions, Σ scales those directions, and U assigns the resulting amounts to the four houses.
?Why the house table must be centred and scaled first

The eigen example used two columns so you could see the cloud. The real table has four: area, bedrooms, age and distance. Standardise each one using the mean and spread calculated from houses A–D, then subtract its mean. Call the resulting centred table X. Singular value decomposition rewrites that same table as three smaller jobs:

X = U Σ Vᵀ
?What U, Σ and Vᵀ mean, and why truncation works

Read the equation from right to left. Each row of Vᵀ is a recipe over the four familiar feature slots. The superscript T means transpose: the recipes would be columns in V; transposing lays them out as rows so a house can take a dot product with each one. Nothing in Vᵀ is an unrelated or arbitrary quantity. A direction can be written with either sign, so this figure consistently points each recipe toward higher predicted house prices; the direction itself still comes from the table.

Σ puts one non-negative strength beside each recipe, largest first. U then records how much of each strengthened recipe belongs to house A, B, C and D. Multiplying all three restores the centred table exactly. Keeping only the first k recipes gives the closest rank-k table under squared error; the rebuild tab lets you watch what each extra recipe repairs.

The letters, tied to the house

Vᵀ: what feature mixtures exist. Σ: how much each mixture matters. U: how much of each mixture each house contains. SVD is a sorted rewrite of the table, which is why dropping weak directions compresses it.

Interview takeawaySVD works on any matrix; keeping the top k singular values gives the closest rank-k reconstruction under squared error.

Why L1 deletes a useless feature and L2 only shrinks it

vector → measure its size with a norm → add that size to the training loss → discourage large coefficients → regularisation
WhatRegularisation makes the model pay for large coefficients as well as prediction error.
WhyA flexible model can give noise a useful-looking weight and overfit the few houses it has seen.
ChooseLasso for feature selection, Ridge for stable small weights, Elastic Net—L1 and L2 together—when you need both.
Lasso L1

What: linear regression plus αΣ|wᵢ|.

Effect: some coefficients become exactly zero, removing those features.

Use when: you have many features, expect some are irrelevant and want many exact zeros in an easier-to-explain model.

Ridge L2

What: linear regression plus ½αΣwᵢ².

Effect: every coefficient shrinks smoothly; correlated features remain represented.

Use when: most features may help a little or correlated columns make the fit unstable.

?Coefficient, penalty, L1 and L2 — the prerequisite words

Coefficient: one learned weight saying how strongly a feature changes the prediction. In the house rule, 3 is the area coefficient.

Penalty: an extra cost added to prediction error. A coefficient must improve the fit enough to justify that cost.

L1: add absolute coefficient sizes, ‖w‖₁ = Σ|wᵢ|. Its pull toward zero stays constant.

L2: use squared coefficient sizes, ‖w‖²₂ = Σwᵢ². Its pull weakens as a coefficient becomes smaller.

r = 5−66−3 j = 102156
j·r = 10(5)+2(−6)+15(6)+6(−3) = 50−12+90−18 = 110
j·j = 10²+2²+15²+6² = 100+4+225+36 = 365
a = 110/365 = 0.3014
α=0.30:   wL1 = max(0.3014−0.30, 0) = 0.0014  ·  wL2 = 0.3014/(1+0.30) = 0.2318
α≥0.3014:   wL1 = 0  ·  wL2 remains above 0 for finite α
The day-of-month feature accidentally fits noise. Lasso can delete it; Ridge keeps it but limits its influence.
?Where the unregularised weight 0.3014 comes from

The four-feature price rule predicts 317.2, 103.6, 603.6, 221.6 k€ for houses A–D. Real sales are measurements rather than formula outputs, so suppose the recorded sale prices are 322.2, 97.6, 609.6, 218.6 k€. Subtracting the model prediction from the recorded sale gives the visible residual vector r = [5, −6, 6, −3] k€.

Someone now adds “day of the month listed”: j = [10, 2, 15, 6]. It is a junk feature, but with only four houses it happens to line up with the residuals. Least squares assigns it the coefficient

a = (j · r)/(j · j) = 110/365 = 0.3014 k€ per day

That is where the 0.3014 in the loss comes from. Divide the one-feature squared error by j·j and ignore the constant part that no choice of w can change; the data term becomes ½(w−0.3014)². It says “the four observations pull this weight toward 0.3014”.

?Why L1 reaches zero, why L2 does not, and what gradient clipping changes

L1 adds α|w|. Its slope keeps the same strength all the way to zero, so the solution is max(0.3014−α, 0) and becomes exactly zero at α = 0.3014. L2 adds ½αw². Its pull fades as w shrinks, giving 0.3014/(1+α): smaller at every step, but never exactly zero for a finite α.

This is the distinction behind the names used later. Lasso is the fitted model with an L1 penalty, so it can remove columns. Ridge, also called L2 regularisation or weight decay in its common optimiser form, continuously shrinks weights. Gradient clipping uses an L2 norm for a different purpose: when the whole gradient vector is longer than a chosen limit, it rescales that vector without changing its direction.

Say it in one line

“The data gave the junk feature weight 0.3014. L1 has a corner at zero and can overpower that pull completely; L2 becomes weaker near zero and therefore only squeezes.”

Interview takeawayLasso produces sparsity through L1’s corner at zero; Ridge stabilises correlated features through smooth L2 shrinkage.

Questions

BASICExplain the dot product to someone with no maths background.
It multiplies matching slots in two vectors and adds them, turning one house and one price list into one predicted price.

For house A, the matching slots are area, bedrooms, age and distance: 120×3 + 3×8 + 41×(−0.4) + 4.2×(−12) = 317.2. That multiply-then-add pattern is the whole operation.

Stack several house vectors into a table and the same operation prices every house. That is matrix multiplication in section 10.6.

Trap

A dot product is not automatically a similarity score. A large house vector can produce a large dot product just because its measurements are large. Cosine divides out both vector lengths when direction is the quantity you want.

BASICWhat do the shapes have to satisfy to multiply two matrices, and why?
(m×n)(n×p) → (m×p): the inner dimensions must match, and they disappear.

The house table has shape [5,4]: five houses, four features per house. The price vector has shape [4]: one weight per feature. The two 4s match, so each row can take one four-slot dot product and produce one price. The result has shape [5].

Trap

Order matters. Xw is defined here; wX is not, because its adjacent dimensions do not match. Matrix multiplication is not commutative.

MIDWhen would you compare the houses with cosine rather than Euclidean distance?
Use Euclidean distance for absolute difference and cosine for the direction, or proportions, of a feature vector.

A 60 m² two-bedroom house and a 120 m² four-bedroom house differ greatly in absolute size, so Euclidean distance is large. Their area-to-bedroom proportion is identical, so the two simplified vectors point in the same direction and cosine is 1.

Once vectors are L2-normalised, cosine and dot product are the same number, while Euclidean distance gives the same ranking in reverse. This is why a retrieval system can normalise once and use fast dot products.

Trap

Cosine deliberately discards magnitude. If absolute floor area matters to the decision, normalising it away destroys useful information.

MIDWhat is the rank of a matrix, and when has it ever mattered to you?
Rank is the number of genuinely independent directions in a matrix — its real information content, as opposed to its shape.

The original house table has four feature columns and four independent directions. Adding square feet appears to create a fifth feature, but it is exactly 10.7639 × area in m², so rank remains 4. The extra column changes the shape and adds no new information.

That matters because the price rule stops being unique. Ridge restores a chosen stable answer by adding λI. PCA and SVD use the constructive side of the same idea on this house table: identify directions that add almost no information and omit them.

Trap

Near-duplicate columns can be worse to diagnose than exact duplicates. An inverse may still exist, but tiny changes in sale data can make its recovered weights swing wildly. That is ill-conditioning.

MIDExplain eigenvectors to a product manager.
They are the directions a transformation leaves pointing the same way — it can stretch them, but it cannot turn them.

In the house records, area and bedrooms rise together. The correlation matrix maps the direction [0.71,0.71] back onto the same line, multiplied by 1.98. That direction means “bigness”; it is an eigenvector and 1.98 is its eigenvalue.

The perpendicular direction has eigenvalue 0.02, so the data barely varies there. PCA keeps bigness and drops the weak direction, preserving 98.9% of the observed spread with one number per house instead of two.

Trap

An eigenvector is a direction, so multiplying it by any nonzero scalar gives the same eigenvector direction. The sign and length used to display it are conventions; the line and its eigenvalue carry the meaning.

SENIORHow are PCA and SVD related?
PCA is SVD applied to mean-centred data — the principal components are the right singular vectors, and the variance explained is the squared singular values.

For the standardised house table, the PCA route forms C = XᵀX/(n−1) and finds C’s eigenvectors. The SVD route works directly on X = UΣVᵀ. The rows of Vᵀ are the same principal feature directions, and σᵢ²/(n−1) is the variance along direction i.

SVD is usually the safer computation because forming XᵀX squares the condition number: a weak house direction becomes even harder to distinguish from rounding noise. Truncating the SVD at k recipes also gives the closest possible rank-k reconstruction under squared entry error.

Trap

PCA requires centring and is scale-sensitive. If area remains in square metres while bedrooms stays between 1 and 8, area dominates because of its unit. Also, maximum variance is not the same as maximum usefulness for predicting sale price.

SENIORWhy does the L1 penalty produce exact zeros when L2 does not?
L1 keeps a finite pull toward zero all the way in; L2’s pull fades with the weight, so it approaches zero without reaching it.

The four house residuals give listing day an unpenalised weight a = 110/365 = 0.3014. With L1, the answer is max(0.3014−α,0), so α = 0.3014 deletes the false feature. With L2, the answer is 0.3014/(1+α), which remains positive for every finite α.

Geometrically, an L1 constraint has corners on the axes, where a coefficient is exactly zero. An L2 constraint is round, so a loss contour usually touches it away from an axis.

Trap

Lasso can choose arbitrarily between correlated columns. If m² and ft² are both present, it may keep either one depending on tiny changes in the rows. Elastic net combines L1 and L2 when stable groups matter.
