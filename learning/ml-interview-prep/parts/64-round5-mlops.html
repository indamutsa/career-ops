Part F · Interview rounds · Module 64

Round 5 · MLOps & production The round that separates people who have trained models from people who have kept them alive. Almost every question here has the same shape: something worked offline and then did not work in production — why, and how would you have caught it?

How to run this round

60 minutes. The rapid-fire section is vocabulary you must not fumble — a hesitation on "what is backpressure" undoes a good architecture answer. The incident section is the heart of it: answer each one as a diagnosis, out loud, in the order you would actually check things. Saying "first I would look at X, because it distinguishes A from B" scores far better than naming the right cause immediately by luck.

Rapid fire — 20 seconds each

BASICWhat is training/serving skew?
The features the model sees in production differ from the ones it was trained on — different code path, different data freshness, or a different definition of the same field. It is the single most common reason a good offline model underperforms live.
MIDWhat is point-in-time correctness?
Every feature value in a training row must be one that was actually knowable at the moment of the event — an as-of join on the event timestamp, not the latest value. The naive join leaks the future and produces a model that looks brilliant and fails on day one.
BASICKafka — queue or log?
A log. Messages are appended and retained for a configured window; consumers track their own offset rather than removing messages. That is why you can replay history and why several independent consumers can read the same stream.
MIDWhat determines ordering in Kafka?
Ordering is guaranteed per partition only. The key determines the partition, so keying by account id gives you per-account ordering — which is exactly what velocity features need — while different accounts stay parallel.
BASICWhat is backpressure?
The signal that a downstream stage cannot keep up, propagated upstream so producers slow down instead of the system falling over. Without it, an overloaded stage fills memory and dies, which is worse than being slow.
MIDFour ways to respond to backpressure?
Buffer with a bounded queue, block the producer, shed load (drop or degrade), or scale out. The word "bounded" is the important one — an unbounded buffer is not a strategy, it is a delayed crash.
MIDAt-least-once, at-most-once, exactly-once?
At-least-once may duplicate, at-most-once may lose, exactly-once needs coordination and is expensive. Standard practice is at-least-once delivery plus idempotent consumers, which gives you exactly-once effects at a fraction of the cost.
MIDEvent time versus processing time?
Event time is when the thing happened, processing time when your code saw it. They diverge under lag and retries. Windowing on processing time silently changes results when you replay, which is why watermarks exist.
MIDWhat does a feature store actually give you?
One definition of a feature, computed once, served two ways — an offline store for point-in-time training joins and a low-latency online store for serving. The value is the shared definition; the two stores are just how it is delivered.
MIDWhat is materialisation lag?
The delay between an event landing and its aggregate appearing in the online store. Train on values with zero lag and serve values that are ten minutes stale and you have built skew into the design — so measure the lag and reproduce it in training.
BASICMLflow, DVC, Airflow, Kubeflow — who does what?
MLflow tracks experiments and registers models, DVC versions data and pipelines, Airflow schedules general DAGs, Kubeflow runs ML pipelines on Kubernetes. They overlap at the edges; the mistake is expecting one to cover all four jobs.
MIDWhat makes a training run reproducible?
Four things pinned together: the code version, the data version, the environment, and the seeds/config. Miss any one and "we cannot reproduce the model that is currently making decisions" becomes a real sentence in a real incident.
MIDShadow, canary, blue-green, A/B — one line each.
Shadow: new model scores live traffic, its output is discarded. Canary: a small real share, increased in stages. Blue-green: two full environments, flip and flip back. A/B: a controlled experiment measuring a business metric.
SENIORShadow or canary — which do you reach for first?
Shadow first, because it has zero blast radius and it validates the plumbing — latency, feature availability, score distribution. Then canary, because it is the only one that tests the real feedback loop of the decision being acted on.
MIDData drift versus concept drift?
Data drift: P(x) changed, the inputs look different. Concept drift: P(y|x) changed, the same inputs now mean something different. Data drift you can see immediately; concept drift you can only confirm once labels arrive.
MIDPSI thresholds?
Below 0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 moderate shift worth investigating, above 0.25 significant. And PSI is the symmetric KL divergence between the two binned distributions — the same quantity from the information-theory module wearing a credit-risk hat.
SENIORIf you could monitor exactly one thing, what?
The prediction distribution. It is available instantly with no labels, and it moves for almost every failure that matters — broken features, drifted inputs, a bad deploy, an upstream schema change. It does not tell you why, but it tells you to look.
MIDWhat is the delayed-label problem?
Ground truth arrives weeks after the prediction — chargebacks are the canonical case — so your recent performance metrics are computed on incomplete labels and look artificially good. Recent windows are censored, not clean.
SENIORWhat is the feedback loop problem?
You only observe outcomes for transactions you approved, so the model never learns whether the ones it blocked were actually fraud. Training on that data reinforces its existing boundary. The fix is a small randomised holdout that is approved regardless of score.
MIDWhy does an offline win often not land online?
Five usual suspects: point-in-time leakage, train/serve skew, a shifted live distribution, the wrong metric (offline AUC versus a business outcome at a threshold), or the decision changing behaviour. Name them as a checklist and work down it.

The incidents — diagnose out loud

Five minutes each. Say the order you would check things, and why.
SENIOROffline AUC 0.94, online precision collapses in week one. What happened?
Almost certainly leakage or skew. I would compare the served feature vectors against the training rows for the same entities first, because that one check separates the two causes immediately.

Step 1 — is the model even seeing the same numbers? Log the exact feature vector used for each live decision, pull the same entities out of the training set, and diff them feature by feature. If the values differ, it is skew and I am now looking for the cause: two implementations of the same feature, a different default for nulls, a unit or timezone mismatch, or the online store serving a staler aggregate than the offline pipeline used.

Step 2 — if the values match, suspect leakage. Rank features by importance and ask of each one: was this knowable at decision time? The classic offenders are counters that were incremented because of this event — a chargeback count, an investigation flag, a merchant risk score recomputed nightly after the fact. The tell is a single feature carrying implausible importance and an offline number that felt too good.

Step 3 — check the join, not just the features. Even correct features leak if the training join took the latest value rather than the as-of value. I would re-run training with a strict point-in-time join and see how much AUC falls. A large drop is the diagnosis.

Step 4 — only then consider distribution. Compare the live input distribution to the training reference with PSI per feature. Real shift is possible, but a drop this large in week one is much more likely to be an engineering defect than the world changing.

Step 5 — and check the metric itself. AUC is threshold-free; production precision is measured at one threshold on a heavily imbalanced stream. A model can have excellent ranking and poor precision at the operating point, especially if the live positive rate is lower than in the training sample. If everything else is clean, this is where I would land.

The prevention I would propose: serve and train from the same feature definitions, log the served vector and train on those logs rather than recomputing history, and add a pre-deploy check comparing offline and shadow score distributions. Shadow mode would have caught this before a single customer was affected.

SENIORConsumer lag has been climbing for two hours. Walk me through it.
Establish whether it is more input or slower processing, then whether the slowness is the model, a dependency, or partition skew. Meanwhile decide what to degrade, because the lag is already customer-visible.

First, the mitigation, not the diagnosis. Lag means decisions are late. If the decision path is synchronous, requests are timing out; if it is asynchronous, fraud is being scored after the money moved. I would confirm what the fallback does right now — a conservative rule-based decision is usually correct — and make sure it is active while I investigate.

Is input up or throughput down? Compare produce rate to consume rate. A traffic spike, a replay, or an upstream backfill dumping history all raise input; a deploy, a slow dependency, or GC pressure lowers throughput. The two have completely different fixes and the graphs separate them in seconds.

If throughput is down, find the slow stage. Latency per stage: feature lookup, model inference, downstream write. In my experience the online store or an enrichment call is the culprit more often than the model. A dependency degrading turns into consumer lag because the consumer blocks on it — which is backpressure arriving from a place people forget to instrument.

Check partition skew. If lag is concentrated in a few partitions, it is a hot key — one merchant or one account generating a disproportionate share. Adding consumers will not help, because a partition is consumed by one consumer; the fix is a better key or a salt on the hot entity. Uniform lag across partitions means genuine capacity shortfall, and there scaling out does work, up to the partition count.

Then apply the backpressure policy deliberately. Options in order of preference: scale consumers, shed low-value work (drop enrichments, skip the expensive features and use a reduced-feature model), degrade to the rule-based path, and only as a last resort drop events. The one thing I would not do is raise the buffer size — that converts a visible problem into an invisible one and then an out-of-memory crash.

Afterwards. Consumer lag should have an SLO and an alert well before two hours. And the incident should produce a load test that reproduces the spike, because "it was fine until it was not" usually means nobody knew the ceiling.

SENIORPSI on your top feature jumps to 0.4 overnight. Do you retrain?
No — not yet. An overnight jump is far more likely to be a pipeline defect than the world changing overnight, and retraining on broken data bakes the defect into the model.

Look at the shape of the shift first. This is the single most useful diagnostic. If one bin absorbed the mass — especially a zero or null bin — that is an upstream failure: a field stopped populating, a join started missing, a schema changed, a unit changed. If the shift is spread smoothly across bins, genuine population change is plausible.

Then check the boring explanations. Was there a deploy? Did an upstream team ship something? Is it a seasonal or calendar effect — a payday, a holiday, a promotion, a new market launching? Did a large merchant onboard? Any of these produce a real, correct shift that needs no model change at all.

Check whether the model actually cares. Input drift on a feature the model barely uses is noise. I would look at the prediction distribution: if scores are unchanged, the urgency drops sharply. If scores moved too, it is affecting decisions now.

Check performance where you can. Labels are delayed, so recent performance is censored. But proxies exist — the manual-review queue's confirmation rate, the alert-to-fraud ratio, complaint volume, the randomised holdout. These give a partial read days before the labels do.

Then decide. Pipeline bug → fix the pipeline, no retrain. Real shift with performance holding → watch, no retrain. Real shift with performance degrading → retrain on data that includes the new regime, and validate on the most recent window rather than a random split. Real shift that is adversarial → treat it as an attack, which may mean a rule now and a model next week.

The framing: drift alerts are a prompt to investigate, not a trigger to retrain. Auto-retraining on drift is how a bad day becomes a bad model.

SENIORA canary at 5 % looks fine, you go to 100 %, and everything degrades. Why?
Something about 5 % was not representative: the traffic was not randomly routed, the load was not proportional, the sample was too small for a rare event, or the decision itself changes the environment at scale.

Routing. If the 5 % was selected by anything correlated with behaviour — a region, a device type, a set of hosts, sticky sessions from a subset of users — it was never a random sample. Hashing the entity id is the fix, and it also gives a consistent experience per user.

Load. Some failures are strictly load-dependent: cache hit rates, connection pool limits, a downstream service that copes at 5 % and throttles at 100 %, memory that fits one model replica but not the fleet. The canary proved correctness, not capacity. A load test against the new model is a separate exercise.

Statistics. If fraud is 0.2 % of traffic, 5 % of traffic contains very few positives. The canary may simply not have had the power to detect a regression in recall. I would ask, before the rollout, how long each stage must run to detect the effect size I care about — and if the answer is a week, either wait or accept that the canary is only checking for gross breakage.

Feedback and interaction. At 5 % the model's decisions barely move the world. At 100 % they do: attackers notice, review queues saturate, approval rates shift, and downstream teams that tuned to the old score distribution start behaving differently. This is a real class of failure that no canary size can fully rule out.

Alerting. A related trap — if the alert is on the blended metric rather than per-variant, a bad model at 5 % is diluted twentyfold and invisible. Always alert per variant.

What I would change. Stages with a hold long enough for the metric to be detectable, per-variant alerting on both model and system metrics, a load test before the last stage, and an automatic rollback wired to a guard metric rather than to a human noticing.

Whiteboard prompt — 20 minutes

"Design the retraining system for a fraud model. Not the model — the system that decides when to retrain, retrains it, and decides whether to ship."

Cover, roughly in this order: the trigger (schedule versus drift versus performance, and why a pure drift trigger is dangerous); how you build the training set given delayed labels, including how far back the label horizon forces you to cut; point-in-time correctness in that join; how the randomised holdout keeps the feedback loop honest; the validation gate — a recent-window evaluation, not a random split, compared against the currently deployed model rather than against the old offline number; what is registered and with what lineage; the promotion path through shadow and canary with guard metrics; and what makes rollback a one-click operation.

Where candidates lose marks: retraining on a fixed weekly schedule with no gate; ignoring that the last N weeks of labels are censored; validating on a random split so recent data leaks into training; comparing the challenger to the champion's historical offline metric instead of running both on the same fresh window; and having no answer for "what if the new model is worse but only on one segment".

Checkpoint — say these out loud, no notes

  • The five reasons an offline win does not land online, in order of likelihood.
  • The four responses to backpressure, and why raising the buffer is not one of them.
  • What the shape of a PSI shift tells you that its magnitude does not.
  • Why a canary must alert per variant and not on the blended metric.
  • Why delayed labels make recent performance look better than it is.
  • The one metric you would keep if you could only keep one, and why.
